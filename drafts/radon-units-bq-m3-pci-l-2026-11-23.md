# Radon Units Explained: Bq/m³ to pCi/L and Back

You get your radon result and it says 143. Or a monitor you bought online shows 3.9. Both could describe the same basement, and neither number means much until you know the unit attached to it.

Canada measures radon in becquerels per cubic metre (Bq/m³). A lot of consumer monitors, apps and American websites use picocuries per litre (pCi/L). The conversion is one multiplication, and once you have it the guideline and your own result line up. This article covers the units, the conversion, a table you can check against, and a few traps that catch people comparing the two.

## What Bq/m³ actually measures

A becquerel is one radioactive decay per second. Radon is a radioactive gas, so its concentration in air is described by how much decay is happening in a given volume of air. So a reading of 200 Bq/m³ describes 200 decays a second in each cubic metre of air.

The Government of Manitoba describes it as a measure of radioactivity: radon "is measured in becquerels per cubic meter (Bq/m3)." You don't need to picture atoms. You need to know that a bigger number means more radon in the air, and that Canada's guideline is written in this unit.

Health Canada's guideline is **200 Bq/m³**. It applies to the average annual radon level in the normal occupancy area of a building, which Health Canada defines as any part of the building where a person is likely to spend more than 4 hours per day. A finished basement or a basement apartment counts. A crawl space, an unfinished basement, a cold room or a furnace room doesn't.

## The conversion, in one line

Health Canada's guide for radon measurements states it directly: "37 Bq/m3 is equal to 1 pCi/L."

That gives you two rules:

- **Bq/m³ to pCi/L:** divide by 37.
- **pCi/L to Bq/m³:** multiply by 37.

Apply that to the guideline and you get 200 ÷ 37 = 5.4 pCi/L (5.405 if you want more decimals). So when an app tells you the guideline is "about 5.4," it means the same line Health Canada draws at 200.

Here is a table of values to check yourself against. It is arithmetic only, using Health Canada's 37 to 1 relationship, and it rounds to one decimal place.

| Bq/m³ | pCi/L | Note |
|---|---|---|
| 37 | 1.0 | The definition of 1 pCi/L |
| 74 | 2.0 | Twice that |
| 100 | 2.7 | World Health Organization's guideline figure |
| 200 | 5.4 | Canadian guideline |
| 400 | 10.8 | Twice the guideline |
| 800 | 21.6 | Four times the guideline; the Canadian guideline was 800 until 2007 |

The 100 Bq/m³ row is the World Health Organization's action guideline, which Lung Saskatchewan and CARST cite when they say 73% of recently tested Saskatchewan homes were above it. The 800 row is there for history. Health Canada changed its guideline to 200 Bq/m³ on 9 June 2007, from 800.

Going the other way, a few pCi/L values you might see on a screen: 1 pCi/L is 37 Bq/m³, 5 pCi/L is 185 Bq/m³, and 10 pCi/L is 370 Bq/m³.

## Why this matters: the 5 pCi/L trap

Look at that last line again. A monitor showing 5 pCi/L is showing 185 Bq/m³. That is under the Canadian guideline of 200, by a small margin. Someone who sees "5" and thinks it's far below a limit of 200 has mixed up the units. Someone who sees "5.4" and thinks it's a mild reading may have missed that 5.4 pCi/L is exactly the guideline.

The reverse mistake happens too. A lab report says 200 and an American app says 5.4, and the two look wildly different. They're the same level written in different units. The Canadian guideline is in Bq/m³, so that's the unit to convert to.

Three habits prevent most of the confusion:

1. **Check the unit before you read the number.** A bare "4" or "150" means nothing. Look for "Bq/m³" or "pCi/L" next to it.
2. **Convert to Bq/m³ before comparing to the guideline.** Multiply any pCi/L figure by 37, then compare it to 200.
3. **Compare like with like.** A long-term average and a one-day reading are different kinds of numbers, even in the same unit.

## Which unit will your result use?

If your test goes through a Canadian lab, expect Bq/m³. The kits sold through HomeRadonTest.ca, the provincial lung associations' partner, are analysed at the Saskatchewan Research Council lab and report in the unit Canadian guidance uses. A kit costs $70.00 before tax at checkout. Retail kits generally run between $50 and $100, according to the Manitoba government.

Digital monitors are less predictable. Some show Bq/m³, some show pCi/L, and some let you switch. If you're buying one, Health Canada says to choose a monitor that has passed C-NRPP performance testing for long-term radon measurements, and C-NRPP keeps a list of approved electronic radon monitors. A cheap monitor without that testing can give you a number with a unit and no reliability.

Library monitors work the same way. Winnipeg Public Library lends radon screening kits free from 1 November to 30 April, for three weeks, and each kit has an electronic monitor meant to sit in the lowest level of a home for at least seven days. Regina Public Library lends digital detectors to Regina residents for three weeks. Check which unit the display shows before you write anything down. ## A reading is not the same as a result

Getting the unit right is half of it. The other half is what kind of reading you're converting.

The guideline is about the average annual level, not a snapshot. Health Canada says "the 200 Bq/m3 guideline applies to your average annual radon level, not short-term measurements." The minimum test length is 91 days, and Health Canada wants those 91 days to fall in the heating season. A three-day reading, whatever its unit, is a snapshot, not the average the guideline is written around.

That's why Health Canada says short-term measurements are not appropriate for informing mitigation decisions. A short screening is useful for deciding whether to go further. A long-term result is what you act on. The post on [long-term vs short-term radon tests](/blog/long-term-vs-short-term-radon-test/) covers that distinction in more detail, and the one on [radon test kits vs digital monitors](/blog/radon-test-kit-vs-digital-monitor/) compares the two ways of getting a long-term number.

Placement affects the number too. Health Canada says to place the detector in the typical breathing zone, at least 30 cm above the floor and at least 30 cm from walls or ceilings, away from heat sources, direct sunlight, vents, doors, windows, sump holes and crawl spaces. If you use a monitor, treat its first few days with caution and watch the longer average it builds, not the number that flashes up on day one.

## What to do with your result

Once you have a long-term number in Bq/m³, the actions are simple and Health Canada states them plainly:

- **Above 200 Bq/m³:** Health Canada recommends corrective action within 1 year. The higher the concentration, the sooner action should be taken.
- **Between 0 and 200 Bq/m³:** C-NRPP says to retest within 5 years or after major renovations.
- **Anywhere under 200:** the health risk is small, but Health Canada also says no level is considered risk free. What you do with a result of, say, 120 or 160 is your decision.

Fixing a high result usually means a sub-slab depressurization system: a pipe through the slab and a fan that pulls radon from under the house. Health Canada says in most homes this reduces the level by more than 80%. After installation, the steps are a short-term test by a certified professional, a long-term test in the first heating season, and a retest every five years.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many Bq/m³ is 1 pCi/L?

37. Health Canada's guide for radon measurements says 37 Bq/m³ is equal to 1 pCi/L. To convert pCi/L to Bq/m³, multiply by 37. To go the other way, divide by 37.

### What is 200 Bq/m³ in pCi/L?

About 5.4 pCi/L. The calculation is 200 divided by 37, which comes to 5.405. So 5.4 pCi/L on a monitor is the same as the Canadian guideline, and 5 pCi/L is 185 Bq/m³, just under it.

### Why does my monitor show pCi/L when Canadian labs use Bq/m³?

Many monitors are built for the American market, where pCi/L is common, and some let you change the display unit. Canadian lab results and Health Canada guidance use Bq/m³. Multiply a pCi/L reading by 37 to compare it with the 200 Bq/m³ guideline, and check that the monitor has passed C-NRPP performance testing.

### Does a reading in pCi/L tell me whether I need mitigation?

Only once you've converted it and confirmed the reading is a long-term average. The guideline applies to your average annual level, and Health Canada says short-term measurements are not appropriate for informing mitigation decisions. If a long-term result is above 200 Bq/m³, Health Canada recommends corrective action within 1 year.
