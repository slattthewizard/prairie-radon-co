# How Does Radon Get Into Your House? Soil Gas, Slabs and Prairie Basements

You can seal every crack you can see in a basement floor and still end up with a high radon result in March. That surprises people, and it's the best way into the question of how radon gets into your house: it isn't coming through one hole, it's being pulled in by the house itself.

This guide walks through where radon comes from under a Prairie home, the pressure difference that draws it inside, the usual entry points in a Winnipeg, Regina or Saskatoon basement, and what does and doesn't keep it out. No scare copy. Just how the air moves.

## Where radon comes from under a Prairie house

Radon is a radioactive gas that forms naturally when uranium in soil and rock breaks down. You can't see it, smell it or taste it. Outdoors it thins out into the open air. Under a house, it collects in the soil and gravel below the slab, and it has somewhere to go.

Health Canada is plain about the stakes, so we'll say it once and move on. Radon exposure is the number one cause of lung cancer in non-smokers, and Health Canada estimates it's behind more than 3,000 lung cancer deaths in Canada each year. It's the second leading cause for smokers. The risk is long-term, not immediate.

It's also common here. For Saskatchewan and Manitoba, the 2024 Cross-Canada Survey on Radon puts it at roughly 1 in 3 of the homes that were tested. Of the Winnipeg homes in that survey, 37.4% were at or above the guideline, and of the Regina homes, 46.6%. Saskatoon came in lowest of the surveyed Prairie cities, at roughly 1 in 6 tested homes. The survey's own line is worth remembering: "There are no areas of Canada that are 'radon-free'."

The guideline itself is 200 Bq/m3. That unit is becquerels per cubic metre, a measure of radioactivity in the air. If you've seen US figures in pCi/L, 37 Bq/m3 equals 1 pCi/L, so 200 Bq/m3 is about 5.4 pCi/L. It's a Health Canada guideline, not a law. Neither Manitoba nor Saskatchewan requires homeowners to test or fix anything.

## How does radon get into your house? The pressure difference

Here's the mechanism in [Health Canada's words](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/health-risks-safety/radiation/radon/about.html): "The air pressure inside your home is usually lower than in the soil surrounding the foundation." Lower pressure inside means the house acts like a very gentle vacuum on the ground beneath it. Soil gas, radon included, moves toward the lower pressure and comes in through any opening in contact with the ground.

A few everyday things make that pull stronger:

- Heat. In a Prairie winter, warm air rises and leaks out through the upper floors and attic. That air gets replaced from below, and some of the replacement comes up out of the soil. As a rule, the colder it is outside, the harder the house draws.
- Anything that blows air out. A clothes dryer, bathroom fans and a range hood all push indoor air outside. The house has to make that air up from somewhere.
- A tight, closed-up house. Windows shut from October to April means less fresh air diluting whatever comes in through the floor.

This is why Health Canada wants at least 91 days of a long-term test to fall in the heating season, and why the Manitoba government says to test ideally in winter, October to April. You want to measure the house during the months it's drawing hardest and you're spending the most time indoors.

It's also why the radon level in two houses on the same street can be very different. Soil, the gravel under the slab, how the foundation was poured and how leaky the upper floors are all change the result. Your neighbour's number doesn't tell you yours.

## Radon entry points in a typical basement

Health Canada lists the usual ways radon gets into a house: "cracks in foundation floor and walls, construction joints, gaps around service pipes, support posts, window casements, floor drains, sumps or cavities inside walls." Here's how those play out in a Prairie basement.

| Entry point | Why soil gas gets through | What usually happens about it |
|---|---|---|
| Open sump pit | A direct opening to the soil and drainage gravel below the slab | An airtight cover, and a trap if the sump also acts as a floor drain. The sump can also become the suction point for a mitigation system. |
| Floor drain | Connects to the space under the slab; a dry trap lets gas straight through | A trap seal device or water trap |
| Cracks in the slab and walls | Concrete shrinks and settles; older slabs have more of them | Caulked and sealed as part of a mitigation job |
| Joint where floor meets wall | Floor and walls are poured separately, leaving a seam around the whole basement | Sealed where it can be reached |
| Gaps around pipes, wires and support posts | Every service line through the foundation leaves a gap | Sealed around the penetration |
| Hollow block walls and wall cavities | Soil gas can travel up inside the wall and out at the top | Assessed by the professional; depends on the wall |
| Crawl space with exposed soil | No slab at all over part of the house | Needs its own approach, and a Regina installer notes crawl spaces can raise the price |

Two things stand out from that list. First, most of these openings are normal, not signs of poor construction. Second, the sump and floor drains are often the biggest single openings, which is why we wrote a separate guide on [radon and sump pit sealing](/blog/radon-sump-pit-sealing/).

## What keeps radon out of your house (and what doesn't)

Sealing helps, but it isn't the fix. Health Canada's 2014 reduction guide is direct: sealing "is not a standalone technique for reducing radon levels." You can't find and close every path, and the house keeps pulling.

The method Health Canada recommends is sub-slab depressurization, also called active soil depressurization. A pipe goes through the basement floor into the gravel or soil underneath, a fan runs continuously, and the gas is vented outdoors. The fan turns the pressure around, so the space under the slab is at lower pressure than the basement and radon gets drawn away from the house instead of into it. Health Canada says it reduces radon by more than 80% in most homes, and recommends having it installed by a C-NRPP certified mitigation professional. Our explainer on [how a radon mitigation system works](/blog/how-a-radon-mitigation-system-works/) has the detail, and our [radon mitigation page](/radon-mitigation/) covers the service side.

A few points worth knowing before anyone quotes:

- The job depends on site conditions. Slab type, what's under it, crawl spaces, a finished ceiling in the way and where the pipe can exit all change the design and the price.
- In a Prairie winter, where the fan sits matters. Health Canada notes a fan in a garage or attic can collect condensation and ice, and a fan indoors venting out a short pipe near ground level reduces that problem.
- The installer should check the system doesn't cause backdrafting of combustion appliances like the furnace, water heater or a wood stove.
- It's not done until it's retested. Health Canada asks for a short-term test by a certified professional once the system is on, then a long-term test the next heating season in the same spot as your original test.

Other approaches don't match it. Health Canada says balanced ventilation with an HRV or ERV generally reduces radon by only 20% to 50%. Opening windows isn't a plan for a Prairie January, and it isn't one Health Canada recommends either.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How does radon get into your house if the basement looks sealed?

The air pressure inside a house is usually lower than in the soil around the foundation, so soil gas gets drawn in through any opening in contact with the ground. That includes hairline cracks, the seam where the floor meets the wall, gaps around pipes, floor drains and sumps. A basement that looks sealed almost always has some of these, and sealing alone isn't a standalone fix according to Health Canada.

### Does radon get in more in winter?

The pull is generally stronger in winter because heated air rises and leaks out up high, and the house makes it up from below. Windows stay shut, so there's less dilution. That's why Health Canada wants at least 91 days of a long-term test in the heating season.

### Can radon come in through a sump pit or floor drain?

Yes. Health Canada names both as entry points. An open sump is a direct opening to the soil under the slab, and it can be fitted with an airtight cover, with a special trap if it also works as a floor drain.

### My house has a radon rough-in pipe. Am I protected?

Not on its own. The rough-in required by the National Building Code "does not actively reduce radon levels"; a fan has to be added to make it a working system. Test first with a long-term test, and if the result is above 200 Bq/m3, have a certified professional look at whether the rough-in can be used.
