# Radon in Manitoba Schools and Daycares: What Parents Can Ask

A child in school or child care spends a lot of the week in one building. Most parents know their own basement should be tested for radon. Fewer have asked whether anyone has tested the classroom, the gym or the daycare playroom.

The short answer for Manitoba is that nobody has to. The longer answer is more useful: what the rules say, what the guideline covers, and what a parent can reasonably ask.

## What the Manitoba radon plan says

On 27 November 2025 the Made-in-Manitoba Radon Action Plan was released as a five-year action plan. It states the position in plain words: "In Manitoba child care facilities are not currently required to test for radon or mitigate." The plan's schools section says schools "are not currently required to test."

That's worth reading twice. It doesn't say schools are high, and it doesn't say they're safe. It says there's no requirement, so whether a given building has been tested is down to the people who run it.

A plan is also not a law. We haven't found a provincial law in Manitoba or Saskatchewan that requires radon testing or mitigation in homes, and the City of Regina calls the Health Canada levels "guidelines only." If you want to read the plan itself, it's on the [Take Action on Radon site (PDF)](https://takeactiononradon.ca/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/Made-in-Manitoba-Radon-Action-Plan-1.pdf).

One gap to be upfront about. The facts we have are about Manitoba. We haven't found anything on what Saskatchewan requires of schools or child care centres, so if you're in Regina, Saskatoon, Moose Jaw or Prince Albert, ask the school division or the centre directly rather than assuming either way.

## What the Canadian guideline covers

The Canadian guideline is 200 Bq/m³ (becquerels per cubic metre), and it isn't limited to houses. Health Canada says it also applies to "public buildings and workplaces with an occupancy of more than 4 hours per day."

A school or child care centre fits that description. Children and staff are there for most of the day, and the guideline's test for the part of a building that counts is any space where a person is likely to spend more than 4 hours a day. The guideline refers to the average annual level, which is why a proper test runs at least 91 days in the heating season.

So the guideline gives a parent a yardstick even where no rule demands a result. If a building has been tested, 200 Bq/m³ is the number to compare it with. For a home that tests over 200, Health Canada says to take corrective action within 1 year.

Health Canada is also clear that "the health risk from radon is long-term not immediate." That's no reason to shrug. It does mean a parent asking calm questions this term is a better response than a panicked phone call tonight.

## Home daycares are homes

Plenty of child care happens in somebody's house. In that case the building is a home, and Health Canada's advice for homes applies: every home should be tested, wherever it is and whatever type it is.

What matters is where the children actually spend their time. The test goes in the lowest lived-in level, in a room where people spend more than 4 hours a day. If the playroom is in the basement, that's where the detector belongs. A crawl space, a cold room or a storage area doesn't count as a normal occupancy area, so a reading there says little about the room the children use.

If you're choosing a home daycare, asking whether the home has had a long-term radon test is a fair question. So is asking to see the result.

## Questions parents can ask a school or child care centre

You don't need to be an expert, and you don't need to be confrontational. A written note to the principal, the division office or the centre's director is enough. Here are questions that tie back to what Health Canada actually recommends:

- Has the building been tested for radon, and when?
- Was it a long-term test of at least 91 days during the heating season? A shorter test doesn't answer the question, because Health Canada says short-term measurements are not appropriate for informing mitigation decisions.
- Were detectors placed in rooms where people spend more than 4 hours a day, such as classrooms, play areas and nap rooms, and not just in a mechanical room or the basement storage?
- Who ran the test? A C-NRPP certified measurement professional is trained to measure radon in residential and large buildings.
- What were the results in Bq/m³, and can parents see them?
- If any room was above 200, what's the plan, and what's the date?
- If nothing has been tested, is a test planned?

Some operators will have answers ready. Others will say it's never come up. Both are useful information. A building that has never been tested is a building with an unknown number, not a bad one.

## What parents can do at home

You can't test a school yourself, but you can test the place where your child sleeps. The nights in one bedroom are a big share of a child's week, and a home test is cheap and fully in your control.

A long-term kit from the provincial lung associations' HomeRadonTest.ca costs about $70 before tax, and the Manitoba government says retail kits generally run between $50 and $100. Winnipeg Public Library lends free screening monitors from 1 November to 30 April, though a library monitor is a screening tool, not a result you should base a decision on. ## Why a school's result doesn't tell you about your house

It cuts both ways. A school with a low reading says nothing about your basement, and a high reading at a daycare doesn't mean the street is affected. Radon depends on the individual building: its foundation, its openings and the air pressure inside it. Our post on [how radon gets into your house](/blog/how-radon-gets-into-your-house/) goes through the mechanism.

The 2024 Cross-Canada Survey on Radon, run by the Evict Radon National Study with Health Canada, found that about 1 in 3 homes in Manitoba and Saskatchewan were at or over 200 Bq/m³ (an unweighted figure). The survey also states that "there are no areas of Canada that are 'radon-free.'" For the Manitoba picture in more detail, see [radon levels across Manitoba and Saskatchewan](/blog/radon-levels-manitoba-saskatchewan/).

## If a building does test high

Health Canada's advice for a home result above 200 Bq/m³ is to take corrective action within 1 year, sooner for higher readings. The guideline's goal is to get the level as low as practicable. In a house, mitigation usually means a pipe through the floor and a fan that runs all the time, and Health Canada says that in most homes it reduces the radon level by more than 80%. We don't have figures for what a school or daycare building would involve, and that's a question for a C-NRPP certified professional, not for a parent or a school secretary.

After any system goes in, Health Canada says to retest. For a home with an active system, that means a short-term test by a certified professional once it's running, a long-term test in the first heating season after, and a retest every five years.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does Manitoba require schools to test for radon?

No. The Made-in-Manitoba Radon Action Plan, released 27 November 2025, says schools are not currently required to test. It says the same about child care facilities, which are not currently required to test for radon or mitigate.

### Does the Canadian radon guideline apply to schools?

Health Canada says the 200 Bq/m³ guideline applies to homes and also to public buildings and workplaces with an occupancy of more than 4 hours per day. It's a guideline, though, not an enforceable limit, so it doesn't by itself force a school to test.

### Can parents ask to see a school's radon results?

You can ask. Nothing we found says a school must publish results, and some may not have any. A polite written request to the principal or division office, asking whether a long-term test has been done and what it showed, is a reasonable place to start.

### Do I need to test my home if my child's school has been tested?

Yes. Health Canada recommends that every home be tested, no matter where it is or what type of building it is. A result for the school building doesn't predict what's in your basement, and a long-term kit from HomeRadonTest.ca is about $70 before tax.
