# How Often to Test for Radon: When a Result Stops Counting

You tested for radon, the number came back under 200, and you filed it. Good. Now, how long does that result stay good?

Not indefinitely. A radon test is a snapshot of one house in one configuration. Change the house, or leave it long enough, and the old number loses its value. Health Canada and C-NRPP both give clear triggers for testing again, and they're easy to put on a list. Here they are, along with what to do after a mitigation system goes in.

## What your first test actually told you

The Canadian guideline is 200 becquerels per cubic metre (Bq/m³), and it applies to your average annual level in the part of the home where you spend more than four hours a day. Health Canada recommends a long-term test of at least 91 days, with that time in the heating season, placed in the lowest lived-in level. Its guide also says testing should run 3 to 12 months to get a true annual average.

So a result is only as good as the conditions it was taken in. It's a measurement of that level, in that house, through those months. It says nothing about the next owner's renovation, or a basement you finish in three years.

Health Canada also recommends that every home be tested, no matter where it is, what type of building it is, or whether it already has a mitigation system. The retest question comes after that one.

## The retest triggers, in one place

Health Canada's measurement guide lists specific situations. C-NRPP, the certification program for radon professionals, adds a general rule for homes that tested low. Here's how they line up.

| Situation | When to test again | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| Result between 0 and 200 Bq/m³ | Within 5 years, or after major renovations | C-NRPP |
| Energy retrofit | After the work | Health Canada measurement guide |
| A floor you didn't test becomes the lowest lived-in level | Test that floor | Health Canada measurement guide |
| Any radon mitigation system installed | After installation (steps below) | Health Canada measurement guide and 2026 factsheet |
| Home with an active system | Every 5 years | Health Canada measurement guide |
| New home | First heating season after occupancy, and again 2 years after the year of construction | Health Canada measurement guide |
| Earlier test ran under 91 days | A new test | Health Canada measurement guide |

Most homeowners will hit one of the first two rows. Let's go through them.

## A low result is good for about five years

C-NRPP's wording for a home that tested between 0 and 200 Bq/m³ is to retest within 5 years or after you make major renovations. That's the baseline. It isn't a sign that anything is wrong. It's housekeeping, because houses change and a kit costs less than most things on your maintenance list.

A long-term kit from HomeRadonTest.ca runs about $70 before tax. The Government of Manitoba puts retail kits at "generally ranges between $50 and $100." Put the date on your calendar when you open the result. Our [radon testing page](/radon-testing/) covers where to place the detector and how to read the result.

## After renovations and energy retrofits

Health Canada lists energy retrofits as a reason to retest. Sealing a house, replacing windows or changing the heating and ventilation can change how air moves through it. We don't have a source that tells you which way the number will go in any particular house, and that's the point: the old result no longer describes the house you have.

C-NRPP's phrase is "major renovations," which is the safer reading to follow. Finishing a basement, adding a floor, digging up the slab or moving the furnace all count as work worth a fresh test afterward. The post on [radon after an energy retrofit](/blog/radon-after-energy-retrofit/) goes into it.

The related trigger is a floor you didn't test. If you've turned a storage area or an unfinished basement into a bedroom or a rec room, it's now the lowest lived-in level and the old result didn't measure it. Health Canada's guideline applies to normal occupancy areas, meaning anywhere a person is likely to spend more than 4 hours a day. Test the new space.

## New homes get two tests

A new house can get a radon test earlier than you'd expect. Health Canada recommends testing in the first heating season after occupancy, and again 2 years after the year of construction. The second date is easy to forget, so put it in the calendar when you move in.

A rough-in pipe, if the builder included one, is only a capped pipe. It doesn't actively reduce radon until a fan is added, so a new home needs the same test as an old one. See the [radon rough-in post](/blog/radon-rough-in-new-homes/) for what that pipe does.

If you bought a new-to-you house and have never tested it, don't wait for the five-year mark. Test during your first heating season. The post on [radon testing when buying a house](/blog/radon-testing-when-buying-a-house/) covers the options at purchase.

## After a mitigation system goes in

This is where retesting matters most, because the system is the thing you paid for and a test is the only proof it worked. Health Canada's 2026 factsheet gives three steps.

1. **Short-term test once the system is on.** A certified professional runs it to confirm the system is lowering the level below the Canadian guideline.
2. **Long-term test in the first heating season.** Do it in the same location as your original test, so the two numbers can be compared.
3. **Retest regularly.** Inspect and maintain the system, and retest every five years.

Health Canada's measurement guide says the same about every five years for homes with active systems. An older 2014 guide uses a different schedule ("within two years of the system activation and at five-year intervals afterwards"), so if a contractor quotes that, the current factsheet's steps are the ones to follow.

Between tests, look at the manometer (the U-shaped gauge on the pipe) from time to time. A level difference between the two sides shows the system is working, so two level sides are worth a call to the installer. The [manometer post](/blog/radon-manometer-reading/) shows what to look for. And never switch the fan off to save electricity. Health Canada's 2014 estimate of the running cost is approximately $50 to $75 a year, a national figure from that year.

Fans wear out. The same 2014 guide says a fan's lifespan "can vary between five to ten or more years," with replacement at $200 to $300, again a 2014 national estimate and not a current Prairie price. A five-year retest is a good moment to ask whether the fan is still doing its job. If the test comes back high after a system was installed, the post on [radon still high after mitigation](/blog/radon-still-high-after-mitigation/) has the next steps.

## What not to count as a retest

A few things look like retests and aren't.

- **A short test under 91 days.** Health Canada says that for measurements shorter than that "a new test is recommended." The guideline is an annual average, so a three-week reading doesn't show it.
- **A library monitor.** Programs in Winnipeg, Regina and elsewhere lend digital monitors. They're useful screening tools, not a replacement for a long-term test.
- **A detector that was moved partway through.** A test is only valid if it stays in the right place, at least 30 cm above the floor, away from walls, vents and sumps, for the whole period. The post on [where to place a radon detector](/blog/where-to-place-radon-detector/) has details.

## What to do with a number over 200

A retest isn't a way to hunt for a lower number. If a long-term test in the right place comes back above 200 Bq/m³, Health Canada recommends corrective action within 1 year, and sooner the higher the number. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How often should I test for radon?

If your last long-term result was between 0 and 200 Bq/m³, C-NRPP says to retest within 5 years or after major renovations. If you have a mitigation system, Health Canada says to retest every 5 years. Test sooner after an energy retrofit, or when you start living on a floor you haven't tested.

### Do I need to retest after a radon mitigation system is installed?

Yes. Health Canada's current factsheet calls for a short-term test by a certified professional once the system is switched on, then a long-term test in the first heating season in the same location as your original test, then a retest every five years.

### Should I retest a new house?

Health Canada recommends testing in the first heating season after you move in, and again 2 years after the year of construction. A rough-in pipe doesn't count as mitigation until a fan is added, so a new home still needs a test.

### Does a renovation change my radon level?

It can, and that's why Health Canada lists energy retrofits as a reason to retest, and C-NRPP says to retest after major renovations. We don't have a source that says which direction a particular renovation will move the result. The only way to know is a new long-term test of at least 91 days.

### Can I use a short-term test to retest?

Not for a decision. Health Canada says short-term measurements aren't appropriate for informing mitigation decisions, and that any test under 91 days calls for a new test. The one short-term test Health Canada endorses is the one a certified professional runs right after a mitigation system is switched on.
