# HRV and Radon: Will a Ventilator Lower a High Reading?

A heat recovery ventilator is a good thing to have in a tight, well-insulated house. It swaps stale indoor air for fresh outdoor air and saves most of the heat on the way. So it's a fair question: if a radon test comes back high, can the HRV you already own, or are about to buy, deal with it?

Sometimes it takes the edge off. For most homes over the guideline, it isn't enough on its own. Health Canada is specific about this, and the numbers are worth looking at before you spend money on the wrong thing.

## What an HRV does, and what radon needs

An HRV (or its cousin the ERV, which also moves some moisture) is a balanced ventilation system. One fan pushes outdoor air in, another pushes the same amount of indoor air out, and a core between them passes heat across. The point is fresh air without throwing away the heat you paid for.

Radon is a different kind of problem. It comes out of the soil, and Health Canada explains that "the air pressure inside your home is usually lower than in the soil surrounding the foundation." The house draws soil gas in through cracks, joints, sumps, floor drains and gaps around pipes. A balanced ventilator dilutes whatever is already inside. It doesn't shut off the supply.

A sub-slab system works at the source. A pipe goes through the foundation floor, and a fan "runs 24/7, pulling radon out and venting it safely outdoors," in Health Canada's wording. That changes the pressure under the slab so less gas gets in to begin with. The two approaches aren't in the same league, and the published reduction figures show it.

## What Health Canada says about HRVs and radon

Health Canada's current factsheet, [Reduce radon in your home](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/environmental-workplace-health/reports-publications/radiation/reduce-radon-home.html), says balanced ventilation (HRV or ERV) "generally reduce radon levels by only 20% to 50%." The older *Radon Reduction Guide for Canadians* from 2014 puts it at 25 to 50 per cent. The same guide says that "when large radon reductions (50 per cent or more) are desired, active soil depressurization is almost always the recommended approach."

Here's how the main options compare, using Health Canada's figures.

| Method | Reduction Health Canada gives | Where it fits |
|---|---|---|
| HRV or ERV (balanced ventilation) | 20% to 50% (2026 factsheet); 25% to 50% (2014 guide) | A modest cut, mostly for readings only slightly over the guideline |
| Active sub-slab depressurization | More than 80% on most Health Canada pages; 90% or more in the 2026 factsheet | The usual choice when you need a large reduction |
| Passive soil depressurization (new homes) | 40% to 90% | Built in at construction, with a fan added later if needed |

The wide ranges matter. A range of 20% to 50% means some homes see a small change and a few see a decent one. You don't find out which kind of house you have until you test again.

## What the numbers mean for your reading

Here's the arithmetic, not a prediction for any particular house. The Canadian guideline is 200 Bq/m³, and Health Canada recommends you take action within 1 year if your long-term average is above it.

- A home that tests at 300 Bq/m³, with a 20% to 50% cut, would land between 150 and 240. Only the better half of that range gets under the guideline.
- A home at 400 Bq/m³ would land between 200 and 320. Most of that range is still above it.
- A home at 800 Bq/m³ would land between 400 and 640. Nowhere near it.

Compare that with a reduction of more than 80%. The same 400 Bq/m³ home would end up at 80 Bq/m³ or less, and the 800 Bq/m³ home at 160 or less.

So an HRV can only be the answer when the original reading is close to 200, and even then you'd be relying on the lucky end of the range. Health Canada's advice is to bring levels down "to as low as practically possible," not to scrape under the line.

## When an HRV might be enough

There are a few situations where ventilation is reasonable to talk about.

**The reading is just over 200.** If the number is in the low 200s and you were already planning to install an HRV for other reasons, the radon benefit is a bonus. You'd still need a long-term retest to see where it landed.

**You're building or renovating anyway.** A new house may get a ventilator for air quality and comfort. That's a good reason to have it. It isn't a radon plan. Health Canada recommends that all new homes include a passive soil depressurization system, and the [radon rough-in post](/blog/radon-rough-in-new-homes/) covers what that pipe does and doesn't do.

**You'd rather avoid drilling through the slab.** Fair enough, but ask the certified professional to explain in writing why they'd recommend one approach over the other for your reading. If the answer is an HRV, the quote should say what reduction they expect and what happens if the retest comes back high.

## What an HRV costs next to a sub-slab system

Health Canada's 2014 guide puts an HRV at "between $1,500 and $3,500 (material and labour)" as a national figure. That's a 2014 estimate, so treat it as a rough marker, not a current quote.

For a sub-slab radon system, Winnipeg installers advertise systems from about $1,500 to $3,000, and a Regina installer quotes $2,500 to $3,500. These are advertised prices from each company's own site, not a market survey. We haven't found local prices for Saskatoon, Brandon, Moose Jaw or Prince Albert, so we won't make any up. An HRV costs about what a radon system does, and does much less about radon. If the HRV is for comfort and air quality, that can still be money well spent. If the goal is radon, it's the wrong tool.

## If you already have an HRV

Having one doesn't mean your radon is fine. Test anyway. Health Canada recommends every home be tested, whatever the type of building, so a ventilated house gets the same long-term test as any other: at least 91 days, with that time during the heating season, in the lowest lived-in level. ## Don't skip the retest

Whatever you choose, the follow-up is the same. Health Canada's steps for a mitigation system are a short-term test by a certified professional once it's running, then a long-term test during the first heating season in the same spot as the original, then a retest every five years. An HRV hasn't got a special exemption. If you install one for radon, the retest is the only evidence it worked.

Two things we won't suggest. Opening windows and running fans aren't radon fixes, and neither is turning an HRV up and hoping. And if a contractor promises a specific reduction before the house has been tested, ask what they're basing it on. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does an HRV reduce radon?

A little, according to Health Canada. Its 2026 factsheet says balanced ventilation generally reduces radon levels by only 20% to 50%, and the 2014 guide gives 25% to 50%. That's much less than a sub-slab system, which Health Canada puts at more than 80% on most of its pages.

### Is an HRV or a radon mitigation system better for a high reading?

For a high reading, a sub-slab system. Health Canada's 2014 guide says active soil depressurization is almost always the recommended approach when you want a reduction of 50 per cent or more. An HRV can make sense for ventilation and air quality, but it isn't built to stop radon entering.

### Can I count on an HRV to get under 200 Bq/m³?

Only if the reading was already close to 200, and even then the result varies from house to house. Do the arithmetic with the range: a 20% cut to a reading of 300 leaves 240, and a 20% cut to 400 leaves 320. The only way to find out is a long-term retest of at least 91 days.

### What does an HRV cost compared with a radon system?

Health Canada's 2014 guide gives $1,500 to $3,500 for an HRV, material and labour, as a national figure. Winnipeg installers advertise radon systems from about $1,500 to $3,000, and a Regina installer quotes $2,500 to $3,500. The figures are from different years, so ask a certified professional for a current written quote.

### If I have an HRV, do I still need to test for radon?

Yes. Health Canada recommends every home be tested, whatever its type or equipment. Use a long-term test of at least 91 days, with that time in the heating season, placed in the lowest lived-in level.
