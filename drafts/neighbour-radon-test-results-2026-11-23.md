# Neighbour's Radon Test Result: Why It Won't Predict Yours

It's a reasonable thing to ask over the fence. The people next door tested, the number came back low, and you'd like to skip the hassle. Or it came back high and now you're wondering whether to worry.

Either way, a neighbour's result is interesting and not much more. Radon is measured house by house, and Health Canada's advice reflects that. This post covers why, and what the city-level numbers can and can't tell you.

## What Health Canada says about testing every home

Health Canada "recommends every home should be tested, no matter where it is located, the type of building, or if it already has a mitigation system."

The wording is blunt on purpose. Location isn't a reason to skip a test. Building type isn't. Not even having a mitigation system is. If a system in your own house doesn't excuse you from testing, a result from the house next door certainly doesn't.

The 2024 Cross-Canada Survey on Radon, carried out by the Evict Radon National Study with Health Canada, CAREX Canada and BCCDC, says it just as plainly: "There are no areas of Canada that are 'radon-free.'" Your street is inside that sentence too.

## Why two houses side by side can read differently

Radon comes out of the soil, and the way it gets into a building has to do with the building. Health Canada explains that "the air pressure inside your home is usually lower than in the soil surrounding the foundation," so soil gas is drawn in through whatever openings exist. The entry points it lists are cracks in the foundation floor and walls, construction joints, gaps around service pipes, support posts, window casements, floor drains, sumps and cavities inside walls.

Those details vary between houses, even identical ones on one street. A few examples of what can differ:

- **The openings.** One basement has an open sump and a cracked slab. The one next door has a sealed lid and a clean floor.
- **The renovations.** Health Canada says to retest after energy retrofits, which means a house that changed since its last test may no longer match the old number. Our post on [radon after an energy retrofit](/blog/radon-after-energy-retrofit/) explains why.
- **The level that was tested.** Health Canada measures in the lowest lived-in level. A neighbour who tested the main floor and a household that tested a finished basement aren't comparing like with like.
- **How the test was done.** The guideline is about the average annual level, and Health Canada says short-term measurements aren't appropriate for informing mitigation decisions. If your neighbour used a few days with a gadget from the hardware store, the figure doesn't carry much weight, even for their own house.
- **The house type.** In the 2024 survey, 21.4% of single-detached homes on the Prairies were at or over 200 Bq/m³, against 16.1% of semi-detached and 10.3% of row houses.

We can't tell you which of these matters most on your street. That's the point. A test of your own house takes the guesswork out.

## What the city numbers show

Cities do differ, and they're worth a look. They also show how wide the spread is inside one city. Here are the 2024 Cross-Canada Survey figures for the four cities it reports on:

| City | Homes at or over 200 Bq/m³ | Geometric mean (Bq/m³) | Highest reading (Bq/m³) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Winnipeg | 37.4% | 139.7 | 1,362 |
| Brandon | 36.1% | 147.0 | 559 |
| Regina | 46.6% | 182.7 | 11,333 |
| Saskatoon | 17.8% | 107.2 | 820 |

These are unweighted figures from a volunteer sample. The report warns that "unweighted values may increase or decrease somewhat once appropriately weighted." They're not a random draw from every house in each city, and the Brandon sample is small, with about 50 to 99 properties. Moose Jaw and Prince Albert aren't in the city tables at all.

What the table does show is that even in Saskatoon, the lowest of the four, roughly 1 in 6 tested homes were at or over 200 Bq/m³, and the highest reading was 820. Regina's highest reading was 11,333. A city average is a statement about a crowd. It says nothing about the house you sleep in.

## If your neighbour tested low

Good for them. It doesn't tell you anything about your house, and you have the same reasons to test as anyone. A long-term test of at least 91 days during the heating season, in the lowest level you live in, is what Health Canada recommends. The Manitoba government suggests testing ideally in winter, October to April.

A long-term kit from the provincial lung associations' HomeRadonTest.ca is about $70 before tax. Libraries in Winnipeg (1 November to 30 April) and Regina lend free digital monitors, and Moose Jaw's library lends them from 1 October to 1 April. They're screening tools, not a final answer. ## If your neighbour tested high

Don't read it as your own number, and don't ignore it either. A high result next door is a reason to test sooner, not evidence that you're over. The guideline is 200 Bq/m³, and Health Canada's advice for a home above it is to take corrective action within 1 year. Health Canada adds that no level is considered risk free, and that the health risk from radon is long-term, not immediate. So there's time to test properly rather than guess.

A few things to keep straight if the neighbour mentions their fix:

- **Their system doesn't protect your house.** Radon systems work on one building. A pipe and fan next door pull nothing from under yours.
- **Check any contractor yourself.** Health Canada recommends a C-NRPP certified mitigation professional, and you can check a certification in the [C-NRPP directory](https://c-nrpp.ca/find-a-professional/). A name from a neighbour is a lead. It isn't a credential.
- **Prices are specific to a house.** Winnipeg installers advertise systems from about $1,500 to $3,000, and a Regina installer quotes $2,500 to $3,500, but those are advertised figures and what your neighbour paid isn't a quote for you. Our [radon mitigation cost page](/radon-mitigation-cost/) has more.

Health Canada says that in most homes a mitigation system reduces the radon level by more than 80%, and our post on [what level needs mitigation](/blog/what-radon-level-needs-mitigation/) explains what to do with a result.

## How to bring it up with a neighbour

Asking is fine. It's a decent way to get people testing, and a talk about numbers can help both households. Here are a few questions that give you something useful:

- Did they use a long-term test or a short one?
- How many days did it run, and in which season?
- Which part of the house was it in?
- Has anything changed since, such as a renovation or a new sump pump?

Then test your own place and swap results if you both like. Just don't treat theirs as a stand-in for yours.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### My neighbour's radon test was low. Do I still need to test?

Yes. Health Canada recommends that every home be tested, regardless of location or building type. Radon levels depend on the individual house, including its openings, its renovation history and the level tested, so one result can't stand in for another.

### Are there any areas without radon?

No. The 2024 Cross-Canada Survey on Radon states that there are no areas of Canada that are radon-free. Levels differ between cities and between houses in the same city, but no area has been shown to be exempt.

### Does a city's average tell me my house's level?

No. City figures are samples from many houses, and the 2024 survey's city results are unweighted samples of volunteers. Regina's tested homes ranged up to 11,333 Bq/m³, and Saskatoon's, the lowest city of the four, still had roughly 1 in 6 tested homes at or over 200.

### How long should my own radon test run?

At least 91 days, with that period falling in the heating season, in the lowest lived-in level of the home. Health Canada recommends tests of 3 to 12 months to measure the average annual level, and says short-term tests shouldn't drive mitigation decisions.
