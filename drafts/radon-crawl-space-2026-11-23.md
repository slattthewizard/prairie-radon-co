# Radon in a Crawl Space: Where to Test and What to Expect

If your house sits over a crawl space, you may have wondered where a radon detector is supposed to go. Down there, where the soil is closest? Upstairs, where you live? The answer from Health Canada is fairly clear, and it surprises people: not in the crawl space.

This article explains why the crawl space is left out of the guideline, where a test belongs instead, how a mitigation system adapts to a house with a crawl space, and why quotes for these jobs can come in higher. We'll also say where our sources stop. There is no published Prairie price for a crawl space job, so we won't invent one.

## Why the crawl space isn't where you test

Canada's radon guideline is 200 Bq/m³. It applies to the average annual radon level in what Health Canada calls the "normal occupancy area" of a building. That means any part of the building where a person is likely to spend more than 4 hours per day.

A finished basement or a basement apartment counts. Health Canada's list of areas that do not count includes a crawl space, an unfinished basement, a cold room, a storage area, a furnace room and a laundry room.

The reasoning is about people, not soil. The guideline is meant to describe the air you breathe for hours at a time. Nobody lives in a crawl space, so a reading taken there doesn't tell you whether your household is above or below 200 Bq/m³. It tells you about a space you don't use.

Health Canada's measurement guide says the same thing from the other direction. Measurements "should be made in a normal occupancy area of the lowest lived-in level of the home." It also says to keep the detector away from sump holes, crawl spaces and exposed foundation cracks. Putting a detector in or next to a crawl space opening can skew the reading.

There's a practical side too. A crawl space is cramped and not meant for sitting a detector for three months. Skip it.

## Where to test a house with a crawl space

Test in the lowest level where someone actually spends time. In a house with a crawl space and no basement, that's usually the main floor: the living room, a bedroom, or wherever the household spends its hours. In a house with a partial basement and a crawl space, test in the finished basement living area, not the crawl space.

The rest of the method is the same as for any house:

- **Use a long-term test.** Health Canada says to run it for a minimum of 3 months, with at least 91 days during the heating season. Health Canada says short-term measurements are not appropriate for informing mitigation decisions.
- **Place it in the breathing zone.** At least 30 cm (1 ft) above the floor and at least 30 cm from walls or ceilings. Keep it away from heat sources, direct sunlight, vents, doors and windows.
- **Time it for the cold months.** Manitoba's government says to test ideally in winter, October to April.
- **Pick a proper kit.** A long-term kit from HomeRadonTest.ca, the provincial lung associations' partner, is $70.00 before tax. The Manitoba government says retail kits generally run between $50 and $100. The post on [where to place a radon detector](/blog/where-to-place-radon-detector/) covers placement in more detail.

If you borrow a library monitor, that's a screening tool. Winnipeg Public Library lends one free from 1 November to 30 April, and Regina Public Library lends to Regina residents. Don't take it for the final word. Our post on [long-term vs short-term radon tests](/blog/long-term-vs-short-term-radon-test/) explains why.

## Why the soil under a crawl space still matters

Leaving the crawl space out of the guideline doesn't mean it's irrelevant to the house. Radon enters from the soil because, as Health Canada says, "the air pressure inside your home is usually lower than in the soil surrounding the foundation." The house draws air up from below, and whatever is under it comes along.

Health Canada lists the usual entry points as cracks in the foundation floor and walls, construction joints, gaps around service pipes, support posts, window casements, floor drains, sumps and cavities inside walls.

Prairie numbers are high enough to make testing worthwhile regardless. In the 2024 Cross-Canada survey, 46.6% of tested Regina homes, 37.4% of Winnipeg homes and 36.1% of Brandon homes (a small sample) came in at or above 200 Bq/m³. Saskatoon was lower, roughly 1 in 6 tested homes. These are unweighted figures from volunteer samples, so they show that radon is common here and say nothing about your house.

## How mitigation adapts to a crawl space

Health Canada's factsheet describes the standard fix. A pipe is installed through the foundation floor and draws radon from beneath the home. A fan attached to the pipe runs 24/7, pulling radon out and venting it outdoors. The factsheet says installers adjust the system "whether you have a foundation slab, crawl space, or sump pit."

So a crawl space is a known situation, not an exception. The details are the professional's call. What the sources let us say is this:

- **The method is the same family.** It's active soil depressurization: suction under the structure, a fan, and a vent to outside.
- **Sealing helps but doesn't carry the job.** Health Canada says cracks, gaps around pipes and other entry points should be sealed to maximize the efficacy of a ventilation system. It also says sealing alone "is not a standalone technique for reducing radon levels." Our post on [sealing foundation cracks for radon](/blog/sealing-foundation-cracks-radon/) goes into that.
- **Results should be good.** Health Canada says "in most situations, you can readily achieve a final level less than 200 Bq/m³" and that systems in most homes reduce radon by more than 80%. The 2026 factsheet puts it at 90% or more.
- **The fan stays on.** Health Canada says it should never be turned off. Where the fan and pipe sit also matters in a cold climate, because a fan and pipe outside the living space can cool in winter, leading to condensation and possibly ice. You can ask a professional how they'd handle that in your house.

## Why quotes can cost more

Here is the part we can source, and the part we can't.

A Regina installer, Regina Radon Solutions, quotes $2,500 to $3,500 for a system. The same company says "more complicated systems with a crawl space or multiple suction points could be much higher." That's one contractor's own statement, not a market survey, and it doesn't give a number.

Winnipeg installers advertise systems from about $1,500 to $3,000. We found no published price for Saskatoon, Brandon, Moose Jaw or Prince Albert. We also found no price anywhere on the Prairies for a crawl space job specifically. The reasonable expectation is that a more complicated job costs more. How much more is something only an on-site quote will tell you.

Two practical consequences:

1. **Get the quote in writing, with the scope spelled out.** Ask what's being done in the crawl space, how many suction points there will be, where the fan and pipe will sit and where the system vents. The [radon mitigation quote checklist](/blog/radon-mitigation-quote-checklist/) has questions to ask, and the [mitigation cost page](/radon-mitigation-cost/) lays out the advertised figures with their sources.
2. **Check the money rules against the quote.** The Manitoba Hydro Home Energy Efficiency Loan covers radon mitigation up to $5,000 over 5 years at 6.2% (O.A.C.), repaid on the energy bill with no down payment, and requires a C-NRPP certified contractor. The loan limit is a limit, so a job that comes in above it leaves you covering the rest. In Saskatchewan, Lung Saskatchewan says radon mitigation counts as an eligible expense under the Home Renovation Tax Credit; confirm that with CRA.

## After the system is installed

Health Canada's steps are the same for every house. Once the system is turned on, a certified professional does a short-term test to confirm it's lowering the level below the guideline. During the first heating season, you do a long-term test in the same location as your original test. After that, inspect and maintain the system and retest every five years. Look at the manometer from time to time, too. The post on [reading a radon manometer](/blog/radon-manometer-reading/) shows how.

If you've already tested and the number is above 200 Bq/m³, Health Canada recommends corrective action within 1 year, and sooner for higher levels. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Should I put a radon detector in my crawl space?

No. Health Canada excludes a crawl space from the normal occupancy area, and it says to keep detectors away from crawl spaces. Test in the lowest level where someone spends more than 4 hours a day, in the breathing zone, for at least 91 days during the heating season.

### Can a house with a crawl space be fixed for radon?

Yes. Health Canada's factsheet says installers adjust sub-slab-style systems to suit a foundation slab, a crawl space or a sump pit. In most homes the system reduces radon by more than 80%, and a certified professional will decide on the layout after seeing the house.

### Does a crawl space make radon mitigation more expensive?

It can. A Regina installer says more complicated systems with a crawl space or multiple suction points could be much higher than its $2,500 to $3,500 range, but it gives no figure. We found no published Prairie price for crawl space work, so get a written quote.

### Is the radon level in my crawl space the same as in my living room?

Not necessarily, and the guideline doesn't compare them. It applies to the average annual level where you spend your time. A reading from the crawl space isn't a substitute, so test the living area with a long-term kit.
