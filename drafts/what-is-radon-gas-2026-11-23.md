# What Is Radon Gas? A Plain Guide for Prairie Homeowners

Radon is a radioactive gas that occurs naturally in the ground. You can't see it, smell it or taste it. It seeps up out of the soil, and some of it ends up inside buildings, where it can collect at levels worth knowing about.

That's the short version. This article covers the longer one: where radon comes from, how it gets into a house, why a test is the only way to find out what's in yours, and what the Canadian guideline says. It's written for homeowners in Manitoba and Saskatchewan, where radon is common.

## Where radon comes from

Radon comes out of the soil. The trouble starts when it's drawn from under a building into the rooms above, where people spend their time.

Because it's a natural part of the ground, it isn't something a builder or a previous owner did. The 2024 Cross-Canada Survey on Radon puts it plainly: there are no areas of Canada that are "radon-free."

## How it gets into a house

Health Canada explains that the air pressure inside your home is usually lower than in the soil around the foundation. That small difference draws soil gas in through whatever openings it can find.

Health Canada lists the usual entry points:

- cracks in the foundation floor and walls
- construction joints
- gaps around service pipes
- support posts
- window casements
- floor drains
- sumps
- cavities inside walls

Our post on [how radon gets into your house](/blog/how-radon-gets-into-your-house/) goes through each route. The short point is that you can't judge a house by looking at it. Health Canada recommends testing whatever the type of building.

## Why you can't tell without testing

Radon levels vary from house to house, which is why city averages can't tell you about yours. Health Canada says every home should be tested, no matter where it's located, what type of building it is, or whether it already has a mitigation system.

A neighbour's result doesn't predict yours, and a general feeling that your basement is dry and fine tells you nothing. Radon is a gas with no smell, so there's no early warning.

What does work is a long-term test, which Health Canada recommends for a minimum of 3 months, ideally during the fall or winter. The test should include at least 91 days inside the heating season. It measures the average annual level, which is what the guideline is written around. Short-term tests are useful as a first look, but Health Canada says they aren't appropriate for deciding on mitigation.

## What the guideline says

The Canadian guideline is 200 becquerels per cubic metre (Bq/m³). A becquerel is a measure of radioactivity. The guideline applies to the average annual radon level in the normal occupancy area of a building, meaning any part of it where a person is likely to spend more than 4 hours a day. That includes a finished basement or a basement apartment. It excludes a crawl space, an unfinished basement, a cold room, a storage area, a furnace room and a laundry room.

Three things to know about it:

1. If a long-term test is above 200, Health Canada recommends taking corrective action within 1 year. The higher the reading, the sooner.
2. The risk below 200 is small, but Health Canada says there is no level that's considered risk-free. Each homeowner decides what level of exposure they're willing to accept.
3. It's a guideline, not a law. We found no Manitoba or Saskatchewan law requiring homeowners to test or reduce radon.

If you're comparing with American sources, you may see pCi/L instead. Health Canada gives the conversion: 37 Bq/m³ equals 1 pCi/L, so 200 Bq/m³ works out to about 5.4 pCi/L.

## How common is it on the Prairies?

Health Canada's current factsheet says 1 in 5 homes in Canada have high radon levels. On the Prairies, the 2024 survey puts it at 1 in 5 (20.0%) of households in the Prairie and Northwest Territories region at or above 200 Bq/m³. The Government of Saskatchewan, citing Health Canada, says Saskatchewan has some of the highest levels in the country, alongside Yukon, Manitoba and New Brunswick.

For individual cities, the 2024 survey's unweighted figures from volunteer samples are 37.4% of tested properties in Winnipeg, 36.1% in Brandon, 46.6% in Regina and 17.8% in Saskatoon. Those are convenience samples, so read them as a sign that radon is common, not as a prediction for any one house. The older 2012 survey found a lower rate in Winnipeg and a much higher one in Brandon, and the two surveys shouldn't be set against each other as a trend. ## Why people worry about it

Health Canada says radon exposure is the number one cause of lung cancer in non-smokers, and that people who smoke and are exposed to radon have an even higher risk. It also says the health risk is long-term rather than immediate: the longer you're exposed to high levels, the greater the risk. We cover this properly in a separate post on [radon and lung cancer](/blog/radon-and-lung-cancer/).

The concern is long-term exposure at home, which is why the guideline is written around annual averages.

## What you can do about it

Test first. A kit from HomeRadonTest.ca, the ordering site for both provincial lung associations, costs $70.00 per 90-day kit before tax, with analysis at the Saskatchewan Research Council included. The Manitoba government says retail kits generally range between $50 and $100. Put it in the lowest level you live in, in the breathing zone at least 30 cm above the floor, away from vents, sumps and windows. The post on [where to place a radon detector](/blog/where-to-place-radon-detector/) covers the rest, and our [radon testing page](/radon-testing/) explains the process.

If the result is above 200, the standard fix is sub-slab depressurization. A pipe goes through the foundation floor, a fan runs continuously to draw radon from under the house, and the gas is vented outdoors. Health Canada says this typically reduces levels by more than 80%, and its 2026 factsheet says 90% or more. Installation can usually be done in a day. The post on [how a mitigation system works](/blog/how-a-radon-mitigation-system-works/) walks through it.

Health Canada recommends a contractor certified through C-NRPP, the Canadian National Radon Proficiency Program. Certification is voluntary, and provinces haven't regulated radon professionals, so it's worth checking. The post on [what C-NRPP certification means](/blog/what-is-c-nrpp-certification/) explains how, and the [C-NRPP find a professional tool](https://c-nrpp.ca/find-a-professional/) lets you look anyone up.

Opening windows or running a fan in the basement isn't a radon fix. Neither is sealing cracks alone: Health Canada says sealing is not a standalone technique, though it helps a system work better.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is radon gas in simple terms?

Radon is a natural radioactive gas that comes from the ground. You can't see, smell or taste it. It gets into homes through openings in the foundation and can build up indoors, and the only way to know how much is in your house is a long-term test.

### How does radon get into a house?

Air pressure inside a house is usually lower than in the soil around the foundation, so soil gas is drawn in through cracks in the floor and walls, construction joints, gaps around pipes, floor drains and sumps. Health Canada lists these as the common entry points.

### What level of radon is dangerous?

Health Canada's guideline is 200 Bq/m³, measured as an average annual level. Above that, it recommends action within 1 year. Below it the risk is small, but Health Canada says no level is considered risk-free, so the decision about how much exposure to accept is yours.

### Can I smell or see radon?

No. Radon is invisible and has no smell, which is why a long-term test is the only reliable way to find out what's in your house.

### Is there a law that requires radon testing in Manitoba or Saskatchewan?

We found no law in either province requiring homeowners to test or mitigate. The guideline is a recommendation. Health Canada still advises that every home be tested.
