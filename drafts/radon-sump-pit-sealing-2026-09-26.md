# Radon Sump Pit Sealing: Why the Lid Matters in a Prairie Basement

Lift the lid off your sump and you're looking down a hole in your foundation. It goes through the slab, into the gravel and the weeping tile, and into the soil gas underneath. If the lid is a square of plywood or a loose plastic disc, a radon sump pit is one of the most direct ways that gas can get into a basement where people sleep, watch TV or rent a suite.

Health Canada lists sumps right alongside foundation cracks and gaps around service pipes as ways radon gets in. It also says plainly that when a radon system goes in, the sump has to be sealed. What it doesn't say, and what nobody honest will tell you, is that a better lid will fix a high reading on its own. This article covers what the lid does, what it doesn't, and where it fits in the order of things.

## Why a radon sump pit matters more than a crack in the floor

Radon is a radioactive gas that comes out of the soil. You can't see or smell it. It gets into houses because, as Health Canada puts it, "the air pressure inside your home is usually lower than in the soil surrounding the foundation." The house pulls a little, and soil gas follows the easiest route in.

A hairline crack is a small route. A sump pit is a big one. In a lot of Prairie basements the pit is where the weeping tile around the footings drains to, so it connects to the gravel bed and the perimeter drainage around the whole foundation. An open or loosely covered pit gives that soil air a clear, wide opening into the house.

That's why Health Canada's guide on measuring radon tells you to keep a detector away from sump holes. A reading taken beside an open pit tells you about the pit, not about the room your kids sleep in.

It's worth being straight about the health side once. Health Canada says radon is the leading cause of lung cancer in people who have never smoked, and estimates it's behind more than 3,000 lung cancer deaths in Canada each year. It's the second leading cause for smokers. That's the reason to deal with it. It isn't a reason to panic about your sump this afternoon.

## Test before you touch the lid

The honest first step isn't a new sump cover. It's a test.

Health Canada's guideline is 200 Bq/m3 (becquerels per cubic metre, a measure of how much radioactive decay is happening in a cubic metre of air). For comparison with American sources, 200 Bq/m3 is about 5.4 pCi/L. The guideline applies to the average annual level in the part of the home you actually use for four or more hours a day, like a finished basement or a basement bedroom. It's a guideline, not a law. No Manitoba or Saskatchewan law requires a homeowner to test or fix anything.

Prairie numbers are high enough to take seriously. Of the Regina homes tested in the 2024 Cross-Canada survey, 46.6% came in at or above 200. Of the Winnipeg homes tested, 37.4% did. Saskatoon was lower, roughly 1 in 6 tested homes. Those are unweighted samples from homes that volunteered, so they tell you radon is common here. They don't tell you anything about your house.

A proper test runs at least 91 days, ideally through the heating season, in the lowest level you live in. Put the detector in the breathing zone, at least 30 cm above the floor and 30 cm from the walls, and away from the sump, vents, windows and heat. HomeRadonTest.ca sells a long-term kit for $70.00 before tax, and the Manitoba government puts kits at "between $50 and $100". ## What Health Canada says about sealing a sump pit for radon

Health Canada's *Radon Reduction Guide for Canadians* deals with sumps directly. Three points from it matter for a homeowner.

First, an open sump can be fitted with an airtight cover. If that sump also acts as a floor drain, the guide says to "add a special trap to the airtight cover" so the drain still works and air can't come up through it.

Second, the sump can be more than a hole to close off. In what the guide calls active sump-hole depressurization, "the sump can be capped and sealed so that it can continue to drain water and also serve as the location for a radon suction pipe." In other words, the pit you were worried about can become the place the system pulls from. The guide adds that if a basement floor drain is connected to the sump, a mechanical trap seal device or a water trap should go in.

Third, in the sub-slab depressurization system Health Canada describes (a pipe through the slab, a fan and a discharge outside), sealing the sump isn't optional. An open pit lets house air get sucked straight back down into the pipe, and the fan ends up pulling from your basement instead of from under the slab. A newer Health Canada factsheet frames sealing cracks, gaps around pipes and other entry points as something that should be done "to maximize the efficacy of a ventilation system". Sealing supports the system. It isn't sold as the system.

You can read the guide yourself: [Health Canada, Radon Reduction Guide for Canadians (PDF)](https://www.canada.ca/content/dam/hc-sc/migration/hc-sc/ewh-semt/alt_formats/pdf/pubs/radiation/radon_canadians-canadiens/radon_canadians-canadien-eng.pdf).

## Three Prairie basements, three different answers

These are illustrative situations, not customer stories. They show how the sump question plays out depending on what you already know.

**An older St. Vital bungalow with a basement bedroom.** The sump sits in the utility room under a piece of plywood, and nobody has ever tested. The right move here is a $70 long-term kit in the bedroom, well away from the pit, left for at least 91 days over the winter. Screwing down a better lid first is tempting, but it muddies the picture: you'd be testing a house you've already changed, with no baseline. If the result comes back over 200, a certified professional will look at whether the sump and its weeping tile make a good suction point. Winnipeg installers advertise systems from about $1,500 to $3,000. In Manitoba, the Manitoba Hydro Home Energy Efficiency Loan covers radon mitigation up to $5,000 over 5 years at 6.2% (O.A.C.), paid on the energy bill, as long as the contractor is C-NRPP certified.

**A Regina rec room where a library monitor read well over 200.** The Regina Public Library lends digital monitors for three weeks at a time, which is a good way to find out whether it's worth doing a proper test. But a three-week reading is screening, not a result. A long-term test comes next. If it confirms a high level, a Regina installer quotes $2,500 to $3,500 for a system, and a sealed sump is often part of it. If the household's income is below the provincial median, the Canadian Lung Association's Lungs Matter program offers up to $1,500. It needs a 90+ day test by a C-NRPP professional showing over 200 and a C-NRPP quote, it won't pay for work already done, and funding is limited. Lung Saskatchewan says radon mitigation counts as an eligible expense under the Saskatchewan Home Renovation Tax Credit; confirm that with CRA before you count on it.

**A Winnipeg house built after 2011 with a capped rough-in pipe.** Manitoba adopted the National Building Code radon rough-in on 1 April 2011. The code's approach calls for a sealed poly membrane, "all openings sealed including any sump pit," and a rough-in pipe, commonly 100 mm, below the basement floor. The catch is that "the depressurization rough-in does not actively reduce radon levels." It's a capped pipe until someone adds a fan. And a sump lid that was sealed at handover doesn't always stay that way. If a plumber swapped the pump and dropped a loose lid back on, that opening is back. Test first. If it's high, a certified professional can check the lid and activate the rough-in, which is often the simpler job. The [radon rough-in post](/blog/radon-rough-in-new-homes/) covers the details.

## Living with a sealed sump

A sealed sump still has to work as a sump. A few practical points:

- **Pump access.** Pumps fail and need replacing. Ask the professional how the lid comes off for service and how it gets resealed afterward. A lid that gets left loose after a pump swap is back to being an opening.
- **Floor drains.** If a floor drain feeds the sump, the trap in it is what stops air coming up that way. A dry trap is an open path. Pour water into floor drains you rarely use.
- **Don't block the drainage.** The cover has to let the pit keep collecting water from the weeping tile. That's why Health Canada's wording talks about capping the sump "so that it can continue to drain water."
- **Retest after any change.** Health Canada says to retest after mitigation, and it's worth doing after any work that touched the sump, the slab or the rough-in. A test is the only way to know.

Two things we won't suggest: opening windows, or running a fan in the basement, as a radon fix. Neither one is a mitigation method.

Can you seal a sump lid yourself? A careful homeowner can fit a gasketed, airtight cover and seal where the cord and discharge pipe pass through. That's a reasonable step. It isn't mitigation, though. Health Canada recommends a C-NRPP certified mitigation professional for the system itself, and you can check anyone's certification in the [C-NRPP professional directory](https://c-nrpp.ca/find-a-professional/). If you've already got a result over 200, Health Canada recommends fixing it within 1 year.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Will sealing my sump pit lower my radon level?

It closes one of the bigger openings, but no one can promise it'll bring a high reading under 200 on its own. Health Canada treats sealing sumps and cracks as something that makes a ventilation system work better, not as a fix by itself. The only way to know what it did is a long-term retest.

### Can the sump pit be used as part of a radon mitigation system?

Often, yes. Health Canada's reduction guide describes capping and sealing the sump so it keeps draining water and also serves as the suction point for the radon pipe. Whether your pit and weeping tile suit that depends on how the house was built, and a certified professional will judge that on site.

### Where should I put a radon detector if my basement has a sump?

In the lowest level you actually live in, in the breathing zone at least 30 cm above the floor and 30 cm from the walls. Keep it away from the sump, vents, windows and heat sources. A detector sitting beside an open pit won't tell you much about the rest of the room.

### Does a new house with a radon rough-in still need the sump sealed?

The National Building Code approach calls for all openings to be sealed, including any sump pit, as part of the rough-in. That seal can be disturbed later, for example when a pump is replaced. The rough-in pipe also does nothing until a fan is added, so a long-term test is still the first step in a newer home.
