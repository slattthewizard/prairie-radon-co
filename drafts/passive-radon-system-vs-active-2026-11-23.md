# Passive vs Active Radon Systems: What the Pipe Does

If your house is a newer build, there may be a capped plastic pipe sticking out of the basement floor, labelled for radon. People call it a radon system. Strictly speaking, it isn't one yet.

The difference between a passive setup and an active one comes down to a fan. This article explains what each does, what Health Canada says about them, and what that means for a home on the Prairies.

## What passive and active mean

Both approaches work by soil depressurization. A pipe goes through or under the basement floor into the gravel or soil beneath, and radon is drawn out of that layer and vented outside before it can be pulled into the house.

**Passive** means there is no fan. Air is meant to move up the pipe on its own. Health Canada's September 2026 factsheet says passive soil depressurization in new homes "can reduce radon levels by 40-90%". That is a wide range, and it is for new homes built with the system in mind.

**Active** means a fan is attached to the pipe. Per Health Canada, "a fan attached to the pipe runs 24/7, pulling radon out and venting it safely outdoors." In most homes, Health Canada says this reduces the radon level by more than 80%, and its 2026 factsheet says 90% or more.

So the real comparison isn't two competing products. A passive pipe is the groundwork, and an active system is the same pipe with a fan on it.

## The rough-in in Prairie new homes

The radon rough-in was added to the National Building Code in 2010. As the City of Regina summarises the 2020 code, section 9.13.4.2.(2) "requires that dwelling units and buildings with residential occupancy be provided with a rough-in system for subfloor depressurization." Most commonly that is a 100 mm pipe below the basement floor, sealed and labelled.

The code approach also calls for a granular layer, a well-sealed poly membrane, all openings sealed including any sump pit, and a properly located rough-in pipe.

Here's the part that surprises people. The City of Regina is blunt: "the depressurization rough-in does not actively reduce radon levels." A fan has to be added later.

Where does that put Manitoba and Saskatchewan?

- Manitoba adopted the National Building Code 2010 as the Manitoba Building Code on 1 April 2011. The 2020 national codes came into force in Manitoba on 1 January 2024.
- Saskatchewan's 2020 codes came into effect on 1 January 2024. We couldn't find the date Saskatchewan first adopted the 2010 rough-in.
- A newer National Building Code, the 2025 edition, introduced a requirement for a passive vertical radon stack in dwelling units where a floor assembly separates a conditioned space from the ground. We found no sign that either province has adopted the 2025 edition, so don't assume a new Prairie house has a full stack.

## Is the rough-in actually in your house?

Not always, and not always in a usable place. A 2013 Winnipeg Parade of Homes study by CARST and the Manitoba Home Builders' Association found rough-in pipes in all but one house, and "unfortunately almost half are in inaccessible locations." The Made-in-Manitoba Radon Action Plan, an advocacy document, says the rough-in is "still not being implemented correctly in all new builds as of March 2025."

Regina says it checks the NBC radon provisions at plan review, and that missing radon details may be noted as a deficiency. We found no radon bylaw beyond the building code in Winnipeg, Saskatoon, Brandon, Moose Jaw or Prince Albert.

If you want to know what your house has, look for a capped, labelled pipe coming out of the basement floor, and ask whoever built it. Our post on [radon rough-in in new homes](/blog/radon-rough-in-new-homes/) goes through what to check.

## What Health Canada recommends

Health Canada "recommends that all new homes include a passive soil depressurization (PSD) system." It also says, for any home, that when large reductions of 50 per cent or more are wanted, "active soil depressurization is almost always the recommended approach."

Put those together and the logic is simple. A passive pipe in a new house is groundwork and may lower radon some. If the test shows a high level, the fan turns that groundwork into a system that Health Canada expects to do the heavy lifting.

## Side by side

| | Passive (rough-in) | Active (with fan) |
|---|---|---|
| Fan | None | Yes, runs continuously |
| Health Canada reduction figure | 40-90% in new homes | More than 80%; 90% or more per the 2026 factsheet |
| Typical situation | New home built with the pipe | Any house with a high test result |
| Ongoing cost | None | Small electricity cost; fan replacement |

On the last row, Health Canada's 2014 estimate for a fan is $200 to $300 to replace, with a lifespan of five to ten or more years, and about $50 to $75 a year in electricity. Those are national 2014 figures, not current Prairie prices. Our [fan replacement page](/radon-fan-replacement/) covers what happens when the fan quits.

## Testing is what decides it

Neither the pipe nor the fan tells you your radon level. A long-term test does.

Health Canada's guide says to retest in a new home in the first heating season after occupancy, and again 2 years after the year of construction. After any radon mitigation system is installed, it says to test again. For an active system, it says to retest every 5 years.

The 2026 factsheet lays out the order after an active system goes in: a short-term test by a certified professional once it's switched on, a long-term test in the same location as the original during the first heating season, then regular checks. Our [guide to long-term and short-term tests](/blog/long-term-vs-short-term-radon-test/) explains why those two tests do different jobs.

If a retest after the rough-in is capped still shows a high number, Health Canada recommends corrective action within 1 year. The guideline is 200 Bq/m³. It's a guideline, and we found no Manitoba or Saskatchewan law requiring a homeowner to test or add a fan.

## Adding the fan

Turning a rough-in into an active system is a job for a C-NRPP certified mitigation professional. Health Canada recommends one, though certification is voluntary and no province regulates the trade. Health Canada says a system "can usually be installed in less than a day."

Where the fan goes matters in a cold climate. Health Canada notes that a fan and pipe in a garage or attic will cool in the colder months, "leading to condensation and possibly ice, which can damage the fan." Putting the fan indoors, with a shorter pipe discharging near ground level through the rim joist, reduces condensation.

Price-wise, Winnipeg installers advertise full systems from about $1,500 to $3,000, and a Regina installer quotes $2,500 to $3,500. Those are company advertisements. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does a radon rough-in pipe lower my radon level?

Not actively. The City of Regina says the depressurization rough-in does not actively reduce radon levels, and that a fan must be added. Health Canada gives 40-90% for passive soil depressurization in new homes, which is a wide range, so a test is the only way to know what yours is doing.

### Do I need a fan if my house has a passive pipe?

Only if a long-term test shows you need to lower the level. Health Canada recommends action within 1 year for results above 200 Bq/m³, and says active soil depressurization is almost always the recommended approach when large reductions are wanted.

### Does a new Prairie home have to include a passive radon stack?

We can't say that. The 2025 National Building Code introduced a passive vertical radon stack requirement, but we found no indication that Manitoba or Saskatchewan has adopted it. The earlier rough-in has been part of the code since 2010, and both provinces' 2020 codes took effect on 1 January 2024.

### How much more effective is an active system than a passive one?

Health Canada says active systems in most homes reduce radon by more than 80%, and its 2026 factsheet says 90% or more. For passive soil depressurization in new homes it gives 40-90%. The two ranges overlap, which is why a post-installation test matters more than the label.
