# Radon in Moose Jaw, Saskatchewan: What We Know and How to Test

Moose Jaw has less radon data than Regina or Saskatoon, and it's better to say so than to dress up thin numbers. There is one solid survey result for the area, from 2012, and then a gap. This article lays out what exists, what doesn't, and the practical route for a Moose Jaw homeowner: borrow a monitor from the library if you like, then run a proper long-term test.

## What the 2012 survey found near Moose Jaw

Health Canada's Cross-Canada Survey of Radon Concentrations in Homes ran long-term tests of at least three months through the 2009-10 and 2010-11 fall and winter seasons, in the lowest lived-in level of each home. The Moose Jaw area falls in the Five Hills health region. The numbers below are for that region, not for Moose Jaw city limits, and they use the 2012 boundaries.

| Area (2012 health region) | Homes tested | % above 200 Bq/m³ | % above 600 Bq/m³ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Five Hills Regional Health Authority (Moose Jaw area) | 118 | 12.7 | 0.8 |
| Regina Qu'Appelle Regional Health Authority | 94 | 25.5 | 4.2 |
| Saskatoon Regional Health Authority | 76 | 7.9 | 0.0 |
| Prince Albert Parkland Regional Health Authority | 108 | 7.4 | 0.0 |
| Saskatchewan total | 1,513 | 16.3 | 1.0 |

So roughly 1 in 8 tested homes in the Five Hills region came in above the guideline, a little under the provincial figure. That is the same survey with the same method, so the comparison between rows is fair. It's also old (the tests ran in 2009 to 2011) and it covers 118 homes. And one home in eight over the guideline is not a small number.

## What the 2024 survey doesn't tell us

The 2024 Cross-Canada Survey on Radon pooled 69,478 long-term test results. Its city tables include Regina and Saskatoon. They do not include Moose Jaw. We have no 2024 city figure for Moose Jaw, and we won't borrow one from somewhere else.

What the 2024 report does say about Saskatchewan as a whole is that the chance of a home being at or over 200 Bq/m³ is approximately 1 in 3 (an unweighted figure), with a geometric mean of 140.7 Bq/m³. Lung Saskatchewan has said around one in three Saskatchewan homes exceed the guideline, and a 2025 fact sheet from CARST and Lung Sask reports that 35% of Saskatchewan homes recently tested were above 200 Bq/m³. All of these come from volunteer samples. They tell you radon is common in the province. They don't tell you what's under your house.

The Government of Saskatchewan's own wording is blunter: "there is no safe level of radon." Health Canada's is a little more careful. Risk below the guideline is small, but no level is risk-free. The Canadian guideline is 200 Bq/m³ and the choice of what to do about it belongs to each homeowner.

The 2024 survey adds that there are no areas of Canada that are "radon-free", and Health Canada recommends testing every home regardless of location. For Moose Jaw, with thin local data, that's the sensible default.

## Borrow a monitor from the Moose Jaw Public Library

The Moose Jaw Public Library, part of Palliser Regional Library, lists radon detectors in its Library of Things. According to the library, the kits can be borrowed from 1 October to 1 April, for free with a library card. That window sits inside the heating season, which is when you want to be measuring.

Lung Saskatchewan says it supports eight libraries and library regions with radon lending programs, so this is part of a wider effort.

Be clear about what a borrowed monitor does. It's a screening tool: a way to get a first impression and decide whether a proper test is urgent. Health Canada says short-term measurements are not appropriate for deciding on mitigation, because the guideline applies to your average annual level. A low monitor reading doesn't clear you, and a high one doesn't mean you must fix anything tomorrow. Both lead to the same next step.

## Run the long-term test

That next step is a long-term test. Health Canada recommends 3 to 12 months, with a minimum of 91 days of the test inside the heating season.

- **Where:** the lowest level of the home where someone spends more than 4 hours a day. A finished basement, a basement bedroom or a basement suite counts. A furnace room or an unfinished cold room doesn't.
- **How high:** in the breathing zone, at least 30 cm (1 ft) above the floor and at least 30 cm from walls and ceilings.
- **Away from:** heat sources, direct sunlight, vents, doors, windows, sump holes and exposed foundation cracks.
- **Cost:** HomeRadonTest.ca, the ordering site for both provincial lung associations, charges $70.00 per 90-day kit before tax. That includes shipping both ways and analysis at the Saskatchewan Research Council. Retail kits generally run $50 to $100, according to the Manitoba government.

## If the result is above 200

Health Canada recommends corrective action within 1 year when a long-term test is above 200 Bq/m³, sooner the higher the number. The usual fix is sub-slab depressurization: a pipe through the floor, a fan that runs continuously, and a vent to the outdoors. Health Canada says it reduces radon levels by more than 80% in most homes, and its 2026 factsheet says 90% or more.

## Money that applies in Saskatchewan

- **Saskatchewan Home Renovation Tax Credit.** The province's page describes a 10.5 per cent credit on up to $4,000 of eligible home renovation expenses, worth up to $420 a year, or up to $525 for seniors on $5,000 of expenses. Only costs above a $1,000 base amount count, and the home must be your principal residence in Saskatchewan. The official page doesn't name radon. Lung Saskatchewan says radon mitigation counts as an eligible expense; confirm with CRA. Our post on the [Saskatchewan tax credit](/blog/radon-mitigation-saskatchewan-tax-credit/) has the detail.
- **Lung Saskatchewan reimbursement.** The Caring Breaths program has offered radon mitigation reimbursement in the past and is currently paused. Check Lung Saskatchewan's site for when it reopens.
- **Lungs Matter (Canadian Lung Association).** Up to $1,500 toward mitigation for low or moderate income households, open to Saskatchewan residents. It needs a Notice of Assessment showing income below the provincial median, a 90-day C-NRPP kit result above 200 Bq/m³ and a quote from a C-NRPP certified professional. It won't cover work already done, and funding is limited.

No Saskatchewan law requires a homeowner to test or mitigate. That's part of why these programs exist: nobody is going to make you, so the incentives matter.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How common is radon in Moose Jaw?

The best figure we have is from the 2012 Health Canada survey: 12.7% of 118 tested homes in the Five Hills health region, which covers the Moose Jaw area, were above 200 Bq/m³. Moose Jaw isn't in the 2024 city tables. Province-wide, newer volunteer-sample data suggests about 1 in 3 Saskatchewan homes are over the guideline, so a test is the only way to know about your own.

### Does the Moose Jaw library lend radon detectors?

Yes. The Moose Jaw Public Library lists radon detectors in its Library of Things, available from 1 October to 1 April and free with a library card. Treat the monitor as a screening tool and follow up with a long-term test of at least 91 days.

### How much does radon mitigation cost in Moose Jaw?

We couldn't find a published Moose Jaw price. For scale, a Regina installer quotes $2,500 to $3,500 and Winnipeg installers advertise about $1,500 to $3,000, but those are other cities. Ask a C-NRPP certified professional for a written quote on your house.

### Is radon testing required by law in Saskatchewan?

We found no Saskatchewan or Manitoba law that requires a homeowner to test for radon or reduce a high level. Health Canada's guideline of 200 Bq/m³ is a recommendation, and it advises testing every home.

### Can I claim radon mitigation on my Saskatchewan taxes?

Lung Saskatchewan says radon mitigation counts as an eligible expense under the Saskatchewan Home Renovation Tax Credit. The provincial page doesn't list radon by name, so confirm with CRA before you rely on it.
