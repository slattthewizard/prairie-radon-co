# Long-Term Radon Test vs Short-Term: Which One to Trust

You borrow a radon monitor from the library, leave it in the basement for a week, and it reads under 200. Most people cross radon off the list right there. The trouble is that a week of readings can't tell you the one number Health Canada's guideline is written around, and only a long term radon test can.

This isn't a knock on short tests. They're free or cheap, they're quick, and they get people thinking about radon. But they answer a different question from the one you actually need answered before you spend money, or decide not to.

Here's how the two kinds of test differ, what each one is good for, and which one to trust when the result matters.

## Why a long term radon test is the one Health Canada counts

Radon is measured in becquerels per cubic metre, written Bq/m3. (If you're reading American material, they use picocuries per litre: 37 Bq/m3 is 1 pCi/L, so the Canadian guideline of 200 Bq/m3 is about 5.4 pCi/L.)

The 200 Bq/m3 figure is a guideline, not a law. No Manitoba or Saskatchewan law requires a homeowner to test or fix anything. And the guideline applies to your average annual radon level in the lived-in part of the house, not to any single week or month.

That's the whole reason long tests exist. Health Canada's measurement guide says short-term measurements "are not appropriate for informing mitigation decisions," and it sets the minimum test length at 91 days. Its advice is a test of at least three months, with at least 91 of those days falling in the heating season, and ideally a test that runs 3 to 12 months. If your test ran shorter than 91 days, Health Canada recommends doing a new one.

Radon levels in a house move around with the weather, the season and how the house is being heated and aired out. A few days or a few weeks catches one slice of that. Three months across a Prairie winter gets you much closer to the yearly average the guideline cares about.

Why bother at all? Health Canada puts it plainly: radon is the number one cause of lung cancer in non-smokers and is estimated to cause more than 3,000 lung cancer deaths in Canada each year ([Health Canada, Radon: What you need to know](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/environmental-workplace-health/reports-publications/radon-what-you-need-to-know.html)). For smokers it's the second leading cause. That's the case for testing. It's also the case for testing properly, once, rather than guessing.

## What short-term tests and library monitors are good for

Short tests aren't useless. They're screening tools, and Health Canada and the lung associations describe them that way.

The free library programmes are a good example. Winnipeg Public Library lends radon screening kits from every branch between 1 November and 30 April, on a three-week loan with no renewals. The monitor is meant to sit at the lowest level of the home for at least seven days. Regina Public Library has 30 kits on three-week loans, also with no renewals, for Regina residents. Moose Jaw Public Library lends kits from 1 October to 1 April, free with a library card. Western Manitoba Regional Library in Brandon is part of the Manitoba lending programme, and Saskatoon libraries have lent detectors too. Our guide to [borrowing a radon detector from the library](/blog/borrow-radon-detector-library/) has the details.

The Manitoba Lung Association describes the library programme as an affordable way to "screen" a home and says Health Canada recommends a long-term three-month kit. That's the honest framing. A borrowed monitor can tell you radon is present and give you a rough sense of scale. It can't tell you your annual average.

Short tests have two other legitimate jobs:

- During a house sale. C-NRPP (the Canadian National Radon Proficiency Program) says a short-term screening "could be conducted to provide some insight" when there's no time for anything longer, with a long-term test after you move in.
- Right after a mitigation system goes in. Health Canada's current advice is for a certified professional to do a short-term test once the system is switched on, to confirm it's bringing the level down, and then a long-term test in the first heating season after the install.

Notice that in both cases the short test is a stopgap. The long test still follows.

## Three Prairie situations, and which test fits

These are made-up but typical examples. The figures come from Health Canada, the lung associations and what local installers advertise.

**A 1962 bungalow in St. Vital with a finished basement bedroom.** The family borrows a Winnipeg library monitor in January, runs it for the three-week loan, and it shows a figure under 200 Bq/m3. Tempting to stop there. But a teenager sleeps down there, and three weeks is well short of 91 days. For about $70 they can put a long-term kit in that bedroom, at breathing height, and have a result that actually lines up with the guideline by spring. If it comes back low, they've got a real answer. If it doesn't, they've found out before another winter goes by. For context, 37.4% of the Winnipeg homes tested in the 2024 Cross-Canada Survey were at or above 200.

**A library monitor reading 480 Bq/m3 in a Regina rec room.** Now the instinct runs the other way: call an installer tomorrow. A Regina installer quotes $2,500 to $3,500 for a system, and more for crawl spaces or multiple suction points. Before booking, it's worth knowing that the Canadian Lung Association's Lungs Matter programme, which offers up to $1,500 to low- or moderate-income households (income below the provincial median), needs a long-term test of at least 90 days from a C-NRPP approved kit showing a result over 200. It also doesn't pay for work that's already done, and funding is limited. Skip the long test and you may skip the grant. A reading of 480 is a strong reason to start that test now rather than wait. For what it's worth, 46.6% of the Regina homes tested in the 2024 survey were at or above 200, so a high screening result here isn't unusual.

**A Saskatoon two-storey sold with a radon condition in the offer.** There's no way to fit 91 days into a normal closing timeline. This is exactly where C-NRPP's short-term screening advice applies, followed by a long-term test once the buyers have moved in. Saskatoon had the lowest numbers of the four Prairie cities in the 2024 survey, roughly 1 in 6 tested homes (17.8%), but that still says nothing about this particular house. How the condition, any holdback or disclosure is worded is a question for your realtor or lawyer, not for a radon blog. ## How to run a long-term radon test properly

A long test is only as good as its setup. Health Canada's placement rules are simple:

- Test in a normal occupancy area on the lowest lived-in level. That means a room someone spends more than four hours a day in: a basement bedroom, rec room or basement suite. Not the furnace room, the laundry room, an unfinished basement or a crawl space.
- Put the detector in the breathing zone, at least 30 cm above the floor and at least 30 cm from walls and ceilings.
- Keep it away from heat sources, direct sun, vents, doors, windows, sump holes and open cracks in the foundation.
- Leave it for at least three months, with at least 91 days in the heating season. Manitoba's government suggests testing ideally in winter, October to April, which on the Prairies is most of the year anyway.

Late September is a good time to order a kit. Start it in October or November and you'll have a heating-season result by late winter. Our guide on [where to place a radon detector](/blog/where-to-place-radon-detector/) walks through the room choice in more detail.

Retest later if things change. Health Canada recommends a new long-term test after energy retrofits (new windows, air sealing and so on), when a floor that wasn't tested becomes the lowest lived-in level, and after any radon mitigation system is installed. Homes with an active system should be retested every five years.

## What to do with the number

If your long-term result is under 200 Bq/m3, you've done what Health Canada asks. It also says there is "no level that is considered risk free," so some people with results in the upper 100s choose to act anyway. That's a judgement call, and a reasonable one either way.

If it's over 200, Health Canada recommends fixing it within one year. The usual fix is sub-slab depressurization: a pipe through the basement slab, a fan, and a discharge outside, with any sump pit sealed. Health Canada recommends a C-NRPP certified mitigation professional for the job. Certification is voluntary in Canada and no province regulates radon contractors, so check the name in C-NRPP's online directory before you sign anything.

On cost, Winnipeg installers advertise systems from about $1,500 to $3,000; a Regina installer quotes $2,500 to $3,500. Every house is different, so treat those as advertised ranges, not quotes. There's no advertised price we could find for Saskatoon, Brandon, Moose Jaw or Prince Albert. Our [radon mitigation page](/radon-mitigation/) explains how the work goes.

Money help depends on where you live. The Canadian Lung Association's Lungs Matter programme is open in both provinces, and in Manitoba the Manitoba Lung Association runs it. Manitoba homeowners also have the Manitoba Hydro Home Energy Efficiency Loan covers radon mitigation up to $5,000 over five years at 6.2% (O.A.C.), with a C-NRPP certified contractor. In Saskatchewan, Lung Saskatchewan has offered reimbursement in the past; check its site. Lung Saskatchewan also says radon mitigation counts as an eligible expense for the provincial Home Renovation Tax Credit; confirm with CRA. There is currently no federal radon tax credit that we could find.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long does a long term radon test take?

Health Canada's minimum is 91 days, with at least 91 days falling in the heating season. It recommends 3 to 12 months for the best picture of your annual average. A test that runs shorter than 91 days should be repeated.

### Can I trust a library radon monitor?

You can trust it as a screening tool, which is how the libraries and lung associations describe it. It shows whether radon is present and roughly how much over a few weeks. It can't tell you your annual average, and it won't qualify you for the Lungs Matter grant.

### Is a digital radon monitor as good as a long-term kit?

It can be, if you run it for at least 91 days and the model has passed C-NRPP's performance testing for long-term home measurements. Health Canada asks buyers to check for that. Short readings from any digital monitor are still screening only.

### My short-term test was over 200. Do I need mitigation?

Not necessarily, but take it seriously. Health Canada says short-term results shouldn't be used to decide on mitigation, so start a long-term test in the lowest lived-in room now. If that result is over 200, Health Canada recommends fixing it within a year.
