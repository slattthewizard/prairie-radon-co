# What Radon Levels Need Fixing? The 200 Bq/m3 Guideline Explained

You have a number. Maybe it came off a library monitor after three weeks in the basement, maybe it came back from a lab, and now you're trying to work out whether it means a $70 retest or a $3,000 mitigation system. This article covers which radon levels Health Canada says need fixing, how that number is supposed to be measured, and what the fix costs in Manitoba and Saskatchewan.

The short version: the Canadian guideline is 200 Bq/m3, measured as a long-term average in the part of the house you actually live in. Above that, Health Canada recommends fixing it. Below that, nobody can make you do anything, but nobody will call it zero risk either.

## What 200 Bq/m3 actually means

Bq/m3 stands for becquerels per cubic metre, a count of radioactive decays per second in a cubic metre of air. American sites use picocuries per litre (pCi/L) instead. Health Canada puts 37 Bq/m3 at 1 pCi/L, so 200 Bq/m3 is about 5.4 pCi/L. If you've been reading US forums and their numbers look oddly small, that's why.

The [Government of Canada radon guideline](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/health-risks-safety/radiation/radon/government-canada-radon-guideline.html) sets 200 Bq/m3 as the average annual level in the normal occupancy area. Most of the confusion about radon levels comes from skipping over the words in that sentence.

"Average annual" means one cold week isn't the number the guideline is written against. Radon rises and falls with the weather, the season and how the house is run, so a snapshot can land well above or below the yearly average.

"Normal occupancy area" means a space someone uses for 4 or more hours a day, like a finished basement with a bedroom or rec room in it. It does not mean the crawl space, an unfinished basement, the furnace room or the laundry room. A high reading beside the furnace is worth knowing about, but it isn't the guideline number.

And "guideline" means advice, not law. No Manitoba or Saskatchewan law requires a homeowner to test or to mitigate. The City of Regina says it plainly: "there is no legislated maximum level or enforceable level."

When a proper long-term result is above 200, Health Canada recommends corrective action "within 1 year." Below 200, Health Canada says there is "no level that is considered risk free." So a result of 150 isn't a pass, exactly. It's under the line where Health Canada recommends action, and what you do with it is your call.

Why test at all? Health Canada's position is that radon is the number one cause of lung cancer in people who have never smoked, and the second leading cause overall after smoking. It estimates that 16% of lung cancers come from radon exposure, which works out to more than 3,000 deaths in Canada each year. That's the health case. It doesn't need dressing up.

## Where your radon level is measured changes the answer

Radon is a soil gas, and it gets into a house through the parts that touch the ground. That's why Health Canada wants the test in the lowest lived-in level: the lowest floor where someone spends 4 or more hours a day. In most Prairie houses that's the basement, if the basement has a bedroom, a suite, an office or a TV room in it.

Health Canada's recipe for a long-term test is specific:

- Run it for at least 3 months. The minimum is 91 days, during the heating season, and 3 to 12 months is ideal.
- Put the detector in the breathing zone, at least 30 cm above the floor and 30 cm from any wall.
- Keep it away from sump pits, vents, windows and heat sources.
- If the test ran for less than 91 days, repeat it.

Short-term tests and digital monitors are screening tools. They're good for telling you whether to take the question seriously, and not good for deciding whether to spend a few thousand dollars. If you want to use a digital monitor as your long-term measurement, it should be one on C-NRPP's approved list. There's more on the difference in [long-term vs short-term radon tests](/blog/long-term-vs-short-term-radon-test/), and on placement in [where to put a radon detector](/blog/where-to-place-radon-detector/).

The number also isn't fixed for the life of the house. Health Canada says to retest after a mitigation system goes in, after energy retrofits, and when a new floor becomes the lowest lived-in level. Finishing an old storage basement into a kids' bedroom counts. So does a new set of windows and a round of air sealing.

## How common high radon levels are on the Prairies

The 2024 Cross-Canada Survey, run by Evict Radon with Health Canada, found that 17.8% of the homes tested nationally were at or above 200 Bq/m3. That's about 1 in 5. For Saskatchewan and Manitoba, the survey puts it at "approximately 1 in 3."

The city rows are worth reading carefully. Of the Regina homes in the 2024 survey, 46.6% were at or above 200. Of the Winnipeg homes tested, 37.4% were. Of the Saskatoon homes tested, 17.8% were, which is roughly 1 in 6 tested homes and the lowest of the four Prairie cities in the tables. Across the Prairies, rural homes came in higher than urban ones: 26.7% against 15.9%.

These are unweighted convenience samples. They describe the homes that were tested, not every house on your street, and they certainly don't describe yours. The survey's own line is the useful one: "There are no areas of Canada that are 'radon-free'." A low-looking city number doesn't let a Saskatoon basement off the hook, and a high Regina number doesn't mean your Regina basement is high. You find out by testing.

## Three Prairie results and what each one means

The same guideline plays out very differently depending on what kind of number you have and what kind of house it came from. Here are three common situations, with names and addresses left out.

### A library monitor reading 480 Bq/m3 in a Regina rec room

The Regina Public Library lends digital screening monitors on three-week loans. Say one sits in a finished rec room and reads 480 Bq/m3 at the end of the loan. That's well over 200, and it's reasonable to be uneasy about it. It's also a screening number from a few weeks, not an annual average.

The next step is a long-term test in the same room, set up by the book: 91 days or more in the heating season, 30 cm off the floor, away from the sump and the furnace. HomeRadonTest.ca sells a kit for $70.00 before tax, and the Manitoba government says kits cost "between $50 and $100." There's a practical reason to do this properly even if you're already sure you'll mitigate. The Lungs Matter grant (more below) requires a test of 90 days or more by a C-NRPP professional showing a result above 200.

### A 1962 bungalow in St. Vital with a basement bedroom

Here the long-term test is done, in the basement bedroom, and the average comes back above 200. Health Canada's advice is corrective action within a year. The usual fix is sub-slab depressurization (also called active soil depressurization): a pipe through the basement slab, a fan, and a discharge outside, so the soil gas under the floor gets pulled away before it comes inside. Health Canada recommends a C-NRPP certified mitigation professional for the job. Any sump pit has to be sealed as part of it.

Winnipeg installers advertise systems from about $1,500 to $3,000. Where the final number lands depends on the house: how the slab is laid out, how many suction points it needs, and where the pipe can run without wrecking a finished room. Nobody can give you a firm price for an older bungalow without looking at it. Once the system is in, you test again to confirm the number came down.

### A 2015 Winnipeg build with a capped rough-in pipe

Manitoba adopted the radon rough-in requirement on 1 April 2011, so a 2015 house should have one. The current code calls for a subfloor depressurization rough-in, commonly a 100 mm pipe below the basement floor, sealed and labelled. The catch is in the code itself: "the depressurization rough-in does not actively reduce radon levels." A capped pipe does nothing on its own. A fan has to be added before it works as a system.

So the rough-in is a head start, not a fix, and it doesn't replace a test. If the long-term result comes back over 200, a certified mitigation professional can quote on activating it. Don't assume yours is easy to reach, either: a 2013 study of Winnipeg Parade of Homes houses found rough-ins in all but one house, but "almost half are in inaccessible locations." Help with the bill depends on the province. It needs the 90-day C-NRPP test above 200 and a C-NRPP quote, it won't pay for work already done, and funding is limited. Manitoba Hydro's Home Energy Efficiency Loan covers radon mitigation up to $5,000 over 5 years at 6.2% (O.A.C.), paid on the energy bill, with no income test, as long as the contractor is C-NRPP certified.

In Saskatchewan, Lung Saskatchewan has offered reimbursement in the past; check its site for the current status. Lung Saskatchewan also says radon mitigation counts as an eligible expense under the provincial Home Renovation Tax Credit. Confirm that with CRA before you count on it. There is currently no federal radon tax credit that we could find.

A note on certification, because it matters for both the loan and the grant. C-NRPP, the Canadian National Radon Proficiency Program, is voluntary: no province regulates radon professionals. Measurement and mitigation are separate certifications, and you can check any professional's name in the [C-NRPP directory](https://c-nrpp.ca/find-a-professional/) before you sign anything.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is 200 Bq/m3 a legal limit in Manitoba or Saskatchewan?

No. It's a Health Canada guideline, and no Manitoba or Saskatchewan law requires a homeowner to test or mitigate. Health Canada recommends corrective action within 1 year when the long-term average in a lived-in area is above 200. Whether you act is up to you.

### My long-term result is 150 Bq/m3. Do I need to fix it?

Health Canada's advice to act within a year applies above 200, but it also says there is no level considered risk free. Some people below the guideline choose to mitigate anyway, especially when a bedroom is in the basement. Retest after any big change to the house, since a retrofit or a newly finished basement can shift the number.

### Can I decide based on a digital monitor or a short test?

Use them to decide whether to run a proper test, not whether to buy a system. Health Canada treats short-term tests and digital monitors as screening. A long-term test of at least 91 days in the heating season is what the guideline is built around, and it's what grant programs ask for.

### Do I need to test again after a system is installed?

Yes. Health Canada says to retest after mitigation to confirm the level came down. It's also worth retesting after energy retrofits and whenever a new floor becomes the lowest lived-in level of the house.
