# Borrowing a Library Radon Detector in Winnipeg, Regina and Beyond

You can find out whether your basement has a radon problem this winter without spending a dollar. A library radon detector costs nothing to borrow in Winnipeg, Regina, Moose Jaw and a string of other Prairie libraries, and it will give you a number within a week or two. The catch is what that number means. It's a screening reading, not the result Health Canada's guideline is built on, and plenty of people either panic over it or relax too early.

## Why libraries lend radon detectors here

Radon is a radioactive gas that comes out of the soil and collects in basements. You can't see it or smell it. Health Canada says radon is the number one cause of lung cancer in non-smokers and estimates it's behind more than 3,000 lung cancer deaths in Canada each year ([Health Canada, testing your home for radon](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/health-risks-safety/radiation/radon/testing-your-home.html)). That's the health case, and it's the only time we'll make it.

The Prairies have a lot of it. In the 2024 Cross-Canada Survey, roughly 1 in 3 of the Saskatchewan and Manitoba homes tested were at or above 200 Bq/m3, compared with about 1 in 5 nationally. Among the homes tested in individual cities, 46.6% of the Regina homes and 37.4% of the Winnipeg homes came in at or above that line. Saskatoon's tested homes ran lower, roughly 1 in 6. None of those are random samples of every house in town, and no part of Canada is radon-free.

Bq/m3 means becquerels per cubic metre, a measure of radioactivity in the air. Health Canada's guideline is 200 Bq/m3 as an average over a year in the part of the home people use. If you've seen US figures in pCi/L, 37 Bq/m3 is 1 pCi/L, so 200 works out to about 5.4 pCi/L.

The guideline is a guideline. No Manitoba or Saskatchewan law requires a homeowner to test or fix anything. The library programmes exist because lung associations and libraries decided a free, low-effort first look would get more people to test at all. Health Canada's own view, repeated by the Manitoba Lung Association, is that the library loan is for screening and a long-term test of three months or more is the real measurement.

No result yet? Test first. The library is a sensible place to start, and if you'd rather talk it through, call [(204) 555-0173](tel:+12045550173) or use the [contact form](/#contact). If you already have a high long-term result, the same number puts you in touch with a certified radon professional.

## Where to borrow a library radon detector on the Prairies

Terms differ by library, and some of them we couldn't confirm. Here's what the libraries themselves have published.

**Winnipeg Public Library.** Every branch lends radon screening kits, free, the same way you'd borrow a book. You need a library card in good standing. The loan is three weeks with no renewals, and the kits are only out between 1 November and 30 April. The kit is a small electronic monitor plus a booklet, and the monitor is meant to sit in a room on the lowest level of the house for at least seven days. The City of Winnipeg's radon screening kits page has the current details.

**Elsewhere in Manitoba.** The Manitoba Lung Association runs the programme with libraries outside Winnipeg too; CBC reported it had spread to more than 60 branches. In the Brandon area, Western Manitoba Regional Library is a lending partner. We couldn't find its loan length or season published, so ask at the desk.

**Regina Public Library.** Regina's library lends a digital radon detector with an instruction guide, free with a library card. You must be a Regina resident. The loan is three weeks with no renewals, and the library has said it has 30 kits, so expect a hold list in the cold months. The kit has included a coupon toward a long-term analog test kit, which is exactly the next step we'd suggest anyway.

**Moose Jaw Public Library.** Radon kits are part of the library's lending collection from 1 October to 1 April, free with your library card.

**Saskatoon.** Saskatoon Public Library has lent radon detectors, launched in 2023 with Lung Saskatchewan's support, and Library of Things YXE is listed as another source. We couldn't verify the current season or loan length, so check with the library before you plan around it.

**Prince Albert and smaller towns.** We didn't find a Prince Albert lending programme. Lung Saskatchewan says it supports eight libraries and library regions in the province, and Take Action on Radon keeps a [list of lending programmes](https://takeactiononradon.ca/resources/lending-programs/) worth checking for your town.

Almost all of these run through the heating season on purpose: windows are shut and the furnace is running, which is when a basement reading tells you most.

## How to set up a borrowed radon detector so the reading counts

Three weeks goes fast, and a badly placed monitor wastes the loan. Health Canada's placement rules for long-term tests apply just as well to a screening monitor:

- Put it on the lowest level someone uses for four or more hours a day. A finished basement, a basement bedroom, a rec room, a basement suite.
- Keep it in the breathing zone, at least 30 cm above the floor and 30 cm from any wall.
- Keep it away from the sump pit, floor drains, heat vents, windows, exterior doors and direct heat.
- Don't put it in a crawl space, the furnace room, the laundry room or an unfinished storage corner. Those aren't where anyone lives, and the guideline doesn't apply there.
- Leave it running and leave it alone for at least seven days. Longer is better, and you have three weeks.
- Live normally. Don't open the windows to "air it out" first, and don't run a fan you wouldn't normally run.

Write down the reading at the end, and if the monitor shows a longer-period average as well as a recent one, write both. A short-term number bounces around with weather, wind and how hard the furnace is working, so a single glance on one afternoon is close to meaningless.

There's more detail on room choice in our guide on [where to place a radon detector](/blog/where-to-place-radon-detector/).

## What the reading can and can't tell you

A library monitor is good at one question: is there enough radon down there that I should take this seriously? It's not built to answer "should I install a mitigation system?" Health Canada is blunt about it: short-term measurements aren't appropriate for mitigation decisions, and the 200 Bq/m3 guideline applies to your average annual level. The minimum test length is 91 days, and it should fall in the heating season. If a test ran shorter than 91 days, Health Canada says to repeat it.

Here's the practical reading of what a screening monitor tells you:

| Option | Cost (CAD) | How long it runs | What it tells you | Counts toward the guideline or grant paperwork? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Library radon detector | Free with a library card | At least 7 days, 3-week loan | A screening snapshot of one room | No |
| Long-term test kit (lab-analysed) | About $70 per kit before tax from HomeRadonTest.ca, the provincial lung associations' supplier | 91 days minimum, 3-12 months ideal | Your average level, the number the 200 Bq/m3 guideline is written for | Yes, if it's a C-NRPP approved long-term test |
| Mitigation system, if the long-term result is high | Winnipeg installers advertise systems from about $1,500 to $3,000; a Regina installer quotes $2,500 to $3,500 | Installed, then retested | Whether the fix worked (retest required) | Grants and loans have their own rules, below |

That last column matters more than it looks. The Canadian Lung Association's Lungs Matter grant, worth up to $1,500 for low- and moderate-income households, needs a long-term test of at least 90 days from a C-NRPP approved kit showing more than 200 Bq/m3. Homeowner digital monitors don't qualify. So even a frightening library reading won't open that door on its own.

The long-term kit is the step we'd point almost everyone to, whatever the library monitor showed. The trade-offs between the two are laid out in [radon test kit vs digital monitor](/blog/radon-test-kit-vs-digital-monitor/) and [long-term vs short-term radon tests](/blog/long-term-vs-short-term-radon-test/).

## Three library readings and what happened next

These are composite situations, not real households, but the numbers behave the way library readings do.

**A 1962 bungalow in St. Vital with a finished basement bedroom.** The family borrowed a kit from their Winnipeg branch in November and left it by the bed for ten days. It settled around 330 Bq/m3. The instinct was to call an installer that week. They did the sensible thing instead and ordered a long-term kit, about $70 before tax, and set it in the same spot for three months over the winter. If that result comes back over 200, Health Canada recommends fixing it within a year, which leaves time to get two quotes. Winnipeg installers advertise systems from about $1,500 to $3,000, which gives them a yardstick for whatever a certified mitigation professional quotes. If the long-term number comes in lower, they've saved themselves the cost of a system they didn't need.

**A Regina rec room reading 140 Bq/m3.** Under 200, so a Regina couple nearly handed the kit back and forgot about it. The trouble is that a two-week reading in a mild stretch of weather can sit well below the annual average, and Health Canada says there's no level of radon considered risk-free anyway. They used the coupon in the kit toward a long-term test. Cheap insurance against a false all-clear, and a rec room where the kids spend their evenings is exactly the space the guideline covers.

**A Moose Jaw bungalow where the monitor went in the furnace room.** The family put the monitor beside the furnace because "that's where the basement stuff is". It read high. But nobody spends four hours a day in a furnace room, and the reading next to a hot appliance and floor drain doesn't represent the space where they actually live. The fix cost nothing: borrow it again in the same October-to-April season, set it up in the basement family room, 30 cm off the floor, and then follow up with a long-term test. The Five Hills health region around Moose Jaw came in lower than Regina's in Health Canada's 2012 survey, but a regional average says nothing about one house.

If your long-term test is already back and it's over 200, call [(204) 555-0173](tel:+12045550173) or use the [contact form](/#contact). We'll put you in touch with a C-NRPP certified mitigation professional who can look at your foundation and quote the job.

## After the library loan: the long-term test, then decide

If you've returned the library radon detector and you're not sure what to do, the order is simple.

**Order a long-term test.** Place it where the library monitor sat, provided that spot followed the rules above. Leave it for at least 91 days in the heating season, then mail it to the lab. The kits sold through the lung associations are analysed at the Saskatchewan Research Council lab.

**Read the result against 200 Bq/m3.** Below 200, you're under the guideline; Health Canada still says no level is risk-free, so it's your call whether to do more. Above 200, Health Canada recommends corrective action within a year. Our [radon testing page](/radon-testing/) goes through what the numbers mean in more detail.

**If it's high, get quotes from certified professionals.** The usual fix is sub-slab depressurization: a pipe through the basement slab, a fan, and a discharge outside. Health Canada recommends a C-NRPP certified mitigation professional do it, and you should retest after it's installed. Crawl spaces, several suction points or an awkward layout can push the price up, which is why nobody can give you an honest number over the phone before seeing the house. See [how radon mitigation works](/radon-mitigation/) for the details.

**Look at the money help before you sign.** In Manitoba, the Manitoba Lung Association runs Lungs Matter, and Manitoba Hydro's Home Energy Efficiency Loan covers radon mitigation up to $5,000 over 5 years at 6.2% (O.A.C.), with a C-NRPP certified contractor required. In Saskatchewan, Lung Saskatchewan says radon mitigation counts as an eligible expense under the provincial Home Renovation Tax Credit; confirm that with CRA before you count on it. Lung Saskatchewan has offered reimbursement in the past, so check its site for the current status. There is currently no federal radon tax credit that we could find. Lungs Matter won't pay for work already done, so apply before the installer starts.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is a library radon detector accurate enough to decide on mitigation?

No. It's a screening tool, and Health Canada says short-term readings aren't appropriate for mitigation decisions. Use it to decide whether to run a long-term test of at least 91 days, then decide on mitigation from that result.

### When can I borrow a radon detector from the library?

Mostly in the heating season. Winnipeg Public Library lends kits from 1 November to 30 April, and Moose Jaw Public Library from 1 October to 1 April. Regina Public Library lends them to Regina residents for three weeks with no renewals; check the library for current availability, and ask your local branch elsewhere.

### My library reading was under 200 Bq/m3. Am I done?

Not necessarily. A week or two of readings can land well below your annual average, and Health Canada says no radon level is considered risk-free. A long-term test, about $70 before tax from HomeRadonTest.ca, is the way to confirm it.

### Can I use a library radon reading to apply for a grant?

No. The Lungs Matter grant needs a long-term test of at least 90 days from a C-NRPP approved kit showing more than 200 Bq/m3, and homeowner digital monitors don't qualify. The Manitoba Hydro loan also requires a C-NRPP certified contractor for the work.
