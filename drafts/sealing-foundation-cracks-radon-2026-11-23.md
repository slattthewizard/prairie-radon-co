# Sealing Foundation Cracks for Radon: What It Can and Can't Do

A crack in a basement floor looks like the obvious problem. You find one, you fill it, you feel like you've done something about radon.

Sometimes that feeling is earned. Mostly it isn't, and Health Canada is direct about why. In its Radon Reduction Guide for Canadians, sealing alone "is not a standalone technique for reducing radon levels." This article covers where radon actually gets in, what sealing is good for, and what a test will tell you that a caulk gun can't.

## Where radon gets into a house

Radon is a radioactive gas that comes from the soil. You can't see it, smell it or taste it. According to Health Canada, it gets inside because "the air pressure inside your home is usually lower than in the soil surrounding the foundation." The house acts like a weak vacuum, and soil gas takes whatever route is open.

Health Canada's list of entry points is longer than most people expect:

- cracks in the foundation floor and walls
- construction joints
- gaps around service pipes
- support posts
- window casements
- floor drains
- sumps
- cavities inside walls

Look at that list again. Cracks are on it, but so are joints, drains and sumps. The floor meets the walls at a joint, and pipes, posts and drains pass through it. Fill the one crack you can see and the others are still there.

For the full picture, the post on [how radon gets into your house](/blog/how-radon-gets-into-your-house/) walks through each route.

## What Health Canada says about sealing

Two points from Health Canada's own material are worth keeping straight.

The first is the one already quoted: sealing "is not a standalone technique for reducing radon levels." That's from the May 2014 Radon Reduction Guide.

The second is that sealing still has a job. Health Canada's September 2026 factsheet says that "cracks, gaps around pipes and other potential radon entry points should be sealed to maximize the efficacy of a ventilation system." So sealing is part of a proper job. It supports a system. It doesn't replace one.

The Government of Manitoba says something similar in plain terms. Caulking cracks and sealing around pipes and drains "helps prevent radon from getting into the home," and installing specially designed traps in floor drains can also help. Notice the word is "helps." Nobody in a government publication says it solves the problem on its own.

## Why sealing alone usually isn't enough

The pressure difference that pulls soil gas in doesn't go away because one opening is closed. If you seal ten openings and an eleventh remains, the gas still has a route. If you seal every visible one, there can still be gaps hidden behind finished walls and openings you can't reach without opening up a wall. That's plain reasoning rather than a measured result, but it matches why Health Canada keeps sealing in a supporting role.

We can't give you a percentage for how much a sealing job lowers a reading. Health Canada doesn't publish one, and we won't make one up. What the guide does say is where large reductions come from. "When large radon reductions (50 per cent or more) are desired, active soil depressurization is almost always the recommended approach."

An active soil depressurization system is a pipe through the slab to the gravel or soil underneath and a fan that runs continuously, venting the gas outdoors. Health Canada says that in most homes it reduces the radon level by more than 80%, and its September 2026 factsheet says reductions of 90% or more are possible. Our [guide to how a radon mitigation system works](/blog/how-a-radon-mitigation-system-works/) covers the parts.

## Seal first, or test first?

Test first. That might feel backwards, but it has a practical reason.

If you seal a house and then test, you've changed the thing you're measuring and you have no baseline. A result of 150 Bq/m³ after sealing might mean the sealing helped. It might also mean the original level was always 150. A result of 350 tells you sealing wasn't enough. Without the "before," the "after" is hard to read.

There's a second reason. Plenty of homes test below the guideline without anyone touching a crack. The 2024 Cross-Canada Survey on Radon found that 1 in 5 Prairie and NWT households were at or above 200 Bq/m³, which means four in five were below it. A long-term test, at least 91 days during the heating season in the lowest lived-in level, is what separates the two groups. The post on [what radon level needs mitigation](/blog/what-radon-level-needs-mitigation/) explains where the lines fall.

Health Canada's guideline is 200 Bq/m³, and it recommends corrective action within 1 year when a long-term test comes back above it. The guideline is not a law. We found no Manitoba or Saskatchewan law requiring a homeowner to test or fix anything.

## After sealing: retest

Health Canada says to test after any radon mitigation system is installed. The same logic applies to sealing work, even if you've only done a few hours of caulking: the only way to know whether the number moved is another long-term test in the same spot as the first one.

Place the new detector where the old one sat. Keep it at least 30 cm above the floor and at least 30 cm from walls, and away from heat sources, sunlight, vents, doors, windows, sump holes, crawl spaces and exposed foundation cracks. That last item matters here. A detector next to an open crack reads the crack, not the room.

Then compare:

- If the second result is below 200 Bq/m³, retest again within 5 years or after major renovations, which is C-NRPP's guidance for that range.
- If it's still above 200, Health Canada recommends corrective action within 1 year of the test showing the high level, and a certified professional can say what a system would involve.

One thing we won't suggest is opening windows or running a basement fan as a way to deal with a high reading. Neither is a mitigation method.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Will sealing my foundation cracks fix a high radon reading?

Probably not on its own. Health Canada says sealing "is not a standalone technique for reducing radon levels." It treats sealing as support for a ventilation system. The only way to know what it did in your house is a long-term retest in the same location as the first test.

### Is it worth sealing cracks if I haven't tested yet?

Test first. A long-term test of at least 91 days gives you a baseline, and many homes come in below 200 Bq/m³ without any work. If you seal first, you can't tell afterward whether the sealing changed anything.

### What sort of cracks and gaps should be sealed for radon?

Health Canada lists cracks in the foundation floor and walls, construction joints, gaps around service pipes, support posts, window casements, floor drains, sumps and cavities inside walls as entry points. The Manitoba government says caulking cracks and sealing around pipes and drains helps, and that specially designed traps in floor drains can also help. A certified professional can tell you which openings matter in your house.
