# Radon Mitigation in Winter: What the Cold Changes on the Prairies

A radon test that runs through a Manitoba or Saskatchewan winter often comes back high, and then the next question arrives fast: can anyone actually put a system in when it's minus 30 outside?

Yes. A sub-slab system is mostly a pipe through the basement floor and a fan, and the work happens inside. But the cold does change a few details, and they're worth knowing before you sign a quote in January. This article covers where the fan goes, what freezing does to it, what gets checked on a winter job, and when your test results show up if you started in the heating season.

## Winter is also testing season

Health Canada says a radon test needs at least 91 days, with that time inside the heating season. The Government of Manitoba says to test "ideally during the winter (ex: October to April)." So the people with fresh results in November and December have usually just started a test, not finished one.

Do the calendar sums. A kit set out on 1 December can't give a valid result before the beginning of March. One started on 1 February is done around the beginning of May. That's the reason the numbers often arrive in spring, when the ground has thawed and the instinct is to put the whole thing off until summer.

You don't have to wait for the weather. Health Canada says that once a long-term result is over 200 Bq/m³, you should take corrective action within 1 year. A year is a long runway, and a few weeks' delay for the thaw is within it. But there's no rule that says the job can't be done in a cold snap, and a high number is a reason to book a professional, not to sit on it.

If you haven't tested yet, the winter months are the right ones to do it. Our [radon testing page](/radon-testing/) explains the steps, and the post on [the best time to test for radon](/blog/best-time-to-test-for-radon/) goes through the season question.

## Where the fan and pipe go matters more in a cold climate

A sub-slab system, which Health Canada calls active soil depressurization, runs a pipe from under the slab to a fan, which then discharges outdoors. The fan "runs 24/7." It doesn't take the winter off.

Health Canada's *Radon Reduction Guide for Canadians* has a cold-climate warning that applies directly to the Prairies: "a fan and pipe located outside the living space (garage or attic) will cool during the colder months of the year, leading to condensation and possibly ice, which can damage the fan."

The guide's fix is straightforward. "Condensation problems can be reduced if the fan is placed indoors and the exhaust is discharged from a shorter pipe near ground level." An indoor fan is "usually vented sideways through the rim joist at ground level." A fan in a garage or attic "is typically vented upwards above the roof."

Here's how the two layouts compare, using the guide's own wording.

| Fan location | How it vents | Cold-weather issue Health Canada notes |
|---|---|---|
| Inside the heated living space | Sideways through the rim joist, at ground level | Condensation problems can be reduced |
| Garage or attic | Upwards, above the roof | The fan and pipe cool in cold months, so condensation and possibly ice can form and damage the fan |

That doesn't make a garage or attic fan wrong. It means the location is a decision to talk through with the professional. Ask where the fan will sit, how the pipe will run, and what the plan is for condensation in the coldest weeks. If a layout has the fan in an unheated space, a good answer will mention it.

One practical thing to ask on top of the guide: where does the exhaust come out in relation to where snow piles up? We don't have a source that quantifies it, so it's a question for the installer rather than a rule.

## What gets checked on a winter job

Cold weather means the furnace, water heater and any fireplace or wood stove are all running hard. Health Canada says a system should be checked to make sure it "does not cause backdrafting of combustion appliances such as a furnace, water heater, fireplace, or wood stove." That check matters in a Prairie February in particular.

After the fan is switched on, Health Canada's current factsheet lists three steps:

1. A short-term radon test by a certified professional once the system is on, to confirm it is lowering the level below the Canadian guideline.
2. A long-term radon test during the first heating season after installation, in the same location as your original test.
3. A regular retest every five years, with the system inspected and maintained.

The second step is where a winter install has an advantage. If the system goes in during the heating season, you can start the long-term follow-up test almost straight away, and the 91-day clock has plenty of cold weather in it. If you install in May, you'll wait for the next heating season before that test means much.

Also check the U-tube manometer, the small gauge on the pipe. A level difference between the two sides shows the system is working. The post on [reading a radon manometer](/blog/radon-manometer-reading/) shows what you're looking at.

## How long it takes

Health Canada says a system "can usually be installed in less than a day," and Take Action on Radon says installation "can generally be completed in a day." Those aren't winter-specific figures, and we haven't found a source on how Prairie weather changes the schedule. Ask the professional how they'd handle a cold day or a frozen yard.

Don't try to cheat the season with a quick fix. Opening windows or running a basement fan isn't a radon method, and the heat you'd lose is real. The [DIY vs professional post](/blog/diy-radon-mitigation-vs-professional/) explains why Health Canada recommends a C-NRPP certified professional for the system itself.

## Running the system through the winter

Once it's in, a few things matter more in the cold.

- **The fan stays on.** Health Canada's guide says the fan "should NEVER be turned off." If the fan stops or starts making a new noise, don't wait for spring. The post on [radon fan noise](/blog/radon-fan-replacement-noise/) covers what to look at.
- **Look at the manometer now and then.** If the two sides of the U-tube are level, call the installer.
- **Keep an eye on exterior pipe.** If part of the pipe is outside, ask what the plan is if ice builds up.
- **Fan life.** Health Canada's 2014 guide says a fan lasts "five to ten or more years," and a replacement runs $200 to $300. Those are 2014 national figures, not current Prairie prices.

You can find a professional and confirm certification in the [C-NRPP professional directory](https://c-nrpp.ca/find-a-professional/). If a contractor says winter work is a problem, ask for specifics. If they say it's no problem, ask how they'll handle the fan location. Either answer tells you something.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a radon mitigation system be installed in winter?

Health Canada says a system can usually be installed in less than a day, and the main parts are a pipe through the slab and a fan. We haven't found a source on how Prairie weather affects scheduling, so ask the professional. Where the fan and exhaust go is the main cold-weather detail.

### Will the radon fan freeze?

It can if it's in an unheated space. Health Canada's guide says a fan and pipe in a garage or attic will cool in the colder months, which can lead to condensation and possibly ice that damages the fan. Placing the fan indoors with a short exhaust near ground level reduces the problem.

### Does radon mitigation cost more in winter?

One Winnipeg installer, Radon Manitoba, advertises an extra 20% for installations between 30 November and 20 May, in its own pricing. We haven't found other companies that publish a seasonal charge, so ask each one whether the date changes the quote.

### When will my radon results arrive if I test in winter?

A long-term test needs at least 91 days during the heating season, so a kit started on 1 December can't finish before early March. Results then depend on mailing the kit to the lab and its analysis time. A result over 200 Bq/m³ means action within 1 year, according to Health Canada.

### Does the furnace matter for a winter install?

Yes. Health Canada says the system should be checked so it does not cause backdrafting of combustion appliances, including a furnace, water heater, fireplace or wood stove. Ask the professional to confirm that check was done.
