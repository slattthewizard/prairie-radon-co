# Radon Detector Guide: Test Kits vs Digital Radon Monitors

A monitor borrowed from the library reads 480 Bq/m3 in your basement after a week, and now you're wondering whether to call an installer or buy a second gadget. Winnipeg installers advertise mitigation systems from about $1,500 to $3,000, so it's worth knowing what kind of radon detector gave you that number, and what it can and can't tell you, before you spend anything.

A Prairie homeowner is likely to meet two kinds of detector. One is a single-use long-term test kit that goes to a lab. The other is a digital monitor with a screen. They do different jobs. The kit gives you the number Health Canada's guideline is written around. The monitor is a good way to find out whether you should care, and a poor way to decide on a mitigation system.

## What a radon detector is actually measuring

Radon is measured in becquerels per cubic metre (Bq/m3), which is a measure of radioactivity in the air. Health Canada's guideline is 200 Bq/m3. If you have a US-made monitor or phone app, it may show picocuries per litre (pCi/L) instead. One pCi/L is 37 Bq/m3, so the Canadian guideline works out to about 5.4 pCi/L.

The word that matters in the guideline is "average". The 200 Bq/m3 figure applies to the average annual level in the normal occupancy area of your home, meaning a space someone uses more than four hours a day. Think finished basement, basement bedroom, rec room. It isn't meant for a crawl space, the furnace room or the unfinished corner where the Christmas boxes live.

Radon levels move around from day to day and season to season. That's why Health Canada says short-term measurements "are not appropriate for informing mitigation decisions" and asks for a test of at least 91 days, with at least 91 days falling in the heating season. Every detector on the market should be judged against that yardstick.

Why bother at all? Health Canada calls radon the number one cause of lung cancer in non-smokers and estimates it's behind more than 3,000 lung cancer deaths in Canada each year. For smokers it's the second leading cause. It's also invisible and has no smell, so a detector is the only way to know your own level. That's the whole case for testing, and you won't get a scarier version of it from us.

## Long-term radon test kits: the result that counts

A long-term kit is a small passive detector with no screen and no batteries. You set it out in the lowest lived-in level of the house, leave it alone for at least three months, then mail it to a lab. You get one number back: the average over the whole test period.

In Manitoba and Saskatchewan, both provincial lung associations send buyers to HomeRadonTest.ca. When we checked in September 2026 it charged $70.00 per single-use 90-day kit before tax, including shipping to and from the lab within Canada, email reminders and the analysis. The kits are analysed at the Saskatchewan Research Council lab. The Manitoba government says retail kits generally cost between $50 and $100.

Here's why the kit is the one that counts. A 91-day result can be compared straight to the 200 Bq/m3 guideline, which is what the guideline was built for. It's also the test the money programs want. The Lungs Matter grant, which can put up to $1,500 toward mitigation for low- and moderate-income households, needs a "long-term 90 day C-NRPP... approved radon test kit", and it says homeowner-level digital monitors don't qualify.

The downside is patience. You wait three months, then you wait for the lab. You get a single number and no detail about which week was worst. If the kit gets knocked over, moved halfway through or pulled early, you may be starting again: Health Canada recommends a new test when a measurement runs shorter than 91 days.

Timing helps. The Manitoba government suggests testing ideally in winter, October to April. A kit set out in October or November in Winnipeg, Regina or Saskatoon covers the heating season and gives you a result in time to plan any work in spring. ## Digital radon monitors: useful, with limits

A digital radon monitor is a plug-in or battery unit with a display. It keeps sampling the air and, depending on the model, shows a recent reading, a running average or both. Some connect to a phone app.

They're good for a first look. Plug one in and within days you have a rough idea whether there's something worth a proper test. They're also handy for comparing rooms, or for keeping an eye on things between proper tests. People like seeing a number move, and honestly that's part of the appeal.

That's also the trap. Day-to-day readings go up and down, and a high week or a low week tells you very little about the yearly average. Health Canada treats short-term readings as screening only. Three weeks of data is still three weeks.

Accuracy varies between models, and some homeowner-level monitors aren't designed to stand in for a lab test. If you want to use a digital monitor for a long-term measurement, Health Canada says to choose one that has passed C-NRPP performance testing for long-term radon measurements in homes. C-NRPP, the Canadian National Radon Proficiency Program, keeps a list of approved electronic monitors on [its website](https://c-nrpp.ca/). Check the model against that list before you rely on it, and remember that the Lungs Matter grant still won't accept it.

Monitor prices vary by model and we don't quote them here. We don't recommend a brand either.

### Borrowing one for free

You may not need to buy a monitor at all. Winnipeg Public Library lends radon screening kits from all 20 branches between 1 November and 30 April. The loan is three weeks with no renewals, and you need a card in good standing. CBC reported that the monitor should sit in the lowest level of the home for at least seven days.

Regina Public Library has 30 kits on a three-week loan with no renewals, for Regina residents. Its kit includes a coupon for an analog long-term test, which is a nice nudge toward the right next step. Moose Jaw Public Library lends kits from 1 October to 1 April, free with a library card. In Brandon, the Western Manitoba Regional Library takes part in the Manitoba Lung Association lending program. Saskatoon libraries have lent detectors too; ask your branch for current terms. Our [library loan guide](/blog/borrow-radon-detector-library/) has more.

## Three Prairie situations and the detector that fits

These are composite examples to show the thinking, not customer stories.

The first is a library monitor reading 480 Bq/m3 in a Regina rec room in early December. That's well over 200, and not a shock: 46.6% of the Regina homes tested in the 2024 Cross-Canada survey came in at or above 200. It's still a three-week screening reading, though. The sensible move is a $70 long-term kit in the rec room, at least 30 cm above the floor and 30 cm from the walls, away from the sump and any vents, left through the winter. Health Canada recommends corrective action within one year of a result over 200, so a proper three-month test fits inside that window. For a sense of scale, a Regina installer quotes $2,500 to $3,500 for a system, more for crawl spaces or multiple suction points.

The second is a 1962 bungalow in St. Vital with a finished basement bedroom. The owners bought a digital monitor and watched it bounce from the low 100s to over 300 from one week to the next. They didn't know which number to believe, and the answer was neither on its own. They could run the monitor through a full heating season if the model is on C-NRPP's approved list, or put a $70 kit in the bedroom at breathing height and let a lab settle it. For context, 37.4% of the Winnipeg homes tested in the 2024 survey were at or above 200. If the result is high, Winnipeg installers advertise systems from about $1,500 to $3,000, and the Manitoba Hydro Home Energy Efficiency Loan covers radon mitigation up to $5,000 over five years at 6.2% (O.A.C.), with a C-NRPP certified contractor.

The third is a Saskatoon two-storey sold with a radon condition in the offer and a closing date a month out. There's no time for a 91-day test. C-NRPP says a short-term screening during a sale can give some insight, with a long-term test after moving in. Saskatoon had the lowest numbers of the four surveyed cities, roughly 1 in 6 tested homes at or above 200, but low odds aren't a result for this house. How a condition, holdback or price adjustment is written is a question for your realtor or lawyer, not a detector. No Saskatoon mitigation price was found; the Winnipeg and Regina figures above are the nearest published ones.

## What to do once you have a number

If a long-term test comes back under 200, Health Canada is careful to say there's no level that is risk free. Retest after an energy retrofit, after a floor that wasn't tested becomes the lowest lived-in level, or if you finish a basement you weren't using before. Our [detector placement guide](/blog/where-to-place-radon-detector/) covers where the kit goes each time.

If it comes back over 200, the usual fix is sub-slab depressurization: a pipe through the basement slab, a fan, and a discharge outside. Health Canada recommends a C-NRPP certified mitigation professional, sealing any sump pit, and retesting after the system goes in. What it costs depends on the house, the slab and how many suction points it needs, which is why a certified professional has to look before quoting. The [radon mitigation page](/radon-mitigation/) explains how the system works.

There's some help with the bill. Lungs Matter offers up to $1,500 to households with income below the provincial median, but it needs the 90-day C-NRPP test and a C-NRPP quote, it won't pay for work already done, and funding is limited. In Saskatchewan, Lung Saskatchewan has offered reimbursement in the past; check its site. Lung Saskatchewan also says radon mitigation counts as an eligible expense for the Saskatchewan Home Renovation Tax Credit; confirm with CRA. There is currently no federal radon tax credit that we could find.

Whoever you hire, you can check their certification in C-NRPP's [find a professional directory](https://c-nrpp.ca/find-a-professional/). Certification is voluntary in Canada and no province regulates radon professionals, so the directory is the check that matters.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is a digital radon detector accurate enough to decide on mitigation?

Not on a short run. Health Canada says short-term measurements are not appropriate for mitigation decisions, and the guideline is based on at least 91 days. A digital monitor on C-NRPP's approved list can be used for a long-term measurement, but a $70 lab-analysed kit is the simpler route for most homes.

### How long does a radon test kit take?

At least three months, with at least 91 days in the heating season. Health Canada says 3 to 12 months gives the best picture of the yearly average. After that you mail the kit to the lab and wait for the result.

### Can I use a library radon monitor for the Lungs Matter grant?

No. The grant needs a long-term 90-day C-NRPP approved test kit showing a result above 200 Bq/m3, and homeowner-level digital monitors don't qualify. A library loan is a free first look; the kit is what the application needs.

### Where should I put a radon detector?

In the lowest lived-in level of the home, in a room someone uses more than four hours a day. Keep it in the breathing zone, at least 30 cm above the floor and 30 cm from walls, and away from sumps, vents, windows, doors and heat sources. Leave it in one spot for the whole test.
