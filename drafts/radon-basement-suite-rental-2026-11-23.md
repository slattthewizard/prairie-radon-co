# Radon in a Basement Suite: What Landlords and Tenants Should Know

A basement suite is about as close as a home gets to the place Health Canada tells you to measure radon. It's the lowest lived-in level, it's in contact with the ground, and someone sleeps there every night. Yet it's also the part of the house where, in a rental, nobody may be sure whose job it is to test.

This article is for both sides of the lease. It covers what Health Canada's guideline says about basement apartments, what the law in Manitoba and Saskatchewan does and doesn't require, and what each party can reasonably do. We'll say plainly where the rules run out, because they run out sooner than most people expect.

## Why a basement suite sits in the radon zone

Radon is a radioactive gas that comes out of the soil. It gets into houses because, as Health Canada explains, "the air pressure inside your home is usually lower than in the soil surrounding the foundation." The gas follows that pull in through cracks in the foundation floor and walls, construction joints, gaps around service pipes, support posts, floor drains and sumps.

A basement suite has the most contact with all of those entry points. It also tends to be where people spend a lot of hours, which is the test Health Canada uses.

The guideline is 200 Bq/m³, and it applies to the average annual level in the "normal occupancy area" of a building. Health Canada defines that as any part of the building where a person is likely to spend more than 4 hours per day, and it names a finished basement or a basement apartment as an example. A crawl space, unfinished basement, cold room, storage area, furnace room and laundry room are excluded.

So there's no ambiguity about the suite. If someone lives in it, it's a normal occupancy area, and the guideline applies to it. The guideline also covers public buildings and workplaces with an occupancy of more than 4 hours per day, which means it isn't only about owner-occupied homes.

## What the law requires (and doesn't)

This is the part people want a clear answer on, and the clear answer is that we didn't find one.

- **No provincial law.** We found no law in Manitoba or Saskatchewan requiring residential radon testing, mitigation, or landlord testing. The City of Regina's advisory says the Health Canada levels "are guidelines only, as there is no legislated maximum level or enforceable level."
- **No municipal power to step in.** The City of Regina also says it "is not able to enforce radon testing/mitigation or act as a liaison between a tenant and an owner." We found no radon bylaw in Winnipeg, Saskatoon, Brandon, Moose Jaw or Prince Albert beyond the building code.
- **The tenancy acts are a policy lever, not a radon right.** Health Canada's action guide for provinces and territories points to the "good repair" sections of the two tenancy statutes: Saskatchewan's *Residential Tenancies Act, 2006*, section 49(1), and Manitoba's *Residential Tenancies Act*, section 59(1). Health Canada lists these as places where a province could act. It does not say they already give tenants a radon right, and we aren't claiming they do. If a dispute is serious, get independent legal advice rather than relying on a blog post.

What exists instead is a recommendation. Health Canada "recommends every home should be tested, no matter where it is located, the type of building, or if it already has a mitigation system." A rental suite is a home.

Governments sometimes act on their own housing. Saskatchewan Housing Corporation, for instance, is testing its own social-housing units with Lung Saskatchewan over 10 years from March 2024. That's voluntary action on its own stock. It isn't a rule for private landlords.

## If you're the landlord or the owner

Even without a legal duty we can point to, there are sensible reasons to test.

**You own the building and the foundation.** Whatever the radon level is, the entry points belong to your property, and the fix is a change to your structure. A tenant can't install a pipe through your slab.

**Health Canada's guideline sets the standard people will measure against.** If a tenant tests and finds a high number, the question of what you do next is a lot easier if you've already looked into it.

**A fix usually serves the whole house.** A sub-slab system pulls radon from under the foundation, so the one system covers the suite and the upstairs. Health Canada says in most homes it reduces the level by more than 80%, and its 2026 factsheet says 90% or more.

Costs, as advertised: Winnipeg installers advertise systems from about $1,500 to $3,000, and a Regina installer quotes $2,500 to $3,500. We found no published price for Saskatoon, Brandon, Moose Jaw or Prince Albert, so we aren't offering one. Our [mitigation cost page](/radon-mitigation-cost/) lays out the sourced figures.

Some money helps may apply to owner-occupied homes more clearly than to rentals, so check before you plan around any of them. The Manitoba Hydro Home Energy Efficiency Loan covers radon mitigation up to $5,000 over 5 years at 6.2% (O.A.C.) and needs a C-NRPP certified contractor. The Saskatchewan Home Renovation Tax Credit is for a principal residence in Saskatchewan, and Lung Saskatchewan says radon mitigation counts as an eligible expense; confirm with CRA, especially if the house is an investment property. The post on [radon mitigation grants in Manitoba](/blog/radon-mitigation-grants-manitoba/) covers the Manitoba side.

**Finishing or renting out a basement changes the test.** Health Canada says to retest if a floor that was not tested becomes the lowest lived-in level. If you turn an unfinished basement into a suite, an earlier test that was done upstairs doesn't tell you about the new bedroom. Health Canada also lists energy retrofits as a retest trigger, which matters if the suite was part of a renovation. See [radon after an energy retrofit](/blog/radon-after-energy-retrofit/).

## If you're the tenant

You have fewer options, but you aren't without any.

1. **Ask whether the unit has been tested.** It's a normal question. A long-term result in Bq/m³ is the answer you want, not "we've never had a problem."
2. **Offer to do the test, with permission.** A long-term kit costs about $70.00 before tax from HomeRadonTest.ca, which the provincial lung associations use. The test needs at least 91 days, ideally all in the heating season, in the main living area of the lowest level. Placement matters, so see [where to place a radon detector](/blog/where-to-place-radon-detector/).
3. **Borrow a screening monitor first.** Winnipeg Public Library lends radon screening kits free from 1 November to 30 April (three-week loan, library card in good standing). Regina Public Library lends detectors to Regina residents for three weeks, and Moose Jaw Public Library lends from 1 October to 1 April. A monitor is a screening step, not a result. The [library lending post](/blog/borrow-radon-detector-library/) covers loan terms.
4. **Keep a record.** Write down the dates, where the detector sat, and the lab's number in Bq/m³. If you need to raise it with the landlord, a dated result is far more useful than a worry.
5. **Don't touch the system.** If the house already has a mitigation system, the fan is supposed to run all the time. Health Canada says it should never be turned off. Health Canada estimated in 2014 that a fan costs about $50 to $75 a year in electricity, and it's worth knowing who pays that under your lease.

If a result is above 200 Bq/m³, Health Canada recommends corrective action within 1 year, with a sooner timeline for higher levels. The decision and the work sit with the owner. You can share the result and point to Health Canada's guidance. Beyond that, we can't say what a Manitoba or Saskatchewan tribunal would conclude, and nothing here is legal advice.

A word on tone. Landlords and tenants both lose when a radon conversation starts as an accusation. A shared set of numbers and one phone call to a certified professional is a better opening than a letter.

## What a basement suite owner can do in order

1. **Test the suite.** Use a long-term test in the lowest lived-in level, not a quick screening.
2. **Read the result in Bq/m³ against 200.** Above 200, mitigate within 1 year.
3. **Get quotes from C-NRPP certified professionals.** The [quote checklist post](/blog/radon-mitigation-quote-checklist/) lists what to ask.
4. **Verify the certification.** The [C-NRPP professional directory](https://c-nrpp.ca/find-a-professional/) lets you check a company before you hire it.
5. **Retest after the system is on.** Health Canada's steps are a short-term test by a certified professional, a long-term test in the first heating season, and another every five years.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does the radon guideline apply to a basement apartment?

Yes. Health Canada defines the normal occupancy area as any part of a building where a person is likely to spend more than 4 hours per day, and it names a finished basement or a basement apartment as part of it. A crawl space, cold room, furnace room or laundry room is not.

### Is a landlord in Manitoba or Saskatchewan required to test for radon?

We found no law in either province requiring landlords to test or mitigate. Health Canada recommends that every home be tested, and it lists the tenancy acts' good repair sections as policy levers, not as an existing radon right. For a specific dispute, get legal advice.

### Can a tenant test for radon without the landlord's permission?

A detector sits in the unit you rent, so many tenants will place one without any trouble. But if the test involves moving things or a professional visit, tell the landlord. Tell them the result too. A long-term kit is about $70.00 before tax, and a library monitor is free for a screening look.

### Who pays if a rental basement has high radon?

There's no rule we could find that settles it, and it will depend on who owns the building and what the lease says. The foundation and any system installed through it belong to the owner, so in practice the owner is the one who can arrange the work. The City of Regina says it can't act as a liaison between a tenant and an owner.
