# Selling a House With Radon: What a Prairie Seller Needs to Know

You're about to list the house, and someone mentions radon. Maybe a buyer's agent asks, maybe you tested years ago, or maybe a neighbour just got a high result. Do you have to test? Do you have to say anything? What does a high number do to a sale?

The answer has a few layers, and some we can't pin down. This article sticks to published sources, flags what we couldn't verify, and lays out the options. It isn't legal advice, so ask your realtor or a lawyer about your own sale.

## Is there a law that makes you test before selling?

We haven't found one. Neither Manitoba nor Saskatchewan has a law we could find that requires a homeowner to test for radon, fix it, or disclose a result when selling. The City of Regina says the Health Canada levels "are guidelines only, as there is no legislated maximum level or enforceable level." The Canadian guideline is 200 Bq/m³ (becquerels per cubic metre), and Health Canada recommends corrective action within 1 year when a long-term test is above it. It's a recommendation, not a statute.

That's the testing side. Disclosure is a different question, and it turns on what you already know.

## Saskatchewan: the known-defect duty

PLEA, Saskatchewan's public legal education group, says a property condition disclosure statement "is not required for a sale." Sellers, though, "must inform buyers about any defects known to the seller that would not be discovered by a reasonable inspection."

Read that carefully. The duty isn't about forms. It's about what you know. If you have a long-term result of, say, 350 Bq/m³ in a drawer, you're a seller who knows something about the house. Whether a radon reading counts as a "defect" for this purpose is a legal question, and we haven't found a source that answers it either way. Ask a lawyer.

The only Saskatchewan realtor form we found was the Association of Saskatchewan REALTORS® property condition disclosure statement, revised in 2008, and it has no radon line. Forms get revised, so check the one your agent actually uses.

## Manitoba: the Property Disclosure Statement

In Manitoba, the Property Disclosure Statement is Appendix A to the Residential Form of Offer to Purchase. It's prescribed under The Real Estate Brokers Act, and the seller uses it to disclose information on the condition of the property "to the best of the seller's knowledge."

Whether that form has a radon question, we couldn't verify. The PDF couldn't be retrieved when we checked, so we won't tell you it does or doesn't. Look at the current form with your agent before you fill it in. Health Canada notes that property disclosure statements in some provinces include a line for radon. We couldn't confirm that Manitoba's is one of them.

## What realtors are expected to do

Health Canada says real estate licensees have professional duties that include "disclosing radon as a known latent defects to buyers." That's Health Canada's description, in a guide written for provincial governments, not a Manitoba or Saskatchewan rule we can cite. In practice it means that if you tell your agent about a radon result, expect them to treat it as something the buyer should hear. Better that than a buyer's inspector finding out first.

There's a plain business case here too. A result you volunteer, with a plan attached, is a conversation. A result a buyer finds after the offer is a dispute.

### Option 1: Do nothing

It's legal as far as we can tell. Health Canada recommends every home be tested, though, so a buyer may ask. If a buyer asks for a test condition and you've never looked, you're negotiating blind.

### Option 2: Test first

If you have time, a long-term test is the one that counts. Health Canada recommends at least 91 days, with that time in the heating season, in the lowest lived-in level. HomeRadonTest.ca sells a long-term kit for $70.00 before tax, and the Manitoba government puts kits at "generally ranges between $50 and $100." Our [radon testing page](/radon-testing/) and the post on [long-term vs short-term radon tests](/blog/long-term-vs-short-term-radon-test/) cover the method.

The catch is time. A 91-day test started on 1 December can't finish before early March. If you're listing in a few weeks, you may not have that long, and it's worth understanding the shortcut.

C-NRPP says that in a real estate sale, "a short-term radon screening could be conducted to provide some insight," with a long-term test after moving in. Health Canada is more cautious: short-term measurements "are not appropriate for informing mitigation decisions," because the guideline applies to an annual average. So a short screening can tell a seller whether to take radon seriously, but it can't tell anyone the real number. If you share a short-term result, call it what it is.

You can hand over an existing result along with whatever quote you've collected, and let the buyer decide. This works best with a long-term test and a certified professional's written estimate. ## If a buyer asks for a radon test

It happens. A buyer may put a radon test in the offer as a condition. A few things help.

- **Ask what kind of test.** A short-term screening and a 91-day test aren't the same. Agree in writing which one counts.
- **Know the placement.** The detector belongs in the lowest lived-in level, at least 30 cm above the floor and away from walls, vents and sumps. The post on [where to place a radon detector](/blog/where-to-place-radon-detector/) has the details.
- **Ask who's testing.** C-NRPP lists certified measurement professionals, and you can check anyone in the [C-NRPP professional directory](https://c-nrpp.ca/find-a-professional/).
- **Have an answer ready if it's high.** Health Canada says to mitigate within 1 year above 200 Bq/m³, so a high result comes with a known next step. We can't say how any particular buyer will react.

Don't try to make a result look better than it is. Opening windows and running fans aren't radon fixes, and a detector that's been moved or run short doesn't give a valid number.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I have to test for radon before selling in Manitoba or Saskatchewan?

We haven't found a Manitoba or Saskatchewan law that requires testing before a sale. The City of Regina says there is no legislated maximum level or enforceable level, and the Canadian guideline of 200 Bq/m³ is a recommendation. Buyers can still ask for a test as part of an offer.

### Do I have to tell a buyer about a high radon result?

In Saskatchewan, PLEA says sellers must inform buyers about defects known to the seller that a reasonable inspection wouldn't find. Whether a radon result counts is a legal question we couldn't answer from the sources. Health Canada also says realtors have duties around disclosing radon as a known latent defect, so talk to your agent or a lawyer.

### Does the Manitoba Property Disclosure Statement ask about radon?

We couldn't verify it. The form is Appendix A to the Residential Form of Offer to Purchase, but the PDF couldn't be retrieved when we checked. Ask your agent for the current version and read the questions yourself.

### Should I mitigate before I list the house?

It's your decision. Health Canada recommends corrective action within 1 year if a long-term test is above 200 Bq/m³, and a sub-slab system reduces levels by more than 80% in most homes. Some sellers prefer to fix it first, and others share the result and a quote with the buyer. A C-NRPP certified professional can give you a written estimate for either path.

### Can I use a short-term test to sell my house?

A short-term screening can give you some insight, as C-NRPP puts it, but Health Canada says short-term measurements aren't appropriate for informing mitigation decisions. The guideline is an annual average, so if you share a short-term result, label it as a screening and plan a long-term test of at least 91 days.
