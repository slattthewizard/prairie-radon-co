# Where to Place a Radon Detector: Placement Rules That Keep the Result Honest

A proper radon test takes at least three months, and the easiest way to waste those three months is to put the detector in the wrong spot. Leave it on the laundry room shelf beside the sump and you get a number that says very little about the basement bedroom where your kid sleeps. Knowing where to place a radon detector before you open the kit is the cheapest part of the whole job.

This guide covers Health Canada's placement rules, the reason behind each one, and what they look like in an ordinary Winnipeg, Regina or Saskatoon basement.

## Why placement matters more on the Prairies

Radon is common here. In the 2024 Cross-Canada Survey, 37.4% of the Winnipeg homes that were tested came in at or above 200 Bq/m3, and so did 46.6% of the Regina homes tested. Saskatoon was lower, at roughly 1 in 6 tested homes. These were households that chose to test, not a random sample, so read them as a rough picture rather than a street-by-street map.

Bq/m3 means becquerels per cubic metre, a count of radioactive decays per second in a cubic metre of air. The US uses picocuries per litre instead. 37 Bq/m3 equals 1 pCi/L, so Canada's 200 Bq/m3 guideline works out to about 5.4 pCi/L.

Why bother at all? Health Canada says radon is the number one cause of lung cancer in non-smokers and estimates it leads to more than 3,000 lung cancer deaths in Canada each year. That's the health case, and it doesn't need dressing up.

The 200 figure is a guideline, not a legal limit. No Manitoba or Saskatchewan law makes a homeowner test or fix anything. It's also an annual average for the part of the house people actually live in. So a result is only useful if the detector sat where the guideline assumes it sat. Put it anywhere else and you've answered a different question.

## Where to place a radon detector: the lowest lived-in level

Health Canada's rule is the lowest level of the home that someone uses for 4 or more hours a day. It calls this the normal occupancy area.

In most Prairie houses that means the basement, as long as the basement is finished and used. A basement bedroom counts. So does a rec room where the kids watch TV most evenings, a home office, or a basement suite. An unfinished basement full of storage bins doesn't count, and neither does a crawl space, a furnace room or a laundry room.

This is where people go wrong. Radon comes up from the soil, so the instinct is to test as low and as close to the dirt as possible, and a worst-case reading from the laundry room feels thorough. It isn't what the guideline measures. A high number from a room nobody sits in can push you toward a mitigation system on the wrong evidence, and a room with more air movement can read lower than the bedroom beside it.

If nobody spends 4 hours a day in the basement, the main floor is your lowest lived-in level, so test there. Finish the basement later and move a bedroom down, and you have a new lowest lived-in level. Health Canada says to retest when that happens.

## Radon detector placement inside the room

Once you've picked the room, the spot inside it matters too. Health Canada's measurement guide is specific:

- Put it in the breathing zone, at least 30 cm above the floor.
- Keep it at least 30 cm away from walls.
- Keep it away from sump pits.
- Keep it away from heating and air vents, windows, and heat sources such as a baseboard heater.

The guide doesn't spell out a reason for every rule, but the logic is plain enough. You want the air the people in that room breathe. A sump pit is an opening to the soil, so the air right over it isn't typical of the room. A vent or a leaky window moves air around and can dilute what the detector sees.

In practice, a small table or a shelf pulled out from the wall at about bedside height works well. Pick somewhere it won't get knocked over, used as a coaster, or unplugged if it's a digital monitor. Write the start date on the kit, leave it where it is for the whole test, and live in the house normally. A test run with the house held in some unusual state doesn't tell you about the house you actually live in.

Here's how common spots stack up:

| Spot | Use it? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Basement bedroom used every night | Yes | Lowest lived-in level, used 4+ hours a day |
| Finished rec room or basement suite | Yes | Normal occupancy area |
| Main-floor bedroom (basement unused) | Yes | Main floor is then the lowest lived-in level |
| Shelf beside the sump pit | No | Health Canada says keep away from sumps |
| Laundry room or furnace room | No | Not a normal occupancy area |
| Unfinished storage basement or crawl space | No | Not a normal occupancy area |
| Windowsill or next to a supply vent | No | Moving air, and the guide says away from both |
| On the floor | No | Below the 30 cm breathing zone |

## How long to leave the detector, and what kind to use

Placement is half of an honest result. Duration is the other half.

Health Canada wants a long-term test of at least 3 months, with a minimum of 91 days in the heating season. Anywhere from 3 to 12 months is ideal. If your test ran shorter than 91 days, Health Canada's advice is to repeat it. Our post on [long-term vs short-term radon tests](/blog/long-term-vs-short-term-radon-test/) goes into why a few days of data can swing so far.

Short-term kits and digital monitors are screening tools. They're handy for a first look, but they don't replace a long-term test. If you plan to use a digital monitor for the long-term test itself, it should be on C-NRPP's approved list. The trade-offs are covered in [radon test kit vs digital monitor](/blog/radon-test-kit-vs-digital-monitor/).

Cost isn't much of a barrier. The Manitoba government puts a long-term kit at "between $50 and $100". Libraries lend digital screening monitors for free, too: Winnipeg Public Library has them at every branch from 1 November to 30 April on a three-week loan, and Regina Public Library has 30 kits on three-week loans with no renewals. Saskatoon libraries have lent detectors as well; check with your branch for current terms. See [borrowing a radon detector from the library](/blog/borrow-radon-detector-library/) for the details.

A borrowed monitor follows the same placement rules. Three weeks in the right room tells you more than three weeks on the dryer.

## Three placement mistakes, and what they cost

These three houses are illustrations built from the rules above, not stories about real customers.

**A 1962 bungalow in St. Vital.** The basement has a teenager's bedroom at one end and the laundry room and sump at the other. The long-term kit goes on the laundry shelf above the sump because that's where it's out of the way. After 91 days the result comes back well over 200. The trouble is that the number describes air above a hole in the slab, not the bedroom. The fix is another long-term kit, $50 to $100 by the Manitoba government's figure, in the bedroom, 30 cm up and away from the walls, and another three months. If the bedroom also reads over 200, Winnipeg installers advertise systems from about $1,500 to $3,000, and any proper install will seal that sump pit anyway. A Manitoba Hydro Home Energy Efficiency Loan can cover radon mitigation up to $5,000 over 5 years at 6.2% (O.A.C.), provided the contractor is C-NRPP certified.

**A Regina rec room with a library monitor reading 480 Bq/m3.** The monitor sat on the coffee table for its three-week loan, which was a sensible spot. It's still a screening number. Regina's tested homes run high, so don't shrug it off, but three weeks isn't an annual average. The next step is a long-term detector in the same rec room, on a surface 30 cm off the floor and clear of the heating vent. If that result is over 200, Health Canada recommends fixing it within a year. A Regina installer quotes $2,500 to $3,500, and more for crawl spaces or multiple suction points. Lung Saskatchewan has offered reimbursement in the past; check its site.

**A Saskatoon two-storey sold with a radon condition in the offer.** The buyer wants a number before closing, and 91 days doesn't fit a closing date. Any short test here is screening, and where it sits still matters: the lowest lived-in level, not the utility room. Saskatoon had the lowest numbers of the four cities surveyed, with 17.8% of tested homes at or above 200 in the 2024 survey, but the survey itself notes there are no areas of Canada that are radon-free. We found no Manitoba or Saskatchewan rule that requires radon testing or disclosure at sale. How a condition, holdback or disclosure should be worded is a question for your realtor or lawyer.

## After the test: what to do with the number

Below 200 Bq/m3, Health Canada's position is that no level is considered risk free. Whether to do anything more is your call.

Above 200, Health Canada recommends corrective action within 1 year. The usual fix is sub-slab depressurization: a pipe through the basement slab, a fan, and discharge outside. Health Canada recommends a C-NRPP certified mitigation professional for the work. How well it works in your house depends on the soil under the slab, the foundation and whether there's a crawl space, which is why nobody honest will promise a result before looking. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I put the radon detector in my unfinished basement?

Only if someone uses that space for 4 or more hours a day. Health Canada's guideline applies to the normal occupancy area, and an unfinished storage basement, furnace room or laundry room isn't one. If nobody spends time down there, test on the main floor instead.

### How high off the floor should a radon detector be?

Health Canada says at least 30 cm above the floor, in the breathing zone, and at least 30 cm from walls. A small table or shelf pulled out from the wall works well. Keep it away from the sump, vents, windows and heaters.

### Should I test more than one room?

The guideline is built around one proper test in the lowest lived-in level. If that level has two separate living spaces, such as a basement suite and the owners' rec room, ask a C-NRPP certified measurement professional whether a second detector is worth it. More detectors in the wrong rooms won't help.

### What if my test ran short or the detector got moved?

If the test lasted less than 91 days, Health Canada says to repeat it. If the detector spent weeks in a different room, the result blends two spaces and won't match the guideline. The clean fix is a fresh long-term test in the right spot.
