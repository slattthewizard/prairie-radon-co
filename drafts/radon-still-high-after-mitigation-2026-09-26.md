# Radon Still High After Mitigation? Why a Retest Can Come Back Over 200

You paid for a radon system, the installer's quick check looked fine, and then the long-term test from the first winter came back over 200. That's an annoying envelope to open. If your radon is still high after mitigation, the system hasn't necessarily failed, and it's rarely a reason to rip anything out. The cause is usually one of a short list, and several of them cost nothing to check.

A quick note on units. Bq/m3 means becquerels per cubic metre, the unit Health Canada uses. Its guideline is 200 Bq/m3 as an average annual level in the part of the home you use more than 4 hours a day, like a finished basement. For comparison, 37 Bq/m3 equals 1 pCi/L, the US unit, so 200 is about 5.4 pCi/L.

It's a guideline, not a legal limit. Neither Manitoba nor Saskatchewan requires homeowners to test or fix. The reason to follow up anyway is the one Health Canada gives: radon is the leading cause of lung cancer in non-smokers and the second overall, linked to more than 3,000 lung cancer deaths in Canada each year. That's worth a calm second look, not a panic.

## Why radon can still be high after mitigation

Sub-slab depressurization, the pipe-through-the-floor system most Prairie homes get, uses a fan that runs around the clock to pull soil gas from under the slab and vent it outside. It also reverses the pressure difference, so the house stops drawing soil air in through the foundation. Health Canada says it cuts radon by more than 80% in most homes.

"Most" is the word to notice. The result depends on what's under your slab and how many openings the house has. When a retest comes back high, the cause nearly always sits in one of two groups.

1. The number is wrong or misleading: a test that was too short, a screening monitor, or a detector in the wrong spot.
2. The system isn't pulling across the whole foundation: a fan that's off or dead, an open sump, a floor drain with no trap, gaps left unsealed, or part of the house the suction never reaches.

It also helps to know what the retest was supposed to look like. Health Canada's current sequence is a short-term test by a certified professional once the system is switched on, then a long-term test during the first heating season in the same spot as your original test, then a retest every five years. If your "retest" was something else, start there.

## Radon retest over 200 after mitigation? Check the test first

Pull out whatever paperwork or device gave you the number and ask four questions.

**How long did it run?** The guideline is about your annual average. Health Canada's minimum is 91 days, with those 91 days in the heating season. Anything shorter should be repeated, and Health Canada says plainly that short-term measurements aren't appropriate for mitigation decisions.

**What kind of device was it?** Digital monitors, including the ones libraries lend, are screening tools. They swing from hour to hour and night to night. If you use one long-term, it should be on C-NRPP's approved list.

**Where was it?** It should sit in the same place as your original test, on the lowest lived-in level, at least 30 cm above the floor and 30 cm from walls. Keep it away from the sump, vents, windows, heat and exposed cracks. A detector parked beside the sump lid tells you about the sump, not the bedroom.

**Did anything change?** Health Canada says to retest after energy retrofits (see [radon after an energy retrofit](/blog/radon-after-energy-retrofit/)) and when a new floor becomes the lowest lived-in level. New windows, fresh insulation or a basement just finished as a suite can all move the number.

A long-term kit is the cheapest step in this whole article. The Manitoba government puts kits "between $50 and $100", and one online seller lists them at $70.00 per kit before tax. ## When the system is the problem: fan, sump and suction

If the test was sound and the average is still over 200, look at the system itself.

**The fan.** Health Canada says the fan should never be turned off: it has to run continuously. Check the switch and the breaker, because a fan that someone switched off over the noise does nothing. Then look at the U-tube manometer on the pipe. A level difference between the two sides shows the system is pulling. Level sides mean no suction: the fan is off, dead, or the pipe has come apart. Our [manometer reading guide](/blog/radon-manometer-reading/) shows what to look for.

A working manometer doesn't prove the whole basement is covered. It only proves the fan is pulling somewhere. Health Canada's 2014 guide puts fan life at five to ten or more years. It also warns that a fan in a garage or attic can collect condensation and ice in cold months, which can damage it. That's a real Prairie issue.

**The sump and floor drains.** An open sump is a straight path for soil gas. Health Canada says a sump can take an airtight cover, with a special trap if it also works as a floor drain, and a floor drain connected to the sump needs a mechanical trap seal or water trap. The [sump pit sealing guide](/blog/radon-sump-pit-sealing/) covers this in detail.

**Unsealed openings.** Radon comes in through cracks in floors and walls, construction joints, gaps around service pipes, support posts and floor drains. Health Canada says sealing these helps the system work, but sealing on its own isn't a fix.

**The suction field.** A system uses one suction point, sometimes several. Whether one point reaches the whole slab depends on what's underneath and how the foundation was built. A crawl space, an addition on its own slab or separate footings can all sit outside the zone the fan is pulling from. Working this out is a job for a C-NRPP certified mitigation professional, usually by drilling small test holes in the slab and checking whether the fan's suction reaches them.

Any change to the fan or pipe should include a check that the system doesn't backdraft the furnace, water heater, fireplace or wood stove. Health Canada lists that check specifically.

One last practical point: call your original installer first. At least one Winnipeg installer advertises a five-year warranty, so read your paperwork before paying someone new.

## Three Prairie scenarios

These are composites of common situations, not customer stories.

**A 1962 bungalow in St. Vital with a finished basement bedroom.** The system went in at a price inside the Winnipeg advertised range of about $1,500 to $3,000. The first-winter long-term test, in the same bedroom spot as the original, came back over 200. The manometer showed a level difference and the fan hummed, so the system was running. In the laundry room, an old floor drain ran into a sump with a loose lid. The fan was pulling from under the slab while the open sump let soil air straight in. The fix to discuss with a certified professional: an airtight cover with a trap on the drain, then another 91-day test. A new kit costs $50 to $100; the lid and trap are quoted on site.

**A Regina rec room and a library monitor.** A year after mitigation, a homeowner borrowed one of Regina Public Library's 30 monitors (three-week loan, no renewals) and saw 480 Bq/m3 on a cold, still night. Three weeks isn't 91 days, and a monitor is a screening tool. The first thing to check that night is the manometer: level sides would mean the fan had stopped. If the fan is running, the next step is a long-term test in the original spot before calling anyone. Only if that long-term average comes back over 200 does the system need a professional look.

**A Saskatoon two-storey with a crawl space under a back addition.** The original system has one suction point in the main basement slab. A long-term test in the family room above the addition comes back over 200. The crawl space sits outside the suction field, so the likely fix is a second suction point or a crawl space treatment, and it needs a mitigation professional on site to confirm. We have no Saskatoon mitigation price. The nearest sourced figure is a Regina installer's $2,500 to $3,500 for a system, with crawl spaces or multiple suction points costing more. For what it's worth, Saskatoon had the lowest numbers of the four cities in the 2024 Cross-Canada survey, roughly 1 in 6 tested homes at or above 200, but a low city average says nothing about one house.

## What to do if your radon retest came back over 200

Work through it in this order.

1. Look at the manometer and listen for the fan. Check the breaker.
2. Look at the sump and every floor drain.
3. Check the test: 91 days or more, a long-term device, the same spot as the original.
4. If the test is sound and the system is running, call your original installer, or find a C-NRPP certified mitigation professional in the [C-NRPP directory](https://c-nrpp.ca/find-a-professional/).
5. After any change, retest: a short-term check by the professional, then a long-term test in the first heating season.

Health Canada recommends corrective action within 1 year when the average is above 200, so there's time to do this properly. It also says no level is considered risk free, so a result a little under 200 is fine to live with, but lower is better. Opening windows isn't the fix. The source for the post-install steps is Health Canada's [Reduce radon in your home](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/environmental-workplace-health/reports-publications/radiation/reduce-radon-home.html) factsheet.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it normal for radon to still be high after mitigation?

It's not the usual outcome, but it does happen. Health Canada says sub-slab depressurization cuts radon by more than 80% in most homes, which leaves some houses that need adjustment. Often the answer is a change to the existing system, like sealing a sump or adding a suction point, but only a site visit can say.

### How soon after installation should I retest?

Health Canada's current advice is a short-term test by a certified professional once the system is switched on, to confirm it's working. Then do a long-term test during the first heating season, in the same place as your original test. After that, retest every five years.

### Can a dead or switched-off fan cause a high retest?

Yes. Health Canada says the fan has to run continuously, and if the two sides of the manometer are level, it's probably not pulling. Health Canada's 2014 national estimate for a replacement fan is $200 to $300.

### Does a retest over 200 mean my home is unsafe?

The 200 Bq/m3 figure is a guideline for the annual average, not a line between safe and unsafe. Health Canada says no level is risk free, and recommends corrective action within a year when the average is above 200. A short test or a monitor reading can't tell you the annual average; a long-term test can.
