# C-NRPP Certification: What It Means When You Hire a Radon Professional

Your radon number came back high, there are two quotes on the kitchen counter, and both companies say they're "certified." Maybe one is. On the Prairies the certification Health Canada points homeowners to is C-NRPP, and knowing what it covers can decide whether the system works and whether you still qualify for help paying for it.

Here's what the letters mean, why they matter more than you'd expect for a voluntary program, and how to check a name in a couple of minutes.

## What C-NRPP is, in plain terms

C-NRPP is the Canadian National Radon Proficiency Program. It's a Canadian radon certification program run by the Canadian Association of Radon Scientists and Technologists (CARST), with oversight by Health Canada. It trains and certifies individual radon professionals. It doesn't certify houses, and it doesn't certify the websites that advertise them.

There are two separate certifications, and they aren't interchangeable:

- A C-NRPP Measurement Professional is trained to measure radon in homes and larger buildings.
- A C-NRPP Mitigation Professional is trained to bring high radon levels down. In a Prairie basement that usually means sub-slab depressurization: a pipe through the slab, a fan, and a discharge outside.

A person can hold one, both or neither. If someone is going to cut into your basement floor, the one you care about is Mitigation.

A quick word on units, since every result and quote uses them. Radon is measured in becquerels per cubic metre, written Bq/m3. Health Canada's guideline is 200 Bq/m3, averaged over a year in the part of the home you actually use: a finished basement, a basement bedroom, a rec room. If you've been reading American sites, 37 Bq/m3 equals 1 pCi/L, so 200 Bq/m3 is about 5.4 pCi/L.

## C-NRPP is voluntary, and that's the catch

No province regulates radon professionals. Health Canada says so directly: it recommends C-NRPP certified professionals, but "certification remains voluntary." Provinces could regulate the trade, and none have. So in Winnipeg, Regina or Saskatoon, anyone can print a business card that says "radon mitigation."

There's no law pushing homeowners either way, either. The 200 Bq/m3 figure is a guideline, not a legal limit, and neither Manitoba nor Saskatchewan requires you to test or to fix anything. The City of Regina's own advisory puts it plainly: "there is no legislated maximum level or enforceable level."

So why bother? Because Health Canada calls radon the number one cause of lung cancer in non-smokers and estimates it's behind more than 3,000 lung cancer deaths in Canada each year. It's the second leading cause for smokers. And it's common here. Of the Winnipeg homes tested for the 2024 Cross-Canada Survey, 37.4% came in at or above 200 Bq/m3; of the Regina homes tested, 46.6%. Saskatoon was lower, roughly 1 in 6 tested homes. Those are unweighted samples of homes that chose to test, not a census. Still, the survey's own line is that "there are no areas of Canada that are 'radon-free'."

If your house hasn't been tested, testing is the first job, not hiring anyone. A long-term test runs at least 91 days in the heating season, is cheap, and is the only thing that tells you whether you need a mitigation professional at all. ## Where C-NRPP certification changes your money

This is the part people tend to find out too late. Voluntary or not, the two main sources of help with mitigation costs in Manitoba and Saskatchewan both require C-NRPP, and one of them won't pay for work that's already been done.

| What you're after | What it asks for | What it's worth (CAD) |
|---|---|---|
| Lungs Matter grant (Canadian Lung Association; run by the Manitoba Lung Association in Manitoba) | A long-term (90+ day) result above 200 Bq/m3 from a C-NRPP approved test kit, a quote from a C-NRPP certified mitigation professional, and household income below the provincial median | Up to $1,500. Not for work already done. Funding is limited. |
| Manitoba Hydro Home Energy Efficiency Loan | Mitigation performed by a C-NRPP certified contractor | Up to $5,000 over 5 years at 6.2% (O.A.C.), repaid on your energy bill, no income test |
| A long-term test done with a digital monitor | The monitor should be on C-NRPP's approved list | Counts as a long-term measurement rather than a screening |
| Saskatchewan Home Renovation Tax Credit | No C-NRPP requirement stated. Lung Saskatchewan says radon mitigation counts as an eligible expense; confirm with CRA | 10.5% on up to $4,000 of eligible expenses, so up to $420 (seniors up to $525 on $5,000), for expenses from 1 October 2024 |

A few notes on that table. Lung Saskatchewan has offered its own reimbursement in the past, but the program is paused; check its site before you count on it. There is currently no federal radon tax credit that we could find. And the order of operations matters for Lungs Matter: test with an approved kit, get a C-NRPP quote, apply, then install. Hire an uncertified installer first and that door is closed. There's more on the Manitoba side in our [radon mitigation grants guide](/blog/radon-mitigation-grants-manitoba/).

## Three Prairie situations where C-NRPP decides things

These are composites of common situations, not customer stories. The numbers come from published sources.

**A library monitor reading 480 Bq/m3 in a Regina rec room.** The Regina Public Library lends 30 digital radon kits for three weeks, no renewals. A three-week reading is a screening, not a long-term test. At 480 Bq/m3 it's well worth following up, but it isn't the number a grant will accept: Lungs Matter needs a 90+ day result from a C-NRPP approved kit, and homeowner-level digital monitors don't qualify. One kit supplier lists long-term kits at $70.00 before tax, and the Manitoba government puts kits "between $50 and $100". Health Canada's advice above 200 Bq/m3 is to take corrective action within a year, so a 91-day test fits inside that window. If the long-term number confirms it, a Regina installer quotes $2,500 to $3,500, with crawl spaces or multiple suction points costing more.

**A 1962 bungalow in St. Vital with a finished basement bedroom.** The long-term test comes back over 200 Bq/m3 and there are two quotes: one from a C-NRPP Mitigation Professional, one from a general contractor who's cheaper and not in the directory. Winnipeg installers advertise systems from about $1,500 to $3,000. If the family wants to spread the cost over five years on the Manitoba Hydro bill, the choice is already made, because the loan only covers a C-NRPP certified contractor. If the basement has a sump pit, it has to be sealed for the system to work, so check that the quote says so.

**A 2015 Winnipeg build with a capped radon rough-in that was never activated.** Manitoba put the National Building Code's radon rough-in into force on 1 April 2011, and homes built under it should have a rough-in: commonly a 100 mm pipe below the basement floor, sealed and labelled. But a rough-in "does not actively reduce radon levels." A fan has to be added. A 2013 study of Winnipeg Parade of Homes houses found rough-ins in all but one, and "almost half are in inaccessible locations." Whether yours is usable is a site question for a mitigation professional, and what it costs to finish depends on where the pipe is. There's no published price for this, so a certified professional will quote it after looking. Test first, though. A rough-in doesn't mean you have a problem.

## How to check a C-NRPP professional before you sign

Checking takes a few minutes and costs nothing. C-NRPP itself tells homeowners that seeing its logo on a company's website isn't enough: double-check the certification before you hire.

1. **Get a person's name, not just a company name.** Certification belongs to the individual who does the work, not the truck or the website.
2. **Look them up in the [C-NRPP Find a Professional directory](https://c-nrpp.ca/find-a-professional/).** It lists certified professionals near you for measurement or mitigation. The Manitoba Lung Association lists C-NRPP's phone number as 1-800-269-4174 if you'd rather call.
3. **Match the certification to the job.** Measurement for testing, Mitigation for installing. A Measurement Professional isn't certified to design your system.
4. **Read the quote for specifics.** Where the suction point goes, where the fan sits, where the pipe discharges outside, whether a sump is sealed, and when the follow-up test happens. Our [radon mitigation quote checklist](/blog/radon-mitigation-quote-checklist/) goes through it line by line.
5. **Plan the retest.** Health Canada says to test again after a system goes in. Certification doesn't guarantee a particular final number; the retest is how you find out what you got.

A couple of things C-NRPP doesn't do. It doesn't set prices, which vary: one Winnipeg firm offers a free 15-minute screening test and another charges $50 for an in-person consultation. It isn't Health Canada approval of any company, either. Health Canada oversees the program and [recommends a certified mitigation professional](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/health-risks-safety/radiation/radon/reducing-levels-home.html); that's a recommendation about who to hire, not an endorsement of a business. And be wary of anyone who treats one reading as a verdict on your home. Without a proper test, nobody can tell you your house is safe or unsafe.

Fans, pipes and sealing are not complicated technology. What you're paying a certified professional for is judgement about your particular slab, soil and layout, plus a system that the grant and loan programs will recognize.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is C-NRPP certification required by law in Manitoba or Saskatchewan?

No. Health Canada recommends C-NRPP certified professionals, but certification is voluntary and no province regulates radon professionals. In practice it's required if you want the Lungs Matter grant or the Manitoba Hydro loan, both of which need C-NRPP work.

### Is Prairie Radon Co C-NRPP certified?

No. We're an advertising service that connects homeowners with independent C-NRPP certified radon professionals. Certification belongs to the individual professional who quotes and does your job, and you can check their name in the C-NRPP directory before you agree to anything.

### Can I test for radon myself without a C-NRPP professional?

Yes. A long-term kit placed in the lowest lived-in level, at least 30 cm above the floor and 30 cm from walls, for at least 91 days in the heating season, gives you a real answer. If you might apply for Lungs Matter, use a C-NRPP approved long-term kit, because homeowner-level digital monitors don't qualify.

### Do I need a C-NRPP test when buying or selling a house?

We found no Manitoba or Saskatchewan rule requiring radon testing or disclosure at sale. C-NRPP suggests a short-term screening can give some insight during a sale, followed by a long-term test after you move in. For radon conditions, holdbacks or disclosure, talk to your realtor or lawyer.
