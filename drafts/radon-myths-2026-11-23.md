# Radon Myths on the Prairies: 8 Common Ones, Answered

Radon has been around as a house problem for a long time, and a lot of folklore has built up around it. Some of it is harmless. Some of it keeps people from testing a house that needs a fix.

Here are eight claims you'll hear at the hardware store, on a real estate walkthrough or across the fence, checked against Health Canada and the 2024 Cross-Canada Survey on Radon.

## Myth 1: "My house is new, so it's fine"

New homes in Manitoba and Saskatchewan do have a feature older ones lack: a radon rough-in. The National Building Code added it in 2010, and Manitoba adopted that code on 1 April 2011. Usually it's a 100 mm pipe below the basement floor, sealed and labelled.

The catch is in the City of Regina's summary of the code: "the depressurization rough-in does not actively reduce radon levels." It's a capped pipe until someone adds a fan. Health Canada recommends that all new homes include a passive soil depressurization system, which "can reduce radon levels by 40-90%", and says to test a new home in its first heating season and again 2 years after the year of construction.

Compliance is a separate worry. The Made-in-Manitoba Radon Action Plan says the rough-in is "still not being implemented correctly in all new builds as of March 2025." A 2013 Winnipeg study found rough-in pipes in all but one house it checked, with almost half in inaccessible locations. New doesn't mean tested. For the detail, see our post on [radon rough-ins in new homes](/blog/radon-rough-in-new-homes/).

## Myth 2: "I don't have a basement problem, so radon isn't my problem"

Radon enters through the foundation, wherever it is. Health Canada lists cracks in the floor and walls, construction joints, gaps around service pipes, support posts, window casements, floor drains, sumps and cavities inside walls. A house without a finished basement still has a foundation.

Health Canada's testing advice is to measure in the lowest lived-in level, and to test every home "no matter where it is located, the type of building, or if it already has a mitigation system." Mitigation also adapts to the foundation: Health Canada says installers adjust a system whether you have a slab, a crawl space or a sump pit. Our post on [how radon gets into your house](/blog/how-radon-gets-into-your-house/) covers the entry points.

## Myth 3: "A quick test from a hardware store tells me what I need to know"

A short test tells you what the air was like for a few days. The Canadian guideline of 200 Bq/m³ applies to the average annual level, and Health Canada says "short-term measurements are not appropriate for informing mitigation decisions." The minimum test length is 91 days, with those days falling in the heating season.

Short tests aren't useless. A library monitor is a screening tool, and Health Canada does endorse a short-term test by a certified professional right after a mitigation system is switched on. But as the main test, they fall short. A long-term kit from the provincial lung associations' HomeRadonTest.ca is about $70 before tax. For the comparison, read [long-term vs short-term radon tests](/blog/long-term-vs-short-term-radon-test/).

## Myth 4: "I'll just seal the cracks"

Sealing is a sensible step. It isn't a fix. Health Canada's reduction guide says sealing alone "is not a standalone technique for reducing radon levels," and a newer factsheet says cracks, gaps around pipes and other entry points "should be sealed to maximize the efficacy of a ventilation system."

So sealing supports a system. The Manitoba government also says caulking cracks and sealing around pipes and drains helps prevent radon getting in, which is true as far as it goes. What actually draws gas out from under the slab is a pipe and a fan. Our posts on [sealing a sump pit](/blog/radon-sump-pit-sealing/) and [how a mitigation system works](/blog/how-a-radon-mitigation-system-works/) cover where each fits.

## Myth 5: "Saskatoon is safe" (or Winnipeg, or any city)

Saskatoon does have the lowest numbers of the four cities in the 2024 survey. About 17.8% of the tested homes were at or over 200 Bq/m³, roughly 1 in 6, and the highest reading was 820 Bq/m³. That's still a real share of homes, and the survey states there are no areas of Canada that are "radon-free."

Cities in the survey are unweighted volunteer samples, so the percentages are a rough sense of scale, not a forecast for your street. The same survey puts Winnipeg at 37.4%, Brandon at 36.1% (a small sample) and Regina at 46.6%. Whatever your city, your own house is the only reading that counts. See [why a neighbour's result doesn't predict yours](/blog/neighbour-radon-test-results/).

## Myth 6: "Radon is only a worry for smokers"

It's close to the reverse. Health Canada says "radon is the #1 cause of lung cancer in non-smokers" and that radon exposure is linked to approximately 16% of lung cancer deaths in Canada. Smokers who are exposed to radon face an even higher risk.

The Government of Manitoba reproduces a Health Canada table of lifetime probability at 70 years' exposure. For a non-smoker it's 1% at the base level, 2% at 200 Bq/m³ and 5% at 800. For a smoker it's 12%, 17% and 30%. Both groups rise. Health Canada also notes that the risk is long-term, not immediate, and that the longer you're exposed, the greater it gets.

## Myth 7: "Opening windows, or running a fan, handles it"

Neither is a mitigation method. Health Canada's list of ways to reduce radon is built around soil depressurization, with sealing as a support and heat or energy recovery ventilation as a partial option. Opening a window in the afternoon doesn't change an annual average, and Health Canada wants the test to include at least 91 days of the heating season.

Ventilation has a place, but a specific one. Health Canada says balanced ventilation (HRV/ERV) "generally reduce radon levels by only 20% to 50%," and that for larger reductions active soil depressurization is "almost always the recommended approach." If you're curious, our post on [HRVs and radon](/blog/hrv-and-radon/) goes through it.

## Myth 8: "Fixing it is a huge, expensive job"

Cost depends on the house, and we won't pretend it's nothing. Winnipeg installers advertise systems from about $1,500 to $3,000, and a Regina installer quotes $2,500 to $3,500, with complicated jobs higher. Health Canada says radon levels can be reduced by more than 80% "for about the same cost as other common home repairs such as replacing the furnace or air conditioner," and that a system can usually be installed in less than a day.

There's help with the bill. In Manitoba, the Hydro Home Energy Efficiency Loan covers radon mitigation up to $5,000 over 5 years at 6.2% (O.A.C.) through a C-NRPP certified contractor, and Lungs Matter pays up to $1,500 for qualifying households. In Saskatchewan, Lung Saskatchewan says radon mitigation counts as an eligible expense for the provincial Home Renovation Tax Credit; confirm that with CRA. ## Bonus myth: "Once it's fixed, it's done"

A system works only while the fan runs. Health Canada says the fan should never be turned off, a fan's lifespan can vary from five to ten or more years, and a replacement was put at $200 to $300 in its 2014 guide. After installation, Health Canada's steps are a short-term test by a certified professional, a long-term test in the first heating season, and a retest every five years. ## What to do with all this

Test your own house with a long-term kit, in the lowest lived-in level, for at least 91 days in the heating season. If the result is above 200 Bq/m³, Health Canada recommends corrective action within 1 year, sooner for higher levels. Our [radon testing page](/radon-testing/) is the place to start.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is radon a real health risk or just hype?

It's real. Health Canada says radon is the number one cause of lung cancer in non-smokers, and that about 16% of lung cancers are estimated to come from radon exposure. The risk is long-term rather than immediate, and testing is how you find out your own level.

### Do new homes in Manitoba and Saskatchewan need a radon test?

Yes. A new home may have a rough-in pipe, but as the City of Regina's code summary puts it, the rough-in does not actively reduce radon levels. Health Canada recommends testing in the first heating season after you move in, and again 2 years after the year of construction.

### Can sealing cracks bring my radon level under 200?

Possibly it helps, but no one can promise it. Health Canada says sealing alone is not a standalone technique for reducing radon levels. A long-term retest after any work is the only way to know what changed.

### Do I have to fix radon if my test is high?

No law in Manitoba or Saskatchewan that we found requires a homeowner to test or fix radon. The Canadian guideline is advice, and Health Canada recommends taking corrective action within 1 year if the level is above 200 Bq/m³. The decision is yours.
