# Radon in Rural Homes and Acreages on the Prairies

Living outside the city doesn't get you out of the radon question. If anything, the best Prairie data we have points the other way.

The 2024 Cross-Canada Survey on Radon, run by the Evict Radon National Study with Health Canada, CAREX Canada and the BC Centre for Disease Control, split its Prairie results into urban and rural homes. Urban homes came in at "1 in 6 (15.9%)" at or above the Canadian guideline of 200 Bq/m³. Rural homes came in at "1 in 4 (26.7%)".

That is a gap worth knowing about if you have an acreage, a farmhouse or a house in a small town. It is not a prediction for your house, and this article explains why. It also covers how a rural household can test without a city library or a drive to the lab, and how to find a certified professional when you live well outside the cities this site mostly talks about.

## What the survey says about rural Prairie homes

The survey collected 69,478 unique long-term test results, almost all of them from 2009 to 2024. The Prairie and Northwest Territories region as a whole had "1 in 5 (20.0%)" of households at or above 200 Bq/m³.

Here are the Prairie pieces that matter if you live outside a city.

| Group of Prairie homes | Share at or above 200 Bq/m³ |
|---|---|
| Urban | 15.9% (about 1 in 6) |
| Rural | 26.7% (about 1 in 4) |
| Single-detached houses | 21.4% (about 1 in 5) |
| Semi-detached houses | 16.1% (about 1 in 6) |

Two cautions come with those numbers. These are results from people who chose to test, not a random sample of every house on the Prairies. And the report doesn't explain why rural homes came out higher, so we won't guess. What the table does tell you is that a rural address is not a reason to skip testing.

The single-detached figure also matters. Most acreages and farmhouses are single-detached homes, and that group sat a little above the Prairie average.

The survey also states that "there are no areas of Canada that are 'radon-free'." That includes the quiet corner of a township.

## One neighbour's result won't tell you yours

If a farm down the road tested low, that tells you about that farm. The survey's own city results show how wide the spread can be: in Regina, 46.6% of tested homes were at or above 200 Bq/m³ and the highest reading was 11,333 Bq/m³. Those are unweighted, volunteer-sample figures, but they show how far apart two homes in one city can land.

Health Canada's advice doesn't depend on where you live. It recommends that every home be tested, no matter where it is located or what type of building it is. For more on how soil gas ends up inside, see [how radon gets into your house](/blog/how-radon-gets-into-your-house/).

## Which building to test on a farm property

The guideline covers the average annual radon level in the "normal occupancy area" of a building, which Health Canada defines as any part where a person is likely to spend more than 4 hours a day.

On a rural property that sorts out roughly like this:

- **The main house.** Test the lowest lived-in level. That is the finished basement if you use it, or the main floor if the house has no basement or only a cellar.
- **Basement suite or bunkhouse for family or hired help.** If someone sleeps or spends long hours there, it counts as a normal occupancy area.
- **Unfinished cellar, cold room, furnace room, laundry room or storage space.** These are excluded from the definition. Don't put the detector in them.
- **A crawl space.** Also excluded as a place to measure. The post on [radon in crawl spaces](/blog/radon-crawl-space/) covers what that means for a house built on one.
- **A shop or office where someone works.** The guideline also applies to workplaces with occupancy of more than 4 hours a day. A farm shop where nobody spends half a day isn't in that category. A home office used most of the day is.

If your house has no basement at all, you still test. You put the detector in the lowest level people actually use.

## Getting a test when the library is a long drive away

City libraries get most of the attention for free radon monitors, but the lending programme reaches further out than people assume. A representative of C-NRPP and CARST told CBC in October 2025 that 31 regional libraries across Manitoba offer the devices in more than 60 branches. In Saskatchewan, Lung Saskatchewan says it supports eight libraries and library regions with radon lending programs.

Two examples near the cities this site covers:

- The Western Manitoba Regional Library (Brandon, Carberry, Glenboro, Hartney, Neepawa) is a partner library in the Manitoba Lung Association's lending programme. Loan terms weren't published on the pages we read, so ask the branch.
- Moose Jaw Public Library, part of Palliser Regional Library, lends kits from 1 October to 1 April, free with a library card.

Check with your own branch before you count on it. Borrowing rules and seasons differ, and some programmes limit loans to residents of a particular town. The [post on borrowing a radon detector from the library](/blog/borrow-radon-detector-library/) has the details we've been able to verify.

A borrowed digital monitor is a screening tool. Health Canada says "short-term measurements are not appropriate for informing mitigation decisions." It tells you whether a proper test is worth doing soon. It doesn't give you the long-term average that the guideline refers to.

## Long-term test kits by mail

For a long-term test you don't need a lab nearby. The provincial lung associations route kit orders to HomeRadonTest.ca. The checkout price we saw was $70.00 per kit before tax, and the site lists the all-inclusive price as covering the test, shipping to and from the lab within Canada, email reminders and the analysis. Kits are analysed at the Saskatchewan Research Council lab.

For comparison, the Manitoba government says retail kits generally run between $50 and $100.

Shipping both ways is a real advantage when your nearest post office is a few kilometres off and your nearest city is an hour away. You order online, the kit arrives with the mail, and it goes back the same way. Our step-by-step guide to [using a long-term radon test kit](/blog/how-to-use-long-term-radon-test-kit/) goes through placement, timing and what to do with the result.

If you want the plain version: put the kit in the lowest lived-in level, leave it for at least 91 days during the heating season, and mail it back.

## Mitigation outside the city

If your long-term result comes back above 200 Bq/m³, Health Canada recommends taking corrective action within 1 year, and sooner for higher levels. That timing is the same on a farm as in a subdivision.

Here are the limits of what we can tell you.

**Prices.** We found advertised prices from Winnipeg installers (roughly $1,500 to $3,000) and a Regina installer ($2,500 to $3,500). We found no published price for Brandon, Saskatoon, Moose Jaw, Prince Albert or any rural area. Whether a company charges for travel to an acreage is something to ask, not something we can state. For what it's worth, one Winnipeg company advertises its $50 in-person consultation as a price for homes inside the city's perimeter.

**Crawl spaces.** The Regina installer says systems with a crawl space or more than one suction point could cost much more. Expect the professional to look at the whole foundation before quoting.

**Reduction.** Health Canada says that a properly installed active system in most homes will reduce the radon level by more than 80%, and its September 2026 factsheet puts typical reductions at 90% or more. Our [guide to how a mitigation system works](/blog/how-a-radon-mitigation-system-works/) explains the pipe, the fan and where it vents.

**Money.** Manitoba households may qualify for a Manitoba Hydro Home Energy Efficiency Loan: up to $5,000 for radon mitigation over 5 years at 6.2% (O.A.C.), paid on the energy bill, with a C-NRPP certified contractor. Check eligibility for your own address and account. On the Saskatchewan side, Lung Saskatchewan says radon mitigation counts as an eligible expense under the provincial Home Renovation Tax Credit; confirm with CRA. Lung Saskatchewan's Caring Breaths reimbursement is currently paused, so check its site.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are rural homes really more likely to have high radon?

In the 2024 Cross-Canada Survey, 26.7% of rural Prairie homes tested at or above 200 Bq/m³, compared with 15.9% of urban ones. Those results come from people who chose to test, so they aren't a random sample. The report doesn't explain the gap. The practical point is that no type of address is safe to assume.

### Can I test my acreage house if there's no library near me?

Yes. Long-term kits are mailed out and mailed back. HomeRadonTest.ca, the ordering site used by both provincial lung associations, listed a single kit at $70.00 before tax, with shipping to and from the lab and the analysis included. A regional library in your area might also lend a digital monitor, but that is a screening tool, not a long-term result.

### Do I need to test the barn, shop or garage?

The Canadian guideline applies to the normal occupancy area of a building, meaning any part where a person is likely to spend more than 4 hours a day. A barn or garage that people only pass through doesn't fit that. A basement suite, a finished lower level or a workspace where someone spends long days does.

### What if I live outside every city on this site?

The testing advice is the same: a long-term test of at least 91 days in the lowest lived-in level, ideally in the fall or winter.
