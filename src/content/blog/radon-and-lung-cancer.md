Radon is a health issue because of one disease. Breathing it over many years raises the risk of lung cancer. That's the whole reason Health Canada has a guideline, and the whole reason this site exists.

It's also easy to overstate. The risk is long-term, it depends heavily on whether you smoke, and for most households it can be managed with a test and, if needed, a fix. This article sets out what Health Canada and the provinces say, using their own wording where it matters, and tries not to add drama to numbers that are serious enough already.

## What Health Canada says, as it says it

Health Canada's 2025 factsheet states: "Radon exposure is the number one cause of lung cancer in non-smokers." It estimates that 16% of lung cancers come from radon exposure, which it puts at more than 3,000 lung cancer deaths in Canada each year. It adds that people who smoke and are exposed to radon have an even higher risk.

An older 2014 Health Canada factsheet frames the same issue from the smoking side. It says almost 90% of lung cancer deaths are caused by smoking, and that radon exposure is linked to approximately 16% of lung cancer deaths in Canada and is the second leading cause of lung cancer for smokers.

Those two statements aren't in conflict. Smoking causes most lung cancer. Radon is the leading cause among people who have never smoked, and a significant second cause among people who have. If you see other figures online, check where they come from. We use Health Canada's.

Other credible sources put it differently. Evict Radon, a University of Calgary-led research group, says 1 in 6 Canadian lung cancers are caused by radon exposure. These are estimates, and they come from different studies, so they won't match to the decimal.

## The risk is long-term, not immediate

Health Canada is specific on timing: "The health risk from radon is long-term not immediate. The longer you are exposed to high levels of radon, the greater your risk."

Nothing about a single weekend in a high-radon basement is cause for alarm. The concern is years of breathing elevated levels in the rooms where you spend your time, which is why the guideline uses an annual average in areas where you spend more than 4 hours a day.

Health Canada also says that below the guideline of 200 Bq/m³ the health risk is small, but no level is considered risk-free. The Government of Saskatchewan puts it more bluntly: "there is no safe level of radon." The choice of how much exposure to accept is yours.

## The numbers behind the risk

Health Canada's 2006 Radon Working Group report includes a table of lifetime lung cancer probability, which the Government of Manitoba reproduces. It assumes exposure over 70 years.

| Exposure level | Smoker | Non-smoker |
|---|---|---|
| Base level | 12% | 1% |
| 200 Bq/m³ | 17% | 2% |
| 800 Bq/m³ | 30% | 5% |

Read it in two directions. Going across, smoking matters far more than radon: at base level a smoker's lifetime risk is 12% and a non-smoker's is 1%. Going down, radon adds to whatever your starting point is. At 200 Bq/m³, the guideline level, the table shows a smoker's risk rising from 12% to 17% and a non-smoker's from 1% to 2%. At 800 Bq/m³, which was the guideline before 2007, it shows 30% and 5%.

Health Canada's 2014 Radon Reduction Guide shows the same idea as a graphic: "High radon only" at 1 in 20, and "Smokers + high radon" at 1 in 3 for lifetime lung cancer risk. Take Action on Radon, a public education campaign, gives matching figures. It says a person living with 800 Bq/m³ of exposure has a 1 in 20 risk of developing lung cancer, 1 in 10 smokers with no exposure to high radon will die of lung cancer, and 1 in 3 smokers exposed to high radon will die of lung cancer.

These are population estimates built on assumptions about a lifetime of exposure. They describe groups, not any one person, and the figures from different documents use different scenarios. Don't mix them into a single number for your own household.

## What this means in the Prairies

Lung cancer is a major cause of death here. The Government of Manitoba says over 600 deaths from lung cancer occur in the province each year. In Saskatchewan, Lung Saskatchewan's Effie Kosmas told CBC in 2024 that one in four cancer deaths in the province are lung cancer related. Radon is one contributor among several, so don't read those figures as radon deaths.

On exposure, the 2024 Cross-Canada Survey on Radon found that about 1 in 5 (20.0%) households in the Prairie and Northwest Territories region contain radon at or above 200 Bq/m³. The city figures vary and come from unweighted, volunteer samples, so our post on [radon levels in Manitoba and Saskatchewan](/blog/radon-levels-manitoba-saskatchewan/) gives them with the caveats attached. The takeaway for a homeowner is simple: on the Prairies, a decent share of tested homes are above the guideline, and no one can tell which without a test.

## Reducing the risk: test, then act

The risk isn't a mystery you have to live with. It comes from a measurable gas in your own air, and you can measure it.

1. **Test.** Health Canada recommends a long-term test of at least 3 months, with a minimum of 91 days in the heating season, in the lowest lived-in level of the home. A kit from HomeRadonTest.ca is $70.00 before tax, with lab analysis included. Our post on [long-term and short-term tests](/blog/long-term-vs-short-term-radon-test/) explains why a short test isn't enough to decide on.
2. **Compare to 200 Bq/m³.** If the average annual level is above it, Health Canada recommends corrective action within 1 year. The higher the number, the sooner it should be done.
3. **Fix it if needed.** In most homes a sub-slab depressurization system lowers radon by more than 80%, according to Health Canada's long-standing wording. Its 2026 factsheet says 90% or more. A pipe goes through the floor, and a fan draws the gas out and vents it outdoors. See [how a mitigation system works](/blog/how-a-radon-mitigation-system-works/).
4. **Retest.** Health Canada says to do a short-term test once a system is running, a long-term test during the first heating season after, and another every five years.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does radon cause lung cancer?

Yes. Health Canada says radon exposure is the number one cause of lung cancer in non-smokers and estimates it is linked to about 16% of lung cancer. The risk is long-term: the longer you're exposed to high levels, the greater it is.

### How much radon exposure causes lung cancer?

There's no single threshold. Health Canada's guideline is 200 Bq/m³ as an average annual level, and it says the risk below that is small but no level is risk-free. Its 2006 table shows lifetime lung cancer risk for a non-smoker of 1% at base level, 2% at 200 Bq/m³ and 5% at 800 Bq/m³.

### Are smokers at greater risk from radon?

Yes. Health Canada says people who smoke and are exposed to radon have an even higher risk, and its 2014 graphic shows 1 in 3 for smokers with high radon, against 1 in 20 for high radon alone. In the 2006 table a smoker's lifetime risk rises from 12% at base level to 17% at 200 Bq/m³.

### Can I get lung cancer from radon in my house if I have never smoked?

Yes, which is why Health Canada calls radon the number one cause of lung cancer in non-smokers. It's still a long-term risk tied to years of exposure, and the way to find out your own exposure is a long-term test. Talk to your doctor about any health concerns.

### If I fix my radon level, does the risk go away?

Reducing radon lowers your exposure, and Health Canada says a system typically cuts levels by more than 80%. No level is considered completely risk-free, and Health Canada recommends retesting after installation and every five years.
