Smoking is the bigger risk. Health Canada says almost 90% of lung cancer deaths are caused by smoking, and radon is linked to roughly 16% of them. Those two numbers sit side by side because the risks overlap, and for a smoker living in a house with high radon the overlap is the part worth understanding.

This article looks at what the published figures say about radon and smoking together, how to read the Manitoba risk table, and why a household with a smoker has good reason to put a radon test near the top of the list. It also covers the limits of the numbers, because a few of them get repeated without their context.

## What Health Canada says about radon and smoking

Health Canada's current factsheet calls radon "the number one cause of lung cancer in non-smokers" and says people who smoke and are exposed to radon "have an even higher risk of lung cancer." Its older factsheet on the subject, titled *Radon: Another Reason to Quit* (2014), goes a step further. It says radon is the second leading cause of lung cancer for smokers.

So radon is not a smokers' problem or a non-smokers' problem. It affects both groups, and the effects stack.

Two things are worth keeping straight. Tobacco does most of the damage: almost 90% of lung cancer deaths are caused by smoking. And radon is the part you can change in the house. You can test the air in your basement and, if the number is high, have a system installed that Health Canada says in most homes cuts the level by more than 80%.

The health risk from radon is long-term, not immediate. Health Canada puts it this way: "The longer you are exposed to high levels of radon, the greater your risk." That matters for a smoking household. Years of exposure accumulate, and so does the time to do something about it.

## The 1 in 3 figure and where it comes from

You will see "1 in 3" attached to smokers and radon. The number is real, but it needs its context.

Health Canada's 2014 *Radon Reduction Guide for Canadians* shows lifetime lung cancer risk in a graphic. For someone exposed to high radon who does not smoke, it reads "1 in 20." For smokers exposed to high radon, it reads "1 in 3."

Take Action on Radon, a national awareness campaign, words it differently:

- A person who lives with an exposure of 800 Bq/m³ has a 1 in 20 risk of developing lung cancer.
- 1 in 10 people who smoke and have no exposure to high radon will die of lung cancer.
- 1 in 3 smokers exposed to high radon will die of lung cancer.

Notice that the 800 Bq/m³ in that first line is four times the Canadian guideline of 200 Bq/m³. Neither source says these odds apply to a house that tests just over 200. These are lifetime risks across a population, not a prediction for any one person. They describe how the odds move, and they move a lot when smoke and radon share a house.

Take the figures for what they are: attributed numbers from Health Canada and Take Action on Radon. The Health Canada graphic dates from 2014, so we won't dress it up as new.

## Reading the Manitoba risk table

The Government of Manitoba reproduces a table from Health Canada's 2006 radon working group report. It shows the lifetime probability of lung cancer after 70 years of exposure at three levels.

| Exposure level | Smoker | Non-smoker |
|---|---|---|
| Base (background) level | 12% | 1% |
| 200 Bq/m³ (the guideline) | 17% | 2% |
| 800 Bq/m³ (four times the guideline) | 30% | 5% |

A few ways to read this honestly.

**Smoking dominates the baseline.** A smoker at the background level has a 12% lifetime probability, higher than a non-smoker living at 800 Bq/m³ (5%). If the question is which habit matters more, the table answers it.

**Radon adds a bigger absolute amount for a smoker.** Going from the base level to 800 Bq/m³ adds 18 percentage points for a smoker (12% to 30%) and 4 points for a non-smoker (1% to 5%). The same radon exposure does more harm where the lungs are already under strain.

**Even the guideline level isn't zero.** At 200 Bq/m³, the smoker's figure is 17% and the non-smoker's is 2%. Health Canada says the risk below the guideline is small but that "there is no level that is considered risk free." The Government of Saskatchewan says there is no safe level of radon. The guideline is a line where Health Canada recommends action, not a boundary between safe and unsafe air.

The table is a 2006 model built on 70 years of exposure. Treat it as a way to understand scale, not as a personal forecast.

## Why a household with a smoker should test first

Health Canada recommends that every home be tested, wherever it is and whatever it's built like. That includes houses with no smoker in them. So the case for a smoking household isn't that everyone else can skip it. It's about order of operations.

If you smoke, or someone you live with does, the combined risk is the highest the published numbers describe. A long-term test is one of the cheapest things you can do about it. The Lung Saskatchewan and Manitoba Lung Association kits sold through HomeRadonTest.ca cost $70.00 before tax, and the Manitoba government says retail kits generally run between $50 and $100.

A few practical points for a smoking household:

1. **Test the level you actually live in.** Health Canada says to measure in the normal occupancy area of the lowest lived-in level, meaning any space where someone is likely to spend more than 4 hours a day. A finished basement, a basement bedroom or a basement suite counts. A furnace room doesn't.
2. **Use a long-term test.** The guideline applies to the average annual level, so Health Canada recommends a test of at least 3 months, with a minimum of 91 days during the heating season. A short-term test is a screening tool and, in Health Canada's words, isn't appropriate for informing mitigation decisions. Our post on [long-term vs short-term radon tests](/blog/long-term-vs-short-term-radon-test/) explains why.
3. **Put the detector where it will give a clean reading.** At least 30 cm above the floor and 30 cm from walls and ceilings, away from heat, sunlight, vents, doors, windows and sump holes. The post on [where to place a radon detector](/blog/where-to-place-radon-detector/) has more.
4. **Don't count a library monitor as the final answer.** Libraries in Winnipeg and Regina lend digital monitors. They are a decent first look, but a long-term test is what Health Canada's advice is built on.

If your household has someone who has been diagnosed with lung cancer, that matters for grants. The Manitoba Lung Association's Lungs Matter program lists Manitoba residents diagnosed with lung cancer among those who can apply for up to $1,500 towards mitigation. The program also needs a long-term C-NRPP approved test kit result and has limited funding.

## What a result tells you

Health Canada's guideline is 200 Bq/m³. If your long-term result is above it, Health Canada recommends taking corrective action within 1 year. The higher the number, the sooner it should happen. If you're under 200, the advice from C-NRPP is to retest within 5 years or after major renovations.

The Manitoba table shows risk rising with the level, and Health Canada says there's no risk-free number, so a result just under 200 is your call to weigh. The post on [what radon level needs mitigation](/blog/what-radon-level-needs-mitigation/) walks through the decision.

## What a fix involves and what it costs

Health Canada's factsheet describes the usual method. A pipe goes through the foundation floor, and a fan attached to it runs around the clock, pulling radon from under the house and venting it outdoors. It says a system can usually be installed in less than a day, and that in most homes this kind of system reduces radon by more than 80%. The 2026 factsheet says 90% or more.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does radon only matter if you smoke?

No. Health Canada says radon is the number one cause of lung cancer in non-smokers. Smoking raises the stakes, since people who smoke and are exposed to radon have an even higher risk, but a non-smoking household still has a reason to test.

### If I smoke outside, does my house still need a radon test?

Yes. Radon comes from the soil under and around the foundation, not from tobacco. Where you smoke doesn't change what the ground is doing. Health Canada recommends that every home be tested, and the only way to know your level is a long-term test of at least 91 days.

### What does the 1 in 3 figure for smokers mean?

It comes from a Health Canada graphic in its 2014 reduction guide, which shows lifetime lung cancer risk for smokers exposed to high radon as 1 in 3, compared with 1 in 20 for high radon alone. Take Action on Radon puts the same idea as 1 in 3 smokers exposed to high radon dying of lung cancer. These are population figures, not a prediction for one household.

### Will fixing radon make up for smoking?

No. Almost 90% of lung cancer deaths are caused by smoking, according to Health Canada, and radon is linked to about 16%. Reducing radon removes one source of risk. It doesn't cancel the other.
