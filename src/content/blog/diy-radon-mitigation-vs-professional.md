Winnipeg installers advertise radon systems from about $1,500 to $3,000, and a Regina installer quotes $2,500 to $3,500. It's a pipe, a fan and some sealant, so plenty of handy Prairie homeowners look at that number and wonder whether DIY radon mitigation would do the same job for less.

Sometimes it can. More often the savings shrink once you notice that DIY rules you out of the grant and the loan available here, and you still need testing afterwards to know it worked.

First, the units. Radon is measured in becquerels per cubic metre (Bq/m3). Health Canada's guideline is 200 Bq/m3, averaged over a year, in the part of the house someone uses four or more hours a day, like a finished basement. That's roughly 5.4 pCi/L if you're reading American material.

It's a guideline, not a law. Neither Manitoba nor Saskatchewan requires homeowners to test or fix anything.

Why take it seriously at all? [Health Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/environmental-workplace-health/reports-publications/radon-what-you-need-to-know.html) says radon is the number one cause of lung cancer in non-smokers and the second leading cause for smokers. It links radon to more than 3,000 lung cancer deaths in Canada each year. That's why a system you install yourself has to actually work, and only a test tells you.

## Can you do radon mitigation yourself in Manitoba or Saskatchewan?

We found no Manitoba or Saskatchewan rule that stops a homeowner from putting in their own system. Radon work itself is not a regulated trade here. Health Canada recommends a contractor certified through the Canadian National Radon Proficiency Program (C-NRPP), but certification is voluntary, and no province has chosen to regulate radon professionals.

Other parts of the job are ordinary renovation work, though. Wiring a fan and cutting through an exterior wall can bring in electrical and building rules, and those vary. Check with your municipality before you start, and use a licensed electrician for any new circuit.

## What a DIY radon mitigation system actually involves

The standard method, and the one Health Canada describes, is sub-slab depressurization, also called active soil depressurization. There's more detail in [how a radon mitigation system works](/blog/how-a-radon-mitigation-system-works/), but the short version runs like this.

- **A suction point.** One, or sometimes several, holes go through the basement slab into the crushed rock or soil below. Cutting through a concrete floor cleanly takes a core drill, which most homeowners have to rent.
- **Pipe and fan.** Pipe runs from that hole to a fan that pulls air from under the slab. Health Canada says an indoor fan is usually vented sideways through the rim joist near ground level, while a fan in a garage or attic is usually vented up above the roof.
- **Sealing.** The sump pit has to be capped and sealed. If a floor drain connects to the sump, it needs a trap seal. Cracks and gaps around pipes should be sealed so the fan isn't pulling basement air.
- **A manometer.** A U-tube gauge on the pipe shows whether the fan is creating suction. The fan must run continuously.
- **Retesting.** Health Canada's current advice is a short-term test by a certified professional once the system is switched on, then a long-term test in the same spot during the first heating season, then retesting every five years.

On paper, that's a weekend. Health Canada says a system can usually be installed in less than a day, though that assumes someone who has done it before. The plumbing is the easy bit. The judgement calls in between are where DIY jobs come unstuck.

Two of them matter more on the Prairies than most places.

First, cold. Health Canada warns that a fan and pipe outside the living space, in a garage or attic, will cool in winter, which can lead to condensation and ice that can damage the fan. Keeping the fan indoors with a short exhaust near ground level reduces that.

Second, backdrafting. A system has to be checked so it doesn't pull exhaust back down the chimney from a furnace, water heater, fireplace or wood stove. That check is easy to skip and not something to guess at.

## What you can safely do yourself

DIY radon mitigation isn't all or nothing. Some of the work suits a homeowner fine.

- **Test properly.** Place a long-term detector in the lowest lived-in level, in the breathing zone at least 30 cm above the floor and 30 cm from walls, away from sumps, vents, windows and heat. Leave it at least 91 days in the heating season. Our guide on [where to put the detector](/blog/where-to-place-radon-detector/) has the details.
- **Screen for free.** Winnipeg Public Library branches lend digital monitors from 1 November to 30 April on three-week loans. Moose Jaw Public Library lends them from 1 October to 1 April, free with a card, and Saskatoon libraries have lent detectors too. Treat any result as a reason to run a long-term test, not as the answer.
- **Seal the obvious gaps.** The Manitoba government says caulking cracks and sealing around pipes and drains helps keep radon out, and special floor drain traps can help too. Health Canada is clear that sealing on its own is not a way to reduce radon. It helps a system work better. It doesn't replace one.
- **Watch the manometer.** If you already have a system, glance at the U-tube gauge from time to time. A level difference between the two sides means the fan is pulling. If the sides read level, have the system checked.

What you shouldn't do is treat an open window or a basement fan as the fix. Neither is a radon reduction method. When a big reduction is needed, Health Canada says active soil depressurization is almost always the recommended approach.

So where does that leave you? DIY can work for a confident homeowner with an accessible rough-in, a simple slab and no grant on the table. For most families with a finished basement, the gap narrows once the grant, the loan and the post-install test are counted.

Health Canada says a system reduces radon by more than 80% in most homes, and only a retest tells you whether yours is one of them. Before you decide, read up on [what C-NRPP certification means](/blog/what-is-c-nrpp-certification/) and see our [radon mitigation page](/radon-mitigation/).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is DIY radon mitigation legal in Manitoba and Saskatchewan?

We found no Manitoba or Saskatchewan rule that stops a homeowner from installing their own radon system, and no province regulates radon professionals. Electrical work and cutting through exterior walls can fall under ordinary building and electrical rules, so check with your municipality first. Health Canada recommends a C-NRPP certified mitigation professional.

### Can I get the Lungs Matter grant or the Manitoba Hydro loan for a DIY system?

No. Lungs Matter needs a long-term C-NRPP test above 200 Bq/m3 and a quote from a C-NRPP professional, and it doesn't cover work that's already done. The Manitoba Hydro Home Energy Efficiency Loan requires a C-NRPP certified contractor.

### Is activating an existing radon rough-in a good DIY job?

It can be the most manageable one, because the pipe through the slab is already in place. The rough-in doesn't actively reduce radon until a fan is added, and a 2013 Winnipeg study found almost half were in inaccessible locations. You still need to vent it correctly, protect the fan from the cold, check for backdrafting and retest.

### How do I know if my DIY system is working?

Health Canada recommends a short-term test by a certified professional once the system is switched on, then a long-term test in the same spot during the first heating season. After that, check the manometer from time to time and retest every five years. The manometer only tells you the fan is pulling, not what your radon level is.
