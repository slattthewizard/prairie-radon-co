A long-term radon test is mostly a matter of putting a small device in the right place and then leaving it alone for a few months. The mistakes people make come from the first part, or from not leaving it alone.

This is the whole process in order, with the numbers Health Canada gives for each step. Your kit will come with its own instructions, and where they differ on a detail of the device itself, follow the kit. The timing, location and result thresholds below are the ones that matter for the answer.

## Step 1: Buy the right kind of kit

Health Canada says "it is important that you conduct a long-term test for a minimum of 3 months." Short-term measurements, it says, "are not appropriate for informing mitigation decisions." So you want a long-term kit.

In Manitoba and Saskatchewan, both provincial lung associations send orders to HomeRadonTest.ca. Its checkout showed $70.00 per single-use 90 Day Test, before tax, with the all-inclusive price covering the test, shipping to and from the lab within Canada, email reminders and the analysis. Kits are analysed at the Saskatchewan Research Council lab. The order total we saw for one test was $73.50.

The Manitoba government says retail kits generally run between $50 and $100. If you'd rather use an electronic monitor, Health Canada says to choose one that has passed C-NRPP performance testing for long-term measurements in homes.

A library monitor is a different thing. It's a free screening tool, and a three-week loan won't give you the long-term average the guideline refers to. Our post on [borrowing a radon detector from the library](/blog/borrow-radon-detector-library/) explains where that fits. Also note that the Lungs Matter mitigation grant requires a long-term 90-day C-NRPP approved test kit, and homeowner-level digital monitors don't qualify.

## Step 2: Pick the season

Health Canada says to test "ideally during the fall or winter time," and that "a minimum of 91 days of the test is during the heating season." The Manitoba government says ideally October to April.

Practically, that means a kit started in October runs into January. A kit started in January runs into April. If you start in late spring, a lot of your test falls outside the heating season, and the result won't mean much. Our [post on the best time to test](/blog/best-time-to-test-for-radon/) goes through the calendar.

## Step 3: Choose the room

This is where tests go wrong. Health Canada says all measurements should be made in a normal occupancy area of the lowest lived-in level of the home. That means a space where someone spends more than 4 hours a day.

- A finished basement, a basement bedroom or a basement suite qualifies.
- If you have no basement, use the lowest level people actually live on.
- An unfinished basement, a cold room, a storage area, a furnace room, a laundry room and a crawl space don't count as normal occupancy areas.

If your basement is only used for storage and you live upstairs, the lowest lived-in level is the main floor. Test there.

## Step 4: Place it correctly

Health Canada's guide says to put the detector:

- in the typical breathing zone, at least 30 cm (1 ft) above the floor
- at least 30 cm from walls or ceilings
- away from heat sources, direct sunlight, vents, doors and windows
- away from sump holes, crawl spaces and exposed foundation cracks

The Manitoba Lung Association's page gives different numbers (1 metre from exterior doors and windows, 0.5 metre from exterior walls, 1 metre off the floor). Health Canada's are the ones we publish.

Our guide on [where to place a radon detector](/blog/where-to-place-radon-detector/) has more on room-by-room choices.

## Step 5: Start it and write down the dates

Follow the kit's instructions to start the test. Then write down the start date and the room, and the date you plan to end it. HomeRadonTest.ca sends automated email reminders, but a note on the calendar costs nothing.

Count the days. The minimum test length is 91 days. Health Canada says that for a test shorter than 91 days, "a new test is recommended." It recommends tests over 3 to 12 months to find the average annual level.

If you go beyond the minimum, that's fine. The guideline refers to the average annual level, and a longer test gets you closer to it.

## Step 6: Leave it alone

Don't move the kit, shelve it somewhere else halfway through, or take it upstairs for a party. Leave it in the same place for the full time.

A few household habits worth avoiding for the duration:

- Don't put it near a vent, a window you open often, or a heat source.
- Don't cover it with a cloth or stash it in a cupboard.
- Don't open windows wide and run fans to "help the test." That changes the house you're measuring, and Health Canada doesn't offer open windows or fans as a radon fix either.

If something does change mid-test, such as a renovation, a flood or the detector falling off a shelf, note the date. It may be reason to run a fresh test.

## Step 7: End it and mail it back

When the 91 days (or your chosen length) are up, follow the kit's instructions to close the test and send it to the lab. For HomeRadonTest.ca kits, shipping to the lab is already included in the price. Write the end date on the paperwork or form the kit asks for.

Mail it promptly. We didn't find a published turnaround time for results, so don't plan around a specific number of days.

## Step 8: Read the result

Canadian labs report in Bq/m³ (becquerels per cubic metre). If you've seen US apps or kits quoting pCi/L, 1 pCi/L equals 37 Bq/m³, so 200 Bq/m³ is about 5.4 pCi/L.

Here's what the number means, using Health Canada and C-NRPP wording.

| Result | What Health Canada and C-NRPP say |
|---|---|
| Above 200 Bq/m³ | Mitigate within 1 year; the higher the level, the sooner |
| 0 to 200 Bq/m³ | Retest within 5 years or after major renovations |

The guideline is 200 Bq/m³ for the average annual level. Health Canada adds that "there is no level that is considered risk free," and that the choice about exposure is each homeowner's. The post on [what radon level needs mitigation](/blog/what-radon-level-needs-mitigation/) goes through the in-between cases.

One result is for one location. Health Canada also asks for a retest after energy retrofits, if a floor that wasn't tested becomes the lowest lived-in level, and in a new home 2 years after the year of construction. See our [radon testing page](/radon-testing/) for the full list.

## Step 9: What next

If the result is below 200 Bq/m³, put the retest date in your calendar. Five years is C-NRPP's outer limit.

If it's above 200 Bq/m³, you have a decision, not an emergency. Health Canada recommends corrective action within 1 year. A mitigation system, typically a pipe through the slab with a fan, reduces radon by more than 80% in most homes per Health Canada. Winnipeg installers advertise systems from about $1,500 to $3,000, and a Regina installer quotes $2,500 to $3,500. Those are company advertisements, not a market survey, and we found no price for Brandon, Saskatoon, Moose Jaw or Prince Albert.

Payment help exists. In Manitoba, the Hydro Home Energy Efficiency Loan covers up to $5,000 over 5 years at 6.2% (O.A.C.) with a C-NRPP certified contractor, and Lungs Matter offers up to $1,500 for qualifying households with a long-term C-NRPP test result and a C-NRPP quote. Lung Saskatchewan says mitigation counts under the Saskatchewan Home Renovation Tax Credit; confirm with CRA. Lung Saskatchewan's Caring Breaths reimbursement is paused at the moment.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long do I leave a long-term radon test kit in place?

At least 91 days, with that time falling in the heating season. Health Canada says a test shorter than 91 days calls for a new one, and recommends 3 to 12 months overall to capture the annual average. Ideally start in the fall or winter.

### Where in the house should I put the kit?

In the lowest lived-in level of the home, in a space where someone spends more than 4 hours a day. Put it in the breathing zone, at least 30 cm above the floor and at least 30 cm from walls, away from heat sources, direct sunlight, vents, doors, windows, sump holes, crawl spaces and exposed foundation cracks.

### Can I move the kit part-way through the test?

No. Leave it where you started it for the whole test. If it gets knocked down or the room changes significantly, write down the date, and consider starting over rather than guessing whether the reading still means something.

### What do I do if my result is over 200 Bq/m³?

Health Canada recommends taking corrective action within 1 year, and sooner for higher levels. The usual route is a mitigation professional certified by C-NRPP, and after the system goes in you retest to confirm it worked.
