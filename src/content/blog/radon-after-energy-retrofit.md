You spent real money making the house tighter: new windows, weatherstripping, a thicker layer of insulation in the attic. What nobody on the job mentioned is that Health Canada lists energy retrofits as one of the times you should test for radon again, even if the house tested fine before.

Checking for radon after energy retrofit work is cheap, it takes a winter, and it is the only way to know whether the work you paid for changed the air your family breathes in the basement.

## Why radon after an energy retrofit is worth a new test

Radon is a radioactive gas that comes out of the soil. It gets into a house because, as Health Canada puts it, the air pressure inside your home is usually lower than in the soil around the foundation. So the house pulls soil gas in through cracks in the floor and walls, construction joints, gaps around service pipes, floor drains and sumps.

An energy retrofit changes how a house breathes. Air sealing closes the leaks you were paying to heat. New windows and doors cut the outdoor air that used to wander in. All of that shifts the pressure balance and how much fresh air is mixing with whatever comes up through the slab.

Which way does the radon number move? It depends on the house. It can go up, stay about the same, or even drop if a ventilation system went in with the work. Anyone who calls your house "fine" or "worse" without a test is guessing. Health Canada's own [Guide for radon measurements in homes](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/publications/health-risks-safety/guide-radon-measurements-residential-dwellings.html) keeps it simple: retest after energy retrofits.

Why bother at all? Health Canada's 2025 factsheet says radon is the number one cause of lung cancer in non-smokers, and that it is estimated to cause 16% of lung cancers, more than 3,000 lung cancer deaths in Canada each year. For smokers it is the second leading cause. The practical part: it is easy to measure and usually fixable.

And it is common here. In the 2024 Cross-Canada Survey, about 1 in 3 homes tested in Saskatchewan and Manitoba were at or above 200 Bq/m3. Of the Regina homes tested, 46.6% were at or above that level; of the Winnipeg homes tested, 37.4%. Saskatoon came in lower, at roughly 1 in 6 tested homes. They are unweighted samples: read them as "this is common", not as your odds.

A quick word on the unit. Bq/m3 means becquerels per cubic metre, a count of radioactive decays per second in each cubic metre of air. Health Canada's guideline is 200 Bq/m3 as a yearly average in the part of the house you actually use, which is about 5.4 pCi/L if you are reading American sources. It is a guideline, not a law. Neither Manitoba nor Saskatchewan requires homeowners to test or fix anything.

## What counts as an energy retrofit for radon testing

Health Canada's guide says "energy retrofits" and leaves it there. In practice, treat any job that changed how air gets in and out of the house as a reason to retest. Around here that usually means:

- Replacing windows or exterior doors
- Air sealing, caulking and weatherstripping
- Adding attic insulation or sealing the attic hatch and pot lights
- Insulating basement walls or the rim joist
- A new furnace, water heater or other change to how the house vents
- Adding an HRV or ERV

A second trigger often rides along with retrofit work. Health Canada says to retest when a floor that wasn't tested becomes the lowest lived-in level. If the retrofit was part of finishing the basement, and the kids' bedroom or the rec room is now down there, that alone is a reason to test again.

Already have a radon system? Health Canada says homes with an active system should be retested every five years anyway. A retrofit is a good reason to do it early.

## How to retest for radon after a retrofit

The test that counts is a long-term test. Health Canada says a minimum of 3 months, with at least 91 days of it in the heating season, and 3 to 12 months is ideal. A test shorter than 91 days should be repeated. That rules out a quick one-week check for any decision about spending money. If you want the longer explanation, our post on [long-term vs short-term radon tests](/blog/long-term-vs-short-term-radon-test/) walks through it.

Timing on the Prairies is easy. Most retrofits happen in summer or early fall, and the heating season is when you want the detector running. Finish the work, then start the test in the fall and pull it in the new year.

Placement matters as much as timing:

- Put it in the lowest level someone spends more than 4 hours a day in, like a finished basement bedroom or family room
- Keep it in the breathing zone, at least 30 cm above the floor and at least 30 cm from walls
- Keep it away from the sump, floor drains, vents, windows, doors, heat sources and direct sun
- Don't put it in a crawl space, furnace room or laundry room

Cost is modest. The long-term kit sold through the provincial lung associations' HomeRadonTest.ca is about $70 per kit before tax, with shipping to the lab and the analysis included. The Manitoba government says retail kits generally run between $50 and $100.

Library monitors are a good free screen while you wait. Winnipeg Public Library lends radon monitors from every branch between 1 November and 30 April, on a three-week loan. Regina Public Library has 30 kits on three-week loans with no renewals. Saskatoon libraries have lent detectors too; check with your branch for current terms. A three-week reading is a screening number, though, not a basis for spending money.

Our [radon testing](/radon-testing/) page covers the options in more detail. If you would rather have a professional do the measurement, look for a C-NRPP certified measurement professional. C-NRPP, the Canadian National Radon Proficiency Program, is voluntary, so check the name in its online directory before you hire anyone.

## Three Prairie retrofit scenarios

These are composite examples, not real clients.

**A 1962 bungalow in St. Vital with a finished basement bedroom.** The owners tested years ago with a kit and got a result under 200. This summer they replaced every window and had the attic topped up and sealed. The old result describes a house whose air moved differently. The sensible move is a $70 long-term kit in the basement bedroom from October into the new year. If it comes back high, Winnipeg installers advertise systems from about $1,500 to $3,000. Manitoba Hydro's Home Energy Efficiency Loan covers radon mitigation up to $5,000 over 5 years at 6.2% (O.A.C.), but check how much of your loan room the windows already used, because the loan has a $12,500 limit per residence.

**A Regina split-level where the rec room is getting spray foam and drywall.** The family borrowed a library monitor and it read 480 Bq/m3 in the rec room over three weeks. That's worth taking seriously, but it is a screening reading taken mid-renovation. Two Health Canada triggers apply at once: an energy retrofit, and a room that is about to become lived-in space. Run a long-term test once the room is finished and in use. If it confirms a high level, a Regina installer quotes $2,500 to $3,500, higher for crawl spaces or multiple suction points. It can be worth asking a certified mitigation professional where a pipe could run before the walls close up.

**A Saskatoon two-storey that already has a radon system.** This year it got new windows and an HRV, and the owners assumed the HRV would handle radon on its own. Health Canada says balanced ventilation generally cuts radon by only 20% to 50%, so the existing system is still doing the heavy lifting. Two checks: look at the [manometer on the radon pipe](/blog/radon-manometer-reading/) to confirm the fan is still pulling, then do a long-term test this winter in the same spot as the original test. Health Canada's 2014 national figure for replacing a fan was $200 to $300 if it ever comes to that.

## If the retest comes back over 200 Bq/m3

Health Canada recommends fixing a home above 200 Bq/m3 within one year. Below 200, it says no level is considered risk free.

The standard fix is sub-slab depressurization, also called active soil depressurization. A certified mitigation professional puts a pipe through the basement floor into the gravel or soil underneath, and a fan pulls the soil gas out and vents it outdoors. Health Canada's pages say it reduces radon by more than 80% in most homes. Installers also seal the sump pit and obvious entry points, and a system can usually be installed in less than a day. The layout depends on your slab, sump and pipe route, which a certified professional has to see.

Opening windows or running a basement fan is not a fix. In a house you just tightened up, it also undoes the retrofit.

## Paying for radon work after retrofit spending

In Manitoba, the Manitoba Hydro Home Energy Efficiency Loan covers radon mitigation up to $5,000, over 5 years at 6.2% (O.A.C.), paid on your energy bill with no income test. The contractor must be C-NRPP certified. Because radon sits under the same $12,500 per-residence limit as other energy work, anyone who financed windows or insulation through Hydro should ask how much room is left. The details are on [Manitoba Hydro's loan page](https://www.hydro.mb.ca/account/loans/home-energy-efficiency-loan/).

The Canadian Lung Association's Lungs Matter program offers up to $1,500 to low- or moderate-income households (income below the provincial median). You need a 90+ day C-NRPP approved test above 200 and a quote from a C-NRPP certified professional, and it does not cover work already done. Funding is limited. In Manitoba the Manitoba Lung Association runs it, and a lung cancer diagnosis also qualifies. In Saskatchewan, Lung Saskatchewan has offered reimbursement in the past; check its site, because the program is paused right now. Lung Saskatchewan says radon mitigation counts as an eligible expense; confirm with CRA. There is currently no federal radon tax credit that we could find.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do new windows increase radon in a house?

They can, but not always. New windows and air sealing change how air moves through the house, and that can push the radon level up, leave it about the same, or occasionally lower it. The only way to know for your house is a long-term test of at least 91 days in the heating season after the work is done.

### How soon after a retrofit should I test for radon?

Once the work is finished and the house is closed up for the season. On the Prairies that usually means starting a long-term test in the fall and ending it in the new year, so at least 91 days fall in the heating season. A test during construction won't reflect how the house behaves now.

### Will an HRV fix a high radon level?

Health Canada says balanced ventilation generally cuts radon by only 20% to 50%. That can help in a home that is a little over the guideline, but it is not the standard fix for a high result. Sub-slab depressurization reduces radon by more than 80% in most homes, according to Health Canada.

### I tested before the renovation and it was fine. Do I really need to test again?

Health Canada says yes: retest after energy retrofits, and again if a floor that wasn't tested becomes the lowest lived-in level. Your earlier result describes the house before the work. A long-term kit is about $70, which is small next to what the retrofit cost.
