Two radon mitigation quotes for the same basement can land a long way apart, and the cheaper one isn't always the better deal. Before you sign either, you want to know what each one includes, who will actually do the work, and whether the test that started all this was the right kind of test.

This is a checklist for Winnipeg, Regina and Saskatoon homeowners who are holding a radon mitigation quote or about to ask for one. It covers the questions worth asking, what Prairie installers advertise, and the funding rules that can cost you money if you sign too early.

One line on why this matters, then we'll get practical. Health Canada says radon is the number one cause of lung cancer in non-smokers, linked to more than 3,000 lung cancer deaths in Canada each year, and the second leading cause for smokers. That's a good reason to fix a high result. It isn't a reason to rush a contract.

## Before you ask for a radon mitigation quote, check the test behind it

A quote is only as sound as the number it's based on. Health Canada's guideline is 200 Bq/m3, which stands for becquerels per cubic metre, a measure of radioactivity in the air. (The US uses pCi/L; 200 Bq/m3 works out to about 5.4 pCi/L.) The guideline applies to the average annual level in the part of the house you use four or more hours a day, like a finished basement, not the furnace room or a crawl space.

The test that decision should rest on is a long-term one: at least 91 days in the heating season, ideally 3 to 12 months, in the lowest lived-in level. Short-term tests and digital monitors are screening tools. They tell you to look harder. They aren't what you should spend a few thousand dollars on.

A few things about that 200 figure. It's a guideline, not a law, and no Manitoba or Saskatchewan rule requires you to test or fix your home. Above 200, Health Canada recommends corrective action within a year, so there's time to test properly and compare quotes without panic. Below 200, Health Canada says no level is considered risk free, which makes it your call rather than anyone's sales pitch.

**Picture a library monitor in a Regina rec room.** A family borrows a digital monitor from the Regina Public Library (three-week loan, no renewals) and it reads 480 Bq/m3 in the basement rec room. The instinct is to call an installer that week. The better move is to put a long-term detector down first. The Manitoba government puts kits at between $50 and $100, and one online seller lists them at $70.00 per kit before tax. If the long-term result does come back high, you'll hold the kind of test the grant programs ask for, which a three-week screening reading is not.

## Who does the work, and are they certified for mitigation?

No province regulates radon professionals. Certification is voluntary, through the Canadian National Radon Proficiency Program (C-NRPP), which is run by CARST with Health Canada oversight. Health Canada recommends a C-NRPP certified mitigation professional for this job.

Two things trip people up. First, C-NRPP certifies measurement professionals and mitigation professionals separately, so someone certified to test isn't automatically certified to install. Second, certification belongs to a person, not a truck or a website. Ask for the name of the certified professional on your job and look them up yourself in the [C-NRPP professional directory](https://c-nrpp.ca/find-a-professional/). It takes two minutes.

This isn't only about workmanship. The Lungs Matter grant and the Manitoba Hydro loan both require C-NRPP certification, so an uncertified installer can cost you access to money. There's more on this in our guide to [what C-NRPP certification means](/blog/what-is-c-nrpp-certification/).

## What a good radon mitigation quote spells out

Most Prairie homes with a basement end up with the same basic system: sub-slab depressurization, also called active soil depressurization. A pipe goes through the basement slab, a fan draws soil gas from underneath, and the pipe carries it outside. Health Canada's page on [reducing radon in your home](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/health-risks-safety/radiation/radon/reducing-levels-home.html) covers the method, and our [radon mitigation page](/radon-mitigation/) walks through it for Prairie houses.

The idea is simple. The details depend on your house, and that's where quotes differ. Put yours side by side and check each line:

| Question to ask | Why it matters | What to watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Who is the C-NRPP certified mitigation professional on this job? | Health Canada recommends one; Lungs Matter and the Manitoba Hydro loan require one | A company name with no person you can look up |
| What test result is the quote based on? | The decision should rest on a long-term test of 91+ days | A quote built only on a short screening reading |
| How many suction points, and where? | Crawl spaces or multiple suction points raise the price | No suction point locations written down |
| Where do the pipe and fan run, and where does it discharge outside? | The route affects cost, noise and what you see in finished rooms | "We'll figure it out on the day" |
| Is the sump pit sealed, and is that in the price? | A sump pit has to be sealed for the system to do its job | Sump sealing listed as an extra, or not mentioned |
| Is there a gauge or manometer showing the system is running? | It's how you'll check the fan years from now | Nothing that tells you the fan is working |
| Who retests after installation, and when? | Health Canada says to retest after mitigation | No retest mentioned |
| What happens if the retest is still above 200? | Nobody knows the final number until the fan runs | A guaranteed number with no plan B |
| Are taxes included? | Two quotes can look different only because of tax | A total with no tax line |

That second-last row deserves a word. Be wary of any quote that promises a specific final reading before the system exists. What a good professional can tell you is how they'll test afterwards and what they'll do if the number is still high. Our article on [radon sump pit sealing](/blog/radon-sump-pit-sealing/) covers the row most often left off a cheap quote.

**Picture a 2015 Winnipeg build with a capped rough-in.** Manitoba adopted the radon rough-in into its building code on 1 April 2011, so a house built in 2015 should have one: typically a 100 mm pipe below the basement floor, sealed and labelled. The rough-in on its own does not actively reduce radon. A fan has to be added. When quotes come in for a house like this, ask each installer whether they plan to use the existing rough-in or cut a new suction point, and why. A 2013 study of Winnipeg Parade of Homes houses found rough-ins in all but one, but almost half were in inaccessible locations, so a rough-in doesn't guarantee a simple job. We have no published price for activating one; the professional who sees where your pipe sits will quote it.

## What Prairie installers advertise, and why quotes differ

Here's what's published. Winnipeg installers advertise systems from about $1,500 to $3,000; a Regina installer quotes $2,500 to $3,500 and says crawl spaces or multiple suction points cost more. Nationally, Health Canada put the typical cost at $2,000 to $3,000 in 2014, and Take Action on Radon put the average at about $3,000 in 2022. All of it is in Canadian dollars, before tax.

These are advertised ranges from individual businesses, not a price list for a city, and they aren't our quote. Nobody can price your house without seeing it. We found no published installer price for Saskatoon, so Saskatoon homeowners should treat the Winnipeg and Regina figures as exactly that: Winnipeg and Regina figures, useful as a rough reference.

Why do two quotes for one house differ? Usually for reasons you can see once you ask:

- **Suction points.** One point or several depends on what's under the slab, which the installer should explain.
- **Crawl spaces.** The Regina installer above prices them higher, and a crawl space usually changes the design.
- **Pipe route.** Running pipe through a finished basement is a different job from running it through a garage or up an outside wall.
- **Season.** One Winnipeg installer advertises 20% off during its off-season, 30 November to 20 May.
- **What's included.** Sump sealing, the gauge and the retest are the lines that go missing.

Ask about visit fees too. One Winnipeg firm offers a free 15-minute screening test; another charges $50 for an in-person consultation. Neither is wrong, but you should know before someone comes to the door.

Then ask about running costs. Health Canada's 2014 national figures put fan electricity at about $50 to $75 a year and a replacement fan at $200 to $300. Those are national estimates, not Prairie quotes, but they're a fair yardstick for asking each installer what their fan will cost to run and replace. Our [radon mitigation cost page](/radon-mitigation-cost/) has the full breakdown.

**Picture a 1962 bungalow in St. Vital.** There's a finished basement bedroom where a teenager sleeps, and a long-term test has come back above 200. Two quotes arrive: one near the bottom of the Winnipeg advertised range, one near the top. On paper the cheap one wins. Line by line, the cheap one lists a single suction point and says nothing about the sump or a retest, while the dearer one includes sump sealing, a manometer and a retest. The gap may not be padding. The fair move is to ask the first installer, in writing, whether those items are in or out, then compare like with like.

## Money to line up before you sign the radon mitigation quote

Order matters here, because one of the main programs won't pay for work that's already done.

**Lungs Matter (Canadian Lung Association).** Up to $1,500 for low- or moderate-income households, meaning income below the provincial median. You need a C-NRPP test of 90 days or more showing a result above 200, and a quote from a C-NRPP professional. It doesn't cover work already completed, and the association describes the funding as limited, so apply before you book the install. In Manitoba it's run by the Manitoba Lung Association, which calls it the only radon mitigation grant program offered in the province. A lung cancer diagnosis in the household also qualifies. Details are in our [Manitoba radon grants guide](/blog/radon-mitigation-grants-manitoba/).

**Manitoba Hydro Home Energy Efficiency Loan.** Covers radon mitigation up to $5,000 over 5 years at 6.2% (O.A.C.), repaid on your energy bill. There's no income test, but the contractor must be C-NRPP certified.

**Saskatchewan.** Lung Saskatchewan has offered reimbursement in the past; check its site for the current status. On the provincial Home Renovation Tax Credit, Lung Saskatchewan says radon mitigation counts as an eligible expense; confirm with CRA before you count on it.

**Federal.** There is currently no federal radon tax credit that we could find.

The practical upshot: if you might qualify for Lungs Matter, get the long-term test and the certified quote, apply, and only then sign. A signed contract and a finished install can close that door.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many radon mitigation quotes should I get?

Two is usually enough to spot a missing line item, and three helps if the first two are far apart. Make sure each one comes from a C-NRPP certified mitigation professional you've checked in the directory. Compare them against the same long-term test result so you're comparing like with like.

### Can I get a quote with only a library monitor reading or a short-term test?

You can ask, but Health Canada bases the decision on a long-term test of at least 91 days, and digital monitors are screening tools. Lungs Matter also asks for a test of 90 days or more above 200. Doing the long-term test first keeps your funding options open.

### Is the price in Saskatoon the same as in Winnipeg or Regina?

We found no published installer price for Saskatoon. Winnipeg installers advertise about $1,500 to $3,000, a Regina installer quotes $2,500 to $3,500, and Health Canada's 2014 national range was $2,000 to $3,000. A certified professional who sees your house will quote the real number.

### Should the quote include a retest after the system goes in?

Health Canada says to retest after mitigation, because that's the only way to know the system worked. A good quote says who does the retest, when, and what happens if the result is still above 200. If it's missing, ask for it in writing before you sign.
