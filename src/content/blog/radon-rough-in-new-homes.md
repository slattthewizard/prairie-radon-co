If your house went up in Manitoba after April 2011, there is probably a pipe under your basement floor that was put there for radon. It has a cap on it, it should have a label, and right now it is doing nothing at all.

That capped pipe is a radon rough-in. A lot of owners of newer homes in Winnipeg, Regina and Saskatoon assume it means the radon question is handled. It isn't. The rough-in is a head start on a fix, not the fix, and you still need a test to know whether you need to use it.

## What a radon rough-in is, and what it isn't

Radon is a radioactive gas that comes out of the soil and collects in basements. You can't see or smell it. Health Canada calls radon [the number one cause of lung cancer in non-smokers](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/environmental-workplace-health/reports-publications/radon-what-you-need-to-know.html) and estimates it causes more than 3,000 lung cancer deaths in Canada each year. For smokers it is the second leading cause. That's the health case, and it's enough; the rest of this is practical.

Radon is measured in Bq/m3, becquerels per cubic metre. Canada's guideline is 200 Bq/m3 as the average over a year in the part of the house you actually use. If you're comparing with American sources, 200 Bq/m3 is about 5.4 pCi/L.

The radon rough-in was added to the National Building Code in 2010. Under the 2020 code, sentence 9.13.4.2.(2) requires new homes to have a rough-in for subfloor depressurization. The City of Regina's summary puts it plainly: most commonly this is a 100 mm pipe below the basement floor, sealed and labelled.

The code also asks for a granular layer under the slab, a well-sealed poly membrane, every opening sealed (sump pit included) and a properly located rough-in pipe. [C-NRPP's page on new builds](https://c-nrpp.ca/new-homes/) walks through those pieces if you want the detail.

Here's the sentence that matters most, again from the City of Regina: "the depressurization rough-in does not actively reduce radon levels." A capped pipe doesn't move any air. To lower radon, a fan has to be connected to it and the pipe has to vent outside. Until that happens, your basement is sitting on the same soil gas as a house with no rough-in at all.

## Which Prairie homes have a radon rough-in pipe

The dates are clearer in Manitoba than in Saskatchewan.

- **Manitoba** adopted the 2010 National Building Code, rough-in included, on 1 April 2011. The 2020 codes came into force on 1 January 2024.
- **Saskatchewan** also follows the National Building Code, and the 2020 codes have applied there since 1 January 2024. We could not find the date Saskatchewan first brought in the 2010 rough-in, so if your Regina or Saskatoon house was built between 2010 and 2023, go and look for the pipe rather than assume it's there.

You may have read about the 2025 National Building Code adding a passive vertical radon stack. Neither Manitoba nor Saskatchewan has adopted the 2025 code as far as we could find, so a new Prairie home is not required to have a full stack. Health Canada does recommend that all new homes include a passive soil depressurization system, but that's a recommendation, not the code here.

Having a rough-in on paper and having a usable one are also different things. A 2013 study of Winnipeg Parade of Homes houses found rough-in pipes in all but one house, but "almost half are in inaccessible locations." A Manitoba radon action plan written by advocates said in March 2025 that the rough-in was still not being done correctly in all new builds.

So look for it. It's usually a capped pipe coming up through the basement slab with a label on it. If the basement has been finished since, it may be behind drywall or under a built-in, and you'll need to know that before anyone quotes.

## Test before you touch the pipe

This is the step people want to skip, and it's the one we'd push hardest. A rough-in tells you nothing about your radon level. Plenty of houses without one test low, and plenty of new houses with one test high.

How common are high readings here? In the 2024 Cross-Canada survey, 37.4% of the Winnipeg homes that were tested came in at or above 200 Bq/m3, and 46.6% of the Regina homes tested did. Saskatoon had the lowest numbers of the four Prairie cities in the survey, 17.8%, or roughly 1 in 6 tested homes. These are unweighted samples of homes that happened to be tested, not a census, but they're enough to say nobody should guess.

Health Canada's method for a real answer:

- A long-term test of at least 3 months, which means a minimum of 91 days during the heating season. Three to twelve months is better.
- Place it in the lowest level you spend time in, like a finished basement bedroom or rec room. A crawl space, furnace room or laundry room doesn't count.
- Put it in the breathing zone, at least 30 cm above the floor and 30 cm from walls, away from the sump, vents, windows and heat sources.

A long-term kit is cheap. HomeRadonTest.ca sells one for $70.00 before tax, and the Manitoba government puts kits at between $50 and $100.

Library monitors are a fair way to get a first look. Winnipeg Public Library lends digital monitors from every branch between 1 November and 30 April on a three-week loan, and Regina Public Library has 30 kits on three-week loans with no renewals. Saskatoon libraries have lent detectors too. A three-week reading is a screening result, though. It can tell you to run a proper test; it can't replace one.

If the long-term result is above 200 Bq/m3, Health Canada recommends fixing it within a year. It's a guideline, not a law, and no Manitoba or Saskatchewan rule makes you test or mitigate. If the result is below 200, Health Canada notes there's no level considered risk free, and that's your call to make with the number in hand.

## Activating a radon rough-in: what the job involves

Activation turns the capped pipe into an active sub-slab depressurization system. The fan pulls soil gas from the gravel under the slab before it can get into the house, and it reverses the pressure difference that draws radon in through the foundation.

In practice a certified mitigation professional will usually:

1. **Find and check the pipe.** Where it sits matters. A pipe in an open mechanical room is easy. One framed into a finished wall means opening the wall, or putting in a new suction point somewhere reachable.
2. **Check what's under the slab.** The rough-in only works as well as the gravel layer and poly around it. Whether it was built right is something only a site visit can tell you.
3. **Connect a fan and run the pipe outside.** Health Canada's guide says an indoor fan is usually vented sideways through the rim joist at ground level, and a fan in a garage or attic is typically vented up above the roof. The fan runs continuously.
4. **Seal openings.** A sump pit has to be sealed, along with other gaps in the slab the professional finds.
5. **Retest.** Health Canada says to retest after any mitigation. That result is the only proof the system is working.

Health Canada recommends a mitigation professional certified through C-NRPP, the Canadian National Radon Proficiency Program. Certification is voluntary, since no province regulates radon professionals, and measurement and mitigation are separate certifications. You can check any name in C-NRPP's online directory. The certification belongs to the person doing the work, so check the person.

Can you just put a fan on the pipe yourself? The trouble is that the financial help below requires a C-NRPP professional, and a fan on a badly placed pipe with an unsealed sump can leave you paying for electricity with little change in the number. There's some help with the bill:

- **Lungs Matter** (Canadian Lung Association, run by the Manitoba Lung Association in Manitoba) offers up to $1,500 to low- or moderate-income households with income below the provincial median. You need a 90-plus day C-NRPP test above 200 and a C-NRPP quote, the work can't already be done, and funding is limited.
- **Manitoba Hydro's Home Energy Efficiency Loan** covers radon mitigation up to $5,000 over 5 years at 6.2% (O.A.C.), paid on your energy bill, with no income test. The contractor must be C-NRPP certified.
- **Saskatchewan:** Lung Saskatchewan has offered reimbursement in the past; check its site. Lung Saskatchewan also says radon mitigation counts as an eligible expense under the Saskatchewan Home Renovation Tax Credit; confirm with CRA.

There's currently no federal radon tax credit that we could find.

## Three typical situations with a capped radon rough-in

These are composites, not customers. They show how the rough-in plays out in real houses.

**A 2015 Winnipeg build with a capped radon rough-in that was never activated.** The basement is finished, with a kids' bedroom and a rec room. The owners find the labelled cap in the utility room and figure they're covered. A library monitor borrowed in November reads high, so they leave a $70 long-term kit in the bedroom, 30 cm off the floor, through the winter. The 91-day result is over 200. Because the pipe is in an open room, activation looks straightforward, but they still get a C-NRPP professional to confirm the sump is sealed and to quote. Winnipeg installers advertise full systems from about $1,500 to $3,000, and the Manitoba Hydro loan could spread the cost over five years.

**A newer Regina house where the pipe got framed in.** The owners finished the basement themselves and built a closet over the capped pipe. That's the "inaccessible location" problem the Winnipeg study found in almost half of its houses. A long-term test comes back over 200. The professional's choice is to open the closet floor area and use the rough-in, or core a new suction point where the pipe can run cleanly to the rim joist. A Regina installer quotes $2,500 to $3,500 for a system, with more for multiple suction points, so the choice affects the price.

**A Saskatoon two-storey being bought, with a rough-in noted on the inspection.** The buyers want to know whether to make radon a condition of the offer. Saskatoon had the lowest numbers of the four surveyed Prairie cities, roughly 1 in 6 tested homes, but that says nothing about this house. A 91-day test won't fit most closing dates, so conditions, holdbacks and disclosure are a conversation for their realtor or lawyer. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does a radon rough-in lower radon on its own?

No. The City of Regina's summary of the code says the depressurization rough-in "does not actively reduce radon levels." It's a capped pipe that makes a fan system easier to add later. Only a long-term test tells you whether you need to add one.

### How do I know if my house has a radon rough-in?

Look in the basement for a capped pipe coming up through the slab, usually labelled. Manitoba homes built under permits from 1 April 2011 should have one, and the 2020 codes have applied in both Manitoba and Saskatchewan since 1 January 2024. If the basement is finished, the pipe may be hidden behind a wall, so check the builder's documents too.

### Should I activate the rough-in before testing?

We'd test first. A long-term test of at least 91 days in the heating season costs far less than a system and tells you whether you need one. If the result is over 200 Bq/m3, Health Canada recommends fixing it within a year, which leaves time to get proper quotes.

### Who should activate a radon rough-in?

Health Canada recommends a C-NRPP certified mitigation professional, and you can check any name in C-NRPP's online directory. The Manitoba Hydro loan and Lungs Matter both require C-NRPP certification. After the fan goes in, retest to confirm the level came down.
