A Regina homeowner borrows a radon monitor from the library, sets it on a side table in the basement rec room, and a few days later it reads 480 Bq/m3. Is that a $3,000 repair, a fluke, or something in between?

Nobody can answer that from one short reading. What we can do is show you what the surveys say about radon levels in Canada, why Manitoba and Saskatchewan sit near the top of them, and what those numbers can and can't tell you about your own house. The short version: radon is common here, you can't see or smell it, a long-term test is cheap, and when a result comes back high, a mitigation system usually brings it down.

## First, what the numbers mean

Radon is measured in becquerels per cubic metre, written Bq/m3. If you're comparing with an American website, 37 Bq/m3 equals 1 pCi/L, so the Canadian line of 200 Bq/m3 works out to about 5.4 pCi/L.

That 200 Bq/m3 is Health Canada's guideline for the yearly average in any space someone uses four or more hours a day, like a finished basement, a basement bedroom or a basement suite. A crawl space, an unfinished basement, a furnace room or a laundry room doesn't count.

If your long-term average is above 200, Health Canada recommends fixing it within one year. If it's below 200, Health Canada is also clear that there is "no level that is considered risk free."

It's a guideline, not a law. No Manitoba or Saskatchewan rule requires a homeowner to test or to mitigate. The City of Regina puts it plainly: "there is no legislated maximum level or enforceable level."

Why bother, then? One sentence, from [Health Canada's radon guideline and factsheets](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/health-risks-safety/radiation/radon/government-canada-radon-guideline.html): radon is the number one cause of lung cancer in non-smokers and the second cause overall after smoking, with an estimated 16% of lung cancers and more than 3,000 lung cancer deaths in Canada each year linked to it. That's the case for testing, and we'll leave it there.

## Radon levels in Canada: the national picture

The most recent large study is the 2024 Cross-Canada Survey run by Evict Radon with Health Canada. Nationally, 17.8% of homes tested were at or above 200 Bq/m3. That's about 1 in 5.

The Prairies and the Northwest Territories, grouped together, came in at 20.0%. For Saskatchewan and Manitoba on their own, the report says "approximately 1 in 3" homes tested were high. That figure isn't weighted to match the real housing stock, so read it as a strong signal rather than a precise rate.

The report also says there are "no areas of Canada that are 'radon-free'." Low-radon areas still have high houses, which is why the map only tells you so much.

## Radon levels in Manitoba and Saskatchewan, city by city

The 2024 survey reported figures for four of the cities we cover. A few warnings before the table. These are convenience samples: homeowners who chose to test. The report says there wasn't enough information to weight the city figures, so they describe the homes that were tested, not every home in town.

| City | Share of homes tested at or above 200 Bq/m3 (2024 survey) | Mean of homes tested | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Regina | 46.6% | 182.7 Bq/m3 | Highest single reading in the city sample: 11,333 Bq/m3 |
| Winnipeg | 37.4% | 139.7 Bq/m3 | |
| Brandon | 36.1% | 147.0 Bq/m3 | Small sample |
| Saskatoon | 17.8% | 107.2 Bq/m3 | Lowest of the four; roughly 1 in 6 tested homes |
| Moose Jaw, Prince Albert | Not reported | Not reported | Not in the 2024 tables |

Source: [2024 Cross-Canada Survey of Radon Exposure in the Residential Buildings of Canadians](https://evictradon.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/2024-Cross-Canada-Radon-Survey-Report-V22.4-FINAL.pdf), Evict Radon with Health Canada.

A few things jump out.

Regina's mean is under 200, yet close to half the homes tested were over it. That's what averages do: a big pile of moderate readings and a handful of very high ones produce a middling mean. Your house isn't the average. The City of Regina, in a June 2024 advisory, cited research that found "50% of homes tested in Regina exceeded 200 Bq/m3."

Winnipeg and Brandon look alike. Of the Winnipeg homes in the survey, 37.4% were at or above the guideline, and Brandon's small sample came in at 36.1%. The report names Regina, Brandon and Winnipeg among the Canadian cities where a quarter to half of homes are high.

Saskatoon is lower, not zero. Of the Saskatoon homes in the survey, 17.8% were at or above 200. That's roughly 1 in 6 tested homes. It doesn't make Saskatoon a high-risk city, and it doesn't make any single Saskatoon basement safe either.

For Moose Jaw and Prince Albert, the only figures we have are from an older survey, covered next.

## The 2012 survey, and why it isn't a trend line

Health Canada ran an earlier cross-Canada survey, published in 2012. It found 23.7% of Manitoba homes tested above 200 Bq/m3 and 16.3% in Saskatchewan. It reported by health region rather than by city:

- Brandon health region: 44.3%
- Assiniboine: 34.5%
- Regina Qu'Appelle: 25.5%
- Five Hills (the Moose Jaw area): 12.7%
- Winnipeg: 12.1%
- Saskatoon: 7.9%
- Prince Albert Parkland: 7.4%

It's tempting to set these beside the 2024 numbers and conclude radon is rising. Don't. The two surveys sampled different homes in different ways, over different areas, and the 2024 city figures aren't weighted. A gap between the two tells you more about who tested and how the results were grouped than about the ground.

The useful takeaway from both surveys is the same: across Manitoba and Saskatchewan, a large share of homes that get tested come back above the guideline.

## Why your neighbour's result isn't yours

Picture a 1962 bungalow in St. Vital with a basement bedroom, a sump pit in the utility room and a few hairline cracks in the floor. The neighbours tested last winter and came in low, so the owners figure they're fine.

They might be. They might not. Radon moves up from the soil through cracks, floor drains, sumps and gaps around pipes, and how much gets in depends on the ground right under the slab, the condition of the foundation, and how the house pulls air in winter. Two houses built the same year by the same builder can test very differently.

The only way to know is a long-term test. Health Canada's method:

- At least three months, with a minimum of 91 days during the heating season. Three to twelve months is ideal.
- In the lowest level someone spends time in, which for this bungalow is the basement bedroom.
- In the breathing zone, at least 30 cm above the floor and 30 cm from any wall, away from the sump, vents, windows and heat sources.

A long-term test kit is cheap. One online seller lists kits at $70.00 each before tax, and the Manitoba government says kits cost "between $50 and $100." Mail it to the lab when the test period ends and you get an average for the season. If the St. Vital result comes back above 200, Winnipeg installers advertise systems from about $1,500 to $3,000. That's their advertised range, not a quote for this house.

Now back to the Regina rec room and its 480 Bq/m3. Library monitors are a good thing. Winnipeg Public Library lends them from every branch between 1 November and 30 April on a three-week loan, Regina Public Library has 30 kits on three-week loans with no renewals, and Moose Jaw Public Library lends them free with a card from 1 October to 1 April. Saskatoon libraries have lent detectors too; check with your branch. Our guide to [borrowing a radon detector from the library](/blog/borrow-radon-detector-library/) has the details.

But a few days on a digital monitor is a screening reading. Radon swings with the weather, the furnace cycle and whether the ground is frozen. Health Canada treats short-term tests and digital monitors as screening tools and bases its guideline on the long-term average. A 480 reading is a strong reason to run a proper 91-day test straight away, not a diagnosis. Our comparison of [long-term and short-term radon tests](/blog/long-term-vs-short-term-radon-test/) explains the difference.

If the long-term test in that Regina rec room does confirm a level over 200, a Regina installer quotes $2,500 to $3,500 for a standard system, with crawl spaces or houses needing more than one suction point costing more.

## If your long-term result is over 200

The standard fix is sub-slab depressurization, sometimes called active soil depressurization. An installer cuts a hole in the basement slab, runs a pipe from under the floor, connects an in-line fan, and vents the soil gas outdoors. The fan keeps the air under the slab at slightly lower pressure than the basement, so radon gets pulled out before it seeps in. The sump pit has to be sealed as part of the job. Our [radon mitigation page](/radon-mitigation/) walks through a typical install.

Health Canada recommends a mitigation professional certified by C-NRPP, the Canadian National Radon Proficiency Program. Certification is voluntary, since no province regulates radon contractors, and measurement and mitigation are separate certifications. You can check any professional's name in C-NRPP's online directory. We're not a contractor and we hold no certification ourselves; we put you in touch with certified professionals who quote and do the work.

After the system is in, test again. Health Canada also recommends retesting after an energy retrofit, such as new windows or air sealing, and whenever a new floor becomes the lowest lived-in level.

What results can you expect? That depends on the house, the soil and the install. A certified professional can tell you what they'd aim for after seeing the basement. Nobody honest guarantees a number over the phone.

Cost, in the only terms we can back up:

- Winnipeg installers advertise systems from about $1,500 to $3,000; a Regina installer quotes $2,500 to $3,500.
- Health Canada's national figure (2014) is $2,000 to $3,000.
- No Saskatoon, Brandon, Moose Jaw or Prince Albert installer we found publishes a price.

Take a Saskatoon two-storey that goes on the market, and the buyer writes a radon test into the offer. There's no local advertised price to lean on, so the parties end up working from Health Canada's national $2,000 to $3,000 and the Regina quote, then getting a real quote from a certified mitigation professional. A proper long-term test can also run longer than a typical closing date. How a condition, a holdback or a disclosure should be worded is a question for your realtor or lawyer, not for us. Saskatchewan doesn't require a property condition disclosure statement, but sellers must disclose known latent defects. Our [radon mitigation cost guide](/blog/radon-mitigation-cost-manitoba-saskatchewan/) covers what pushes a quote up or down.

There is some help with the bill. The Canadian Lung Association's Lungs Matter program offers up to $1,500 for low- and moderate-income households, run in Manitoba by the Manitoba Lung Association. It needs a 90-day-plus C-NRPP test above 200 and a C-NRPP quote, doesn't cover work already done, and funding is limited. Manitoba Hydro's Home Energy Efficiency Loan covers radon mitigation up to $5,000 over five years at 6.2% (O.A.C.), paid on your energy bill, with no income test, as long as the contractor is C-NRPP certified. In Saskatchewan, Lung Saskatchewan has offered reimbursement in the past, so check its site. Lung Saskatchewan also says radon mitigation counts as an eligible expense for the provincial Home Renovation Tax Credit; confirm that with CRA before you count on it. There is currently no federal radon tax credit that we could find.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What percentage of homes in Canada have high radon?

In the 2024 Cross-Canada Survey, 17.8% of homes tested nationally were at or above 200 Bq/m3, about 1 in 5. For Manitoba and Saskatchewan the report says approximately 1 in 3 tested homes. Those are unweighted figures from homeowners who chose to test.

### Is Saskatoon low enough that I can skip testing?

Of the Saskatoon homes in the 2024 survey, 17.8% were at or above the guideline, roughly 1 in 6 tested homes. That's the lowest of the four Prairie cities in the report, but it still means plenty of high houses. A long-term test is the only way to know where yours sits.

### Is 200 Bq/m3 a legal limit in Manitoba or Saskatchewan?

No. It's Health Canada's guideline for the yearly average in a lived-in space, and no Manitoba or Saskatchewan law requires homeowners to test or mitigate. Health Canada recommends fixing a home above 200 within one year.

### Can a library radon monitor replace a long-term test?

No. A library monitor gives you a screening reading over a few weeks at most. Health Canada's guideline is based on a test of at least 91 days in the heating season, so use the monitor to decide how urgently to start a long-term test, not to decide whether you need a mitigation system.
