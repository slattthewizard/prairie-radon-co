It's February, you're hauling laundry past the white pipe in the basement, and you notice the liquid in the little U-tube on the side of it is sitting level. Last fall it wasn't. Is the radon fan dead, is the gauge broken, and is your family breathing whatever the system was put in to remove?

The radon manometer is the cheapest, simplest piece of your mitigation system, and it's the one most people never look at after the installer leaves. It takes about five seconds to read. This post covers what a normal reading looks like, what a changed one can mean, and the one big thing the gauge can't tell you at all.

## What a radon manometer actually measures

Most sub-slab depressurization systems in Winnipeg, Regina and Saskatoon have a U-tube manometer fixed to the vent pipe, usually somewhere you can see it from the basement floor. It's a clear tube bent into a U, part-filled with coloured liquid, with one end connected into the pipe and the other open to the room.

When the fan runs, it pulls air out of the pipe. That makes the pressure inside the pipe slightly lower than the pressure in your basement, and the liquid gets pushed up on one side and down on the other. The gap between the two levels is the reading. If the fan stops pulling, the pressure evens out and the liquid settles level.

That's all it does. It tells you the fan is creating suction in the pipe. Health Canada's [Radon Reduction Guide for Canadians](https://www.canada.ca/content/dam/hc-sc/migration/hc-sc/ewh-semt/alt_formats/pdf/pubs/radiation/radon_canadians-canadiens/radon_canadians-canadien-eng.pdf) puts it simply: check the U-tube manometer from time to time, and a difference in level between the two sides shows the system is working.

Here's the catch, and it matters. A manometer measures pressure, not radon. An uneven reading tells you the fan is pulling. It doesn't tell you your radon level is below 200 Bq/m3 (becquerels per cubic metre, Health Canada's guideline, about 5.4 pCi/L in US units). Only a radon test tells you that.

Why test at all? Health Canada says radon is the number one cause of lung cancer in non-smokers and the second leading cause overall, linked to more than 3,000 lung cancer deaths in Canada each year. That's the whole reason these systems exist, and it's the reason a working fan on its own isn't proof of anything.

## How to read the radon manometer: normal, level and jumpy

There's no single "right" number for every house. The reading depends on the fan, the length and routing of the pipe, and how easily air moves through the gravel or soil under your slab. Two houses on the same street can show very different readings and both be working fine.

So the useful question is not "is my number good?" It's "has my number changed?"

Some installers mark the starting level on the tube or the pipe label when they commission the system. If yours didn't, make your own baseline: once your post-install test has come back below the guideline, put a small piece of tape at each liquid level and write the date on it. Now you have something to compare against.

Some drift over the year is normal. Frozen ground, wet spring soil and a dry late summer can all change how the air moves under the slab, so the liquid may creep a little in either direction between seasons. A small shift isn't a fault. A big one, or a sudden one, is worth a look.

Don't switch the fan off to "zero" the gauge or check it. Health Canada's 2014 guide says the fan should never be turned off and has to run continuously to work.

## What changes in the U-tube can mean

This is where it helps to think like the person who'll diagnose it. A changed reading is a clue, not a verdict, and the same symptom can have more than one cause depending on where things sit along the pipe.

| What you see | What it can mean | What usually follows |
|---|---|---|
| Liquid uneven, close to your taped baseline | The fan is pulling as it was at commissioning | Nothing, apart from the retest schedule below |
| Liquid level, fan silent | Fan has stopped: tripped breaker, unplugged, or failed | Check the power; if that's fine, the fan likely needs replacing. Health Canada's 2014 national estimate for a fan replacement is $200 to $300 |
| Liquid level, fan still humming | Blocked or iced exhaust, a disconnected pipe joint, or a gauge fault | A certified mitigation professional should trace it before anyone swaps parts |
| Reading well above the baseline | Possibly a restriction on the suction side, such as water or ice sitting in a low spot, depending on where the restriction is relative to the gauge | Have it inspected; don't drill or cut the pipe yourself |
| Reading drifting down slowly over months | A tiring fan, a small leak, or partial blockage | Worth a professional look and a fresh long-term test |
| Liquid missing, tube cracked or knocked off | The gauge can't tell you anything | Replace the gauge, then confirm the fan is pulling |

A few notes on that table. The fan replacement figure is Health Canada's 2014 national estimate, not a current Prairie price, and it doesn't include any other work the professional finds. Health Canada's same guide says a fan lasts five to ten or more years, so on an older system a level reading with a silent fan is often just the fan reaching the end of its life.

If the fan is humming but the gauge says it isn't pulling, resist the urge to replace the fan first. That's how people spend money on a new fan and still have a blocked exhaust.

## When the manometer looks fine but radon is still high

This one surprises people. The fan pulls, the liquid is uneven, and a long-term test still comes back over 200 Bq/m3.

It happens because suction in the pipe doesn't guarantee suction across the whole slab. An unsealed sump, a floor drain with no trap, a second slab area the suction point doesn't reach, or a crawl space that was never included can all leave radon a way in. Health Canada notes that sealing cracks and gaps helps a system work better, and that a sump can be capped and sealed so it still drains while acting as a suction point.

## Your radon manometer schedule: what to check and when to retest

The gauge is a between-tests check. The tests are what actually tell you where you stand. Health Canada's current advice after an install is a short-term test by a certified professional once the system is turned on, then a long-term test during the first heating season in the same spot as your original test, then a retest every five years.

A simple routine for a Prairie house:

- Glance at the manometer when you change the furnace filter, and again after any hard cold snap.
- Listen to the fan now and then. A new rattle, grinding or silence is worth noting alongside the gauge reading.
- Keep your taped baseline and the date of your last long-term test somewhere you'll find them.
- Retest after energy retrofits (new windows, air sealing, a new furnace), since Health Canada lists these as reasons to retest.
- Retest if a new floor becomes the lowest lived-in level, such as a basement you've just finished.

On running costs, Health Canada's 2014 guide puts fan electricity at about $50 to $75 a year nationally, depending on fan size and energy rates. It's a small bill, and it's the reason some people are tempted to unplug the fan. Don't. The system does nothing when it's off.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What should my radon manometer read?

There isn't one correct reading for every house. It depends on your fan, the pipe run and the soil under your slab. What matters is that the liquid is uneven and stays close to the level it showed after a successful post-install test.

### My radon manometer is level. Is my house unsafe?

A level reading means the fan probably isn't pulling, so the system may not be doing its job. It doesn't tell you your radon level either way. Check the fan's power, then have a certified professional look at it and run a new test.

### Does a good manometer reading mean my radon is below 200 Bq/m3?

No. The manometer measures pressure in the pipe, not radon in your air. Health Canada recommends a long-term test in the first heating season after install and a retest every five years.

### Can I fix or adjust the manometer myself?

You can check that the fan has power and put tape at the liquid levels to track changes. Replacing the gauge, fan or pipe sections is best left to a C-NRPP certified mitigation professional. A poorly sealed repair can quietly undo what the system was put in to do.
