A Regina installer quotes $2,500 to $3,500 for a radon mitigation system, and more for crawl spaces or homes that need more than one suction point. So when a long-term test comes back high, the next question is nearly always the same one. Who helps pay for this?

If you've been searching for a radon rebate in Saskatchewan, the honest answer for late September 2026 is: less than you'd hope, but not nothing. Lung Saskatchewan's own reimbursement program is paused. The provincial Home Renovation Tax Credit is open, and Lung Saskatchewan says radon mitigation counts toward it. A national grant through the Canadian Lung Association helps some lower-income households. And there is currently no federal radon tax credit that we could find.

Below is what each one is actually worth on a Prairie job, and the order you need to do things in so you don't rule yourself out.

## Is there a radon rebate in Saskatchewan right now?

Not in the cash-back sense. Lung Saskatchewan has offered reimbursement in the past through its Caring Breaths program, but that program is paused. Its notice says Caring Breaths will return in 2026 for people who need radon mitigation and can show financial need. It hasn't published the new amounts or an opening date, so we won't guess at either. Check [Lung Saskatchewan's radon page](https://www.lungsask.ca/education/lung-health/radon) before you book anything.

What a Saskatchewan homeowner can use today comes down to two things:

- The Saskatchewan Home Renovation Tax Credit, claimed on your provincial income tax.
- The Canadian Lung Association's Lungs Matter grant, for low- and moderate-income households.

Is it worth the paperwork? Health Canada says radon is the number one cause of lung cancer in non-smokers and estimates it's behind more than 3,000 lung cancer deaths in Canada each year. It's the second leading cause for smokers. That's the case for testing, stated once. Nobody can tell you whether your house is high without a test, and that includes us.

The Canadian guideline is 200 Bq/m3, which means 200 becquerels per cubic metre (about 5.4 pCi/L, if you're comparing with US sources). It applies to the average over a year in the part of the house you actually use, like a finished basement or a basement bedroom. It's a guideline, not a law. No Saskatchewan rule makes a homeowner test or fix anything.

## How the Saskatchewan Home Renovation Tax Credit works for radon mitigation

The [Government of Saskatchewan](https://www.saskatchewan.ca/residents/taxes-and-investments/tax-credits/home-renovation-tax-credit) describes it as a 10.5 per cent credit on up to $4,000 of eligible home renovation expenses, saving up to $420 a year in provincial income tax. Seniors can save up to $525, based on up to $5,000 of expenses.

The conditions that matter for radon:

- The home has to be your principal residence in Saskatchewan.
- The expense has to be incurred on or after October 1, 2024.
- Only qualified expenses above a $1,000 base amount count.

Now the catch. The government's own list of eligible work doesn't name radon. Lung Saskatchewan says radon mitigation counts as an eligible expense; confirm with CRA before you rely on it. We'd rather say that plainly now than have you find out at tax time.

### What that's worth on a real job

Because of the $1,000 base, a radon system on its own usually won't reach the $420 ceiling. Here's the arithmetic, reading the base the way the government page describes it (only the amount above $1,000 counts). It's an illustration, not tax advice, so run your own numbers past a tax preparer.

A $2,500 job, the bottom of that Regina installer's range, leaves $1,500 above the base. At 10.5 per cent that's $157.50. A $3,500 job leaves $2,500 above the base, or $262.50. Getting the full $420 takes $5,000 of eligible spending in the year, which a radon system alone rarely hits. If you're doing other renovation work the same year, ask how it combines.

That's real money, but it's a partial offset of a few hundred dollars. Anyone telling you the province covers your radon system is overselling it.

## Lungs Matter: the national radon mitigation grant Saskatchewan homeowners can apply for

The Canadian Lung Association's Lungs Matter program offers up to $1,500 towards home radon mitigation. It's aimed at low- or moderate-income households, and the rules are specific:

- Household income below the provincial median, shown on a CRA Notice of Assessment.
- A long-term test result, at least 90 days, from a C-NRPP certified radon test kit, showing more than 200 Bq/m3.
- A quote from a C-NRPP certified radon mitigation professional.
- The work can't already be done. Apply first, install second.

Funding is described as limited, so there's no promise of approval. The Manitoba version of the program says homeowner digital monitors don't qualify as the test, and the national one asks for a C-NRPP certified kit. Plan on a lab kit, not a library monitor.

Can you take a Lungs Matter payment and also claim the tax credit on the same job? We didn't find a rule that says either way. Ask the Lung Association, and ask a tax preparer how a grant affects what you can claim.

## What a radon system costs before any help

Almost every Prairie house with a basement gets the same basic fix, called sub-slab depressurization. A pipe goes through the basement slab, a fan pulls soil gas from under the floor, and the pipe discharges it outside. Any sump pit has to be sealed. Then you retest to confirm it worked. Our [radon mitigation page](/radon-mitigation/) goes through the install in more detail.

On price, Winnipeg installers advertise systems from about $1,500 to $3,000; a Regina installer quotes $2,500 to $3,500. We found no published mitigation price for Saskatoon, Moose Jaw or Prince Albert, so if you live there, treat the Regina figure as a Regina figure and nothing more. Health Canada's national range (from 2014) was $2,000 to $3,000. Your own price depends on the slab, the sub-slab fill, where the pipe can run and whether there's a crawl space, and only a site visit settles that. The full breakdown is in our [radon mitigation cost guide](/blog/radon-mitigation-cost-manitoba-saskatchewan/).

Running costs are small but not zero. Health Canada's 2014 national figures put fan electricity at about $50 to $75 a year and a replacement fan at $200 to $300.

## Three Saskatchewan homes, three different answers

These are composites to show how the rules play out. They aren't real clients.

A 1970s bungalow in Regina with a basement rec room where the kids spend their evenings. The owners borrow a digital monitor from the library for three weeks in January, and it reads 480 Bq/m3. That's a screening number, not a result, so they order a 90-day lab kit (HomeRadonTest.ca lists it at $70.00 before tax) and set it in the rec room, 30 cm off the floor and away from the walls and the sump. It comes back over 200. Say the C-NRPP professional's quote lands at $3,000, inside the range the Regina installer advertises. Their income is above the provincial median, so Lungs Matter is out. The tax credit, if CRA accepts it, is worth $210 on the $2,000 above the base.

A two-storey in Saskatoon with a basement suite, owned by a young family whose income sits below the provincial median. In the 2024 Cross-Canada survey, roughly 1 in 6 of the Saskatoon homes that were tested came in at or above 200, so a high result here isn't a given. Theirs is high on a 90-day C-NRPP kit. The order matters for them: they get a C-NRPP quote and apply to Lungs Matter before booking the install, because the grant won't pay for work already done. No Saskatoon installer publishes a price, so the only local reference they have is the Regina range, and the quote is what counts.

A house in Moose Jaw that had a system put in during the summer of 2024, and now the fan on the side of the house has started rattling. The original install was before October 1, 2024, so it doesn't count toward the tax credit. A replacement fan, which Health Canada's 2014 national figures put at $200 to $300, falls well under the $1,000 base on its own. No program pays for this one. It's still worth doing, and worth a retest afterward.

## Getting the order right so you don't lose the money

Most people who miss out on help do it by doing things in the wrong order. This sequence keeps every door open:

1. **Test long-term.** Health Canada wants at least three months, with a minimum of 91 days in the heating season, in the lowest level you live in. Put the detector in the breathing zone, at least 30 cm above the floor and 30 cm from walls, away from sumps, vents, windows and heat. Library monitors (Regina lends them for three weeks; Moose Jaw from October 1 to April 1; Saskatoon libraries have lent them too) are for screening only. Our guide to [long-term vs short-term radon tests](/blog/long-term-vs-short-term-radon-test/) covers the difference.
2. **Read the result calmly.** Above 200 Bq/m3, Health Canada recommends fixing it within a year. Below 200, it says no level is considered risk free, but the choice is yours.
3. **Check Lungs Matter before signing anything,** if your income might be below the provincial median.
4. **Get a quote from a C-NRPP certified mitigation professional.** C-NRPP is a voluntary program run by CARST with Health Canada oversight, and you can check any professional in its online directory. Both the grant and most sensible hiring decisions depend on it. More in [what C-NRPP certification means](/blog/what-is-c-nrpp-certification/).
5. **Keep the invoice** with the date and the address of your principal residence, and confirm the tax credit claim with CRA.
6. **Retest after the install.** It's the only way to know the system did its job.

One thing that isn't on the list: opening windows or running a basement fan. Neither is a fix, and neither will get you a result that qualifies for anything.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does Lung Saskatchewan still offer a radon rebate?

Not right now. Lung Saskatchewan has offered reimbursement in the past through Caring Breaths, but the program is paused, with a notice saying it will return in 2026. No current amount has been published, so check its site before counting on it.

### Can I claim radon mitigation on the Saskatchewan Home Renovation Tax Credit?

Lung Saskatchewan says radon mitigation counts as an eligible expense; confirm with CRA, because the government's list doesn't name radon. The expense has to be on or after October 1, 2024, at your principal residence, and only the amount above the $1,000 base counts.

### Is there a federal radon tax credit or grant?

There is currently no federal radon tax credit that we could find. The closest national help is the Canadian Lung Association's Lungs Matter grant of up to $1,500, which is limited to low- and moderate-income households and needs a C-NRPP test and quote.

### Does a library radon monitor reading qualify me for any help?

No. Library monitors are screening tools, and a three-week loan is far short of the 91-day minimum Health Canada sets. The Lungs Matter grant asks for a long-term result of at least 90 days from a C-NRPP certified test kit, so use the library reading as a reason to test properly.
