## 1. Guideline and health

### 1.1 Canadian guideline

- "The Canadian guideline for radon is 200 becquerels per cubic metre (Bq/m³)." [S1]
- The guideline covers "the average annual radon level" in "the normal occupancy area of a building". [S1]
- "Normal occupancy area" means "any part of the building where a person is likely to spend more than 4 hours per day". This includes a finished basement or a basement apartment. It excludes a crawl space, an unfinished basement, a cold room, a storage area, a furnace room and a laundry room. [S1]
- "While the health risk from radon exposure below the Canadian guideline is small there is no level that is considered risk free. It is the choice of each homeowner to decide what level of radon exposure they are willing to accept." [S1]
- Scope: the guideline applies to homes and also to "public buildings and workplaces with an occupancy of more than 4 hours per day". [S1]
- History: on 9 June 2007 the federal guideline changed to 200 Bq/m³. It had been 800 Bq/m³. [S51, CBC News, 24 July 2007]. The Health Canada guideline page itself does not give the adoption date. [S1]
- The Government of Saskatchewan says "there is no safe level of radon". [S17]

### 1.2 Mitigation timeline (current)

- "If you've tested your home, and the radon level is above the Canadian guideline of 200 Bq/m³, Health Canada recommends that you take corrective action to lower the level within 1 year. The higher the radon concentrations, the sooner action should be taken to reduce levels to as low as practically possible." [S1]
- The measurement guide says the same: "Individuals mitigate within 1 year for radon levels higher than 200 Bq/m3." [S7]
- C-NRPP gives it as: "Above 200 Bq/m3 – Mitigate the building within one year" and "0 - 200 Bq/m3 – Retest again within 5 years or after you make major renovations". [S40]
- The goal is "as much as is practicable", which is consistent with "the ALARA (as low as reasonably achievable) principle". "In most situations, you can readily achieve a final level less than 200 Bq/m³." [S1]
- **OUTDATED, DO NOT PUBLISH as current advice:** the May 2014 Radon Reduction Guide used two tiers: "200 - 600 Bq/m3 fix your home within 2 years" and "Above 600 Bq/m3 fix your home within 1 year". [S4] The City of Regina's June 2024 advisory still reproduces an older Health Canada table. [S45] Current Health Canada pages give a single "within 1 year" timeline. [S1][S7]

### 1.3 Lung cancer statistics, exactly as Health Canada states them

- "Radon exposure is the number one cause of lung cancer in non-smokers. 16% of lung cancers are estimated to be from radon exposure, resulting more than 3,000 lung cancer deaths in Canada each year." [S2, Health Canada factsheet, 2025]
- "People who smoke and are exposed to radon have an even higher risk of lung cancer." [S2]
- "Radon is the #1 cause of lung cancer in non-smokers." [S9]
- "Almost 90% of lung cancer deaths are caused by smoking. Radon exposure is linked to approximately 16% of lung cancer deaths in Canada and is the second leading cause of lung cancer for smokers." [S3, Health Canada, 2014]
- Lifetime lung cancer risk, as a Health Canada graphic shows it: "High radon only = 1 in 20" and "Smokers + high radon = 1 in 3". [S4, Health Canada Radon Reduction Guide, May 2014]
- Take Action on Radon puts it as: "A person who lives in an exposure of 800 Bq/m 3 has a 1 in 20 risk of developing lung cancer", "1 in 10 people who smoke and have NO exposure to high radon will die of lung cancer" and "1 in 3 smokers exposed to high radon will die of lung cancer". [S35]
- The Government of Manitoba reproduces Health Canada's 2006 risk table ("Report of the Radon Working Group on a New Radon Guideline for Canada"). Lifetime probability of lung cancer at 70 years' exposure: smoker at base level 12%, at 200 Bq/m3 17%, at 800 Bq/m3 30%; non-smoker at base level 1%, at 200 Bq/m3 2%, at 800 Bq/m3 5%. [S16]
- "The health risk from radon is long-term not immediate. The longer you are exposed to high levels of radon, the greater your risk." [S2]
- Manitoba: "Each year, over 600 deaths from lung cancer occur in the province." [S16]
- Saskatchewan: Lung Saskatchewan's Effie Kosmas "said one in four cancer deaths in Saskatchewan are lung cancer related" (CBC, 12 Nov 2024). [S49]
- Evict Radon (University of Calgary-led research): "1 in 6 Canadian lung cancers are caused by radon exposure." [S60]
- A University of Calgary study "estimates that more than 10 million Canadians are being exposed to high levels of radon" (CBC, 12 Nov 2024). [S49]
- **DO NOT PUBLISH:** CBC (2022) quotes the Saskatchewan Health Authority as "about 4,000 lung-cancer deaths each year in Canada". [S50] This conflicts with Health Canada's "more than 3,000". **USE Health Canada's figure.**

### 1.4 Explaining Bq/m³ and pCi/L

- Health Canada: "Note: 37 Bq/m3 is equal to 1 pCi/L". [S7]
- Radon "is measured in becquerels per cubic meter (Bq/m3), which is a measure of radioactivity". [S16]
- Derived from S7, arithmetic only: 200 Bq/m³ ÷ 37 = 5.405… pCi/L. To convert pCi/L to Bq/m³, multiply by 37.
- Plain-English line that is safe to use: Canadian labs report in Bq/m³. US kits and apps may show pCi/L, and 1 pCi/L = 37 Bq/m³ [S7].
- The US EPA action level (4 pCi/L) was **not fetched today**. It is on the NOT FOUND list.

## 3. Testing

### 3.1 Health Canada's recommended method

- "It is important that you conduct a long-term test for a minimum of 3 months." [S8]
- "you need to do a long term test, for 3 months, ideally during the fall or winter time." [S2]
- "Testing should be conducted so that a minimum of 91 days of the test is during the heating season." [S7]
- "To accurately determine the average annual radon level in a home, Health Canada recommends conducting radon tests over a period of 3 to 12 months." [S7]
- Location: "All measurements should be made in a normal occupancy area of the lowest lived-in level of the home", meaning a space "in which an individual spends more than 4 hours per day". [S7]
- Placement: "in the typical breathing zone (at least 30 cm, or 1 ft, above the floor)" and "at least 30 cm from walls or ceilings". Keep it away from heat sources, direct sunlight, vents, doors and windows, sump holes, crawl spaces and exposed foundation cracks. [S7]
- Manitoba Lung Association gives different placement numbers: "1 metre (3 feet) from any exterior doors or windows, 0.5 metre (2 feet) from exterior walls, 1 metre (3 feet) off the floor". [S26] **USE Health Canada's [S7] numbers** if the site gives placement distances.
- Manitoba government: test "ideally during the winter (ex: October to April)". [S16]
- A test shorter than 91 days: "Health Canada recommends that measurements occur over a time period no less than 91 days... A new test is recommended." [S7]
- Electronic monitors: "make sure you choose one that has passed the C-NRPP performance testing for conducting long-term radon measurements in homes." [S8]
- When to retest (Health Canada) [S7]:
  - after energy retrofits
  - if a floor that was not tested becomes the lowest lived-in level
  - "After any radon mitigation system is installed"
  - in a new home, "2 years after year of construction", as well as in the first heating season after occupancy
  - homes with active systems "should be retested every 5 years"
- Health Canada "recommends every home should be tested, no matter where it is located, the type of building, or if it already has a mitigation system." [S7]

### 3.2 Role of short-term tests

- "short-term measurements are not appropriate for informing mitigation decisions." [S7]
- "The 200 Bq/m3 guideline applies to your average annual radon level, not short-term measurements. The minimum test length is 91 days". [S7]
- Library monitors are screening tools. Manitoba Lung Association: Health Canada recommends "a long-term 3-month kit. The library lending program provides patrons with an affordable option to screen their homes". [S29]
- The Lungs Matter grant needs a "long-term 90 day C-NRPP... approved radon test kit. Homeowner-level digital monitors do not qualify." [S28]
- In a real-estate sale, C-NRPP says "A short-term radon screening could be conducted to provide some insight", with a long-term test after moving in. [S42]
- Short-term tests do have one role Health Canada endorses: a short-term test by a certified professional right after a mitigation system is switched on (section 4.4). [S5]

## 4. Mitigation

### 4.1 How sub-slab depressurization (active soil depressurization, ASD) works

- "A pipe installed through the foundation floor draws radon from beneath your home." "A fan attached to the pipe runs 24/7, pulling radon out and venting it safely outdoors." Installers adjust the system "whether you have a foundation slab, crawl space, or sump pit." [S5, Health Canada factsheet, Sept 2026]
- The system "also reverses the air pressure difference between the house and soil, reducing the amount of radon that is drawn into the home through the foundation. One, or sometimes multiple, suction points are inserted through the floor slab into the crushed rock or soil underneath". [S4]
- Venting: an indoor fan "is usually vented sideways through the rim joist at ground level". A fan in a garage or attic "is typically vented upwards above the roof". [S4]
- Cold-climate note (relevant to the Prairies): "a fan and pipe located outside the living space (garage or attic) will cool during the colder months of the year, leading to condensation and possibly ice, which can damage the fan". "Condensation problems can be reduced if the fan is placed indoors and the exhaust is discharged from a shorter pipe near ground level". [S4]
- Backdrafting: check that the system "does not cause backdrafting of combustion appliances such as a furnace, water heater, fireplace, or wood stove". [S4]
- "A system can usually be installed in less than a day". [S5] "Installation is straightforward and can generally be completed in a day." [S37]
- Why radon gets in: "The air pressure inside your home is usually lower than in the soil surrounding the foundation." Entry points include "cracks in foundation floor and walls, construction joints, gaps around service pipes, support posts, window casements, floor drains, sumps or cavities inside walls." [S9]

### 4.2 Reduction claims, as Health Canada states them (two versions exist)

- Current factsheet (published Sept 2026): "typically lowers radon levels by more than 90%" and ASD "can reduce radon levels by 90% or more". [S5]
- Health Canada pages "Reducing radon levels in your home" and "What you need to know" (2025): "in most homes will reduce the radon level by more than 80%" [S6]; "Radon levels can be reduced by more than 80% for about the same cost as other common home repairs such as replacing the furnace or air conditioner." [S2] The May 2014 guide uses the same 80% line. [S4]
- Government of Manitoba: sub-slab depressurization is "The most effective method – reductions of 90 per cent or greater". [S16]
- **USE:** "more than 80%" is the conservative wording found on the most Health Canada pages. "90% or more" may be used when attributed to Health Canada's Sept 2026 factsheet [S5].
- Other methods (Health Canada):
  - balanced ventilation (HRV/ERV) "generally reduce radon levels by only 20% to 50%" [S5]; the 2014 guide says an HRV "might reduce radon levels by 25 to 50 per cent" [S4]
  - passive soil depressurization (new homes) "can reduce radon levels by 40-90%" [S5]
  - "When large radon reductions (50 per cent or more) are desired, active soil depressurization is almost always the recommended approach." [S4]
- Contractor performance claims (these are each company's own claims, not Health Canada's):
  - Radon Manitoba: "Our average post-mitigation radon level is an incredible 11 Bq/m³" and a guarantee of "90 Bq/m³ or lower for a full five years". [S64]
  - Calgary Radon Detection and Ventilation Kings: "up to 95%". NOT PRAIRIE. [S67][S68]
  - **Do not publish** any of these as typical results.

### 4.3 Sump pits and sealing

- "Active sump-hole depressurization": "the sump can be capped and sealed so that it can continue to drain water and also serve as the location for a radon suction pipe. If a basement floor drain is connected to the sump, a mechanical trap seal device or water trap should be installed". [S4]
- "Open sumps – Sumps can be fitted with an airtight cover. If the sump also acts as a floor drain, add a special trap to the airtight cover". [S4]
- Sealing alone "is not a standalone technique for reducing radon levels". [S4]
- "Cracks, gaps around pipes and other potential radon entry points should be sealed to maximize the efficacy of a ventilation system." [S5]
- Manitoba government: caulking cracks and sealing around pipes and drains "helps prevent radon from getting into the home". "Installing specially designed traps in floor drains can also help". [S16]

### 4.4 After installation: testing and upkeep

- Health Canada, Sept 2026:
  - "Step 1: Short-term test. Once your radon reduction system is turned on, it's important that a certified professionals perform a short-term radon test to confirm it is reducing the radon level to below the Canadian Guideline."
  - "Step 2: Long-term test. During the first heating season after installation, do a long-term radon test in the same location as your original test."
  - "Step 3: Retest regularly... inspect and maintain it regularly and retest for radon every five years." [S5]
- 2014 guide wording: "an initial long-term measurement should be made within two years of the system activation and at five-year intervals afterwards." [S4] **USE the 2026 wording [S5].**
- "the fan should NEVER be turned off; it must run continuously for the system to work properly." [S4]
- Check the U-tube manometer "from time to time". A level difference between the two sides shows the system is working. [S4]

### 4.5 Fan lifespan and running cost

- "The lifespan of a fan can vary between five to ten or more years. Replacement cost ranges between $200 and $300." [S4, Health Canada, May 2014, national, not city-specific]
- "There is also a small operating cost for electricity for the fan, approximately $50 to $75 a year, depending on size of fan and energy rates." [S4, same]
- Both figures are from 2014. Present them as "Health Canada estimates", not as current Prairie prices.

## 6. Rules, codes and money

### 6.1 Is residential testing or mitigation legally required in MB or SK?

- **No law found in either province.** The City of Regina says the Health Canada levels "are guidelines only, as there is no legislated maximum level or enforceable level". "the NBC and the City of Regina do not require mandatory testing for radon gas or require the connection of mitigating systems". [S45]
- Regina FAQ: "Although the Government of Canada has provided recommended levels, there is no legislated limit and it is up to the property owner to assess what risk they are comfortable with." [S45]
- Manitoba: "In Manitoba child care facilities are not currently required to test for radon or mitigate". [S31] The same plan's schools section says schools "are not currently required to test". [S31]
- Tenants: the City of Regina "is not able to enforce radon testing/mitigation or act as a liaison between a tenant and an owner." [S45] Health Canada's action guide cites "good repair" sections of both tenancy acts: Saskatchewan's Residential Tenancies Act, 2006, s. 49(1) and Manitoba's Residential Tenancies Act, CCSM c R119, s. 59(1). [S14] Do not claim these give tenants a radon right; Health Canada lists them as policy levers.
- Saskatchewan Housing Corporation is testing its own social-housing units with Lung Saskatchewan over 10 years from March 2024. This is voluntary government action on its own stock, not a law. [S18]

### 6.2 Building code radon rules for new homes

- National Building Code: the radon rough-in "was added to Canada's National Building Code in 2010". [S15]
- What the NBC requires: "granular layer, well sealed poly-membrane layer, all openings sealed including any sump pit, and a properly located rough-in pipe." [S42]
- NBC 2020, as the City of Regina summarises it:
  - Sentence 9.13.4.2.(2) "requires that dwelling units and buildings with residential occupancy be provided with a rough-in system for subfloor depressurization"
  - "Most commonly, this is achieved with a 100 mm diameter pipe below the basement floor", sealed and labelled
  - "the depressurization rough-in does not actively reduce radon levels"; a fan must be added later [S45]
- **Manitoba:** "Manitoba adopted the NBC 2010 as the Manitoba Building Code on April 1, 2011." [S56] (The Made-in-Manitoba plan says "adopted by Manitoba in 2012" [S31]; **USE 1 April 2011** from S56, which is the primary study.) The 2020 national codes "came into force in Manitoba on January 1, 2024", enforced in Winnipeg through Building By-Law 4555/87. [S55]
- **Saskatchewan:** "The 2020 codes come into effect in Saskatchewan on January 1, 2024." [S20] Health Canada lists Saskatchewan and Manitoba as provinces that "follow the National Building Code". [S14] The date Saskatchewan first adopted the 2010 radon rough-in was not found.
- NBC 2025: "A requirement has been introduced to provide a passive vertical radon stack in dwelling units and home-type care occupancies where a floor assembly separates a conditioned space from the ground". [S57] Neither province has adopted NBC 2025 as far as we could find. **Do not say Prairie new homes need a full stack.**
- Health Canada "recommends that all new homes include a passive soil depressurization (PSD) system". [S5]
- Manitoba compliance concern (advocacy source): the rough-in is "still not being implemented correctly in all new builds as of March 2025". [S31] A Fall 2013 Winnipeg Parade of Homes study found: "Rough‐in pipes were installed in all but one house, unfortunately almost half are in inaccessible locations." [S56]
- Municipal: Regina enforces the NBC radon provisions at plan review; missing radon details "may be noted as a deficiency during plan review". [S45] No Winnipeg, Saskatoon, Brandon, Moose Jaw or Prince Albert radon bylaw beyond the building code was found.

### 6.3 Real-estate disclosure

- Health Canada: "In some Canadian provinces, property disclosure statements include a line for radon." [S14] The examples Health Canada gives are Nova Scotia, New Brunswick, Quebec and British Columbia. Manitoba and Saskatchewan are not listed. [S14] That list came from a WebFetch summary; recheck before quoting the names.
- Saskatchewan (PLEA, public legal education): "A property condition disclosure statement is not required for a sale". Sellers "must inform buyers about any defects known to the seller that would not be discovered by a reasonable inspection". [S58] The Association of Saskatchewan REALTORS® form we found (revised 2008) has no radon line. [S71]
- Manitoba: the Property Disclosure Statement is "Appendix A to Residential Form of Offer to Purchase", prescribed under The Real Estate Brokers Act. The seller uses it "to disclose information on the condition of the property, to the best of the seller's knowledge". [S59] Whether the form has a radon line was not verified; the PDF could not be retrieved.
- Health Canada says real estate licensees have professional duties that include "disclosing radon as a known latent defects to buyers". [S14]

### 6.4 Financial help (MB and SK)

**Manitoba**
- **Lungs Matter (Manitoba Lung Association):**
  - "a payment of up to $1,500 towards the cost of radon mitigation when completed by a radon mitigation professional"
  - who qualifies: Manitoba residents diagnosed with lung cancer, or in a "low to moderate-income household" ("Priority given to low-income households")
  - needs a "long-term 90 day C-NRPP... approved radon test kit"; applications are assessed monthly [S28]
  - "Lungs Matter is the only radon mitigation grant program offered in Manitoba." [S27]
- **Manitoba Hydro Home Energy Efficiency Loan:**
  - radon mitigation loan limit "$5,000", term "5 years"
  - "Annual interest rate is 6.2% (O.A.C) for the first five years"
  - "To be eligible for financing, radon mitigation must be performed through a C-NRPP certified contractor"
  - payments go on the energy bill with "No down payment", and the loan counts toward the "$12,500 limit per residence" [S43]
  - "There are no income thresholds for eligibility." [S44]
- Low uptake: "only 18 Manitoba households used of the funds in 2024" (Lungs Matter). Hydro financed "only an average of seven mitigation systems... per year over the last decade". [S31]

**National (open to MB and SK residents)**
- **Canadian Lung Association Lungs Matter:**
  - "You may be eligible for up to $1,500 towards home radon mitigation"
  - for "low- or moderate-income households"; needs a CRA Notice of Assessment showing household income "less than the provincial median", a "long-term test results (minimum 90 days) from a C-NRPP certified radon test kit" above 200 Bq/m3, and "a quote from a C-NRPP certified radon mitigation professional"
  - not available for mitigations "already completed"
  - "Limited funding is available." [S30]

**Saskatchewan**
- **Lung Saskatchewan Caring Breaths (radon mitigation reimbursement): PAUSED as of 26 Sept 2026.** The notice says "our Caring Breaths Programs will be returning soon in 2026" and that support will cover people "In need of radon mitigation who demonstrate financial need". "Application will be open soon." [S22]
  - Previous terms still on Lung Sask's radon page: "up to $500 in reimbursement" for a "single-detached primary residence" mitigated by a C-NRPP professional, and "up to $1,000" for people with a lung cancer diagnosis and their primary caregivers. [S21]
  - CBC (2022) reported "up to $1,000" generally. [S50]
  - **Do not publish any amount** until the programme reopens. Say "Lung Saskatchewan has offered reimbursement in the past; check its site".
- **Saskatchewan Home Renovation Tax Credit (2025 and later tax years):**
  - "save up to $420 annually in provincial income tax by claiming a 10.5 per cent tax credit on up to $4,000 of eligible home renovation expenses"
  - "Seniors may save up to $525 annually based on maximum eligible home renovation expenses of $5,000"
  - expenses must be "incurred on or after October 1, 2024", and only qualified expenses above a "$1,000 (base amount)" count
  - the home must be your principal residence in Saskatchewan [S19]
  - The government page does **not** list radon by name. [S19] Lung Sask and CARST say radon mitigation qualifies: "Saskatchewan homeowners may save up to $420... including radon mitigation". [S21][S38] Take Action on Radon: "Saskatchewan is the first province to have a tax credit which covers radon mitigation." [S33] The NWT scan notes "radon is not explicitly listed". [S44]
  - **Publish only as:** "Lung Saskatchewan says radon mitigation counts as an eligible expense; confirm with CRA."
  - **DO NOT PUBLISH:** the Manitoba plan's "10.5% tax credit on up to $20,000" [S31]. That conflicts with the government page.

**Federal**
- **No federal tax credit or grant for radon mitigation was found.** The Feb 2026 jurisdictional scan lists federal support only as awareness programmes ("Take Action on Radon program and the 100 Test Kit Challenge"). [S44] This is evidence of absence, not a statement from the federal government. Safe wording: "There is currently no federal radon tax credit that we could find."

**Nova Scotia (context only)**
- "Nova Scotia Radon Reduction Grant Program provides financial support to low-income Nova Scotia households to mitigate their homes (up to $2,500)" (as quoted in the Manitoba plan). [S31] It is not available in MB or SK. Not verified on a Nova Scotia government page.

## 7. C-NRPP

- C-NRPP is the Canadian National Radon Proficiency Program: "a Canadian radon certification program operated by the Canadian Association of Radon Scientists & Technologists (CARST) with oversight by Health Canada." [S34]
- "C-NRPP provides training and certification to radon professionals, so that you can hire a radon professional with confidence." [S39]
- **Voluntary:** "Health Canada recommends radon mitigation and measurement professionals certified through the Canadian National Radon Proficiency Program (C-NRPP). However, certification remains voluntary. While Canadian provinces have jurisdiction for regulating professions, none have acted on this for radon professionals." [S13]
- Health Canada "recommends that the contractor be certified as a radon mitigation professional from the Canadian National Radon Proficiency Program (C-NRPP)." [S6]
- The two professional types: "A C-NRPP Measurement Professional is trained to measure radon in residential and large buildings. A C-NRPP Mitigation Professional is trained to help with reducing high radon levels." [S40]
- New-home certification: "Controlling Radon in New Canadian Homes (CRNCH)". [S42]
- How a homeowner checks a professional:
  - "C-NRPP double check: Have you seen our logo on a company's website? Double-check their certification here before you hire them!" [S39]
  - Use the "online tool to find a certified radon professional near you for radon measurement or mitigation". [S39][S41]
  - C-NRPP phone: "1-800-269-4174" (as given by the Manitoba Lung Association). [S27]
- Certification is required for money: Lungs Matter (national and MB) needs C-NRPP tests and quotes [S28][S30], and the Manitoba Hydro loan needs a C-NRPP contractor [S43].
- C-NRPP also keeps "a list of approved electronic radon monitors". [S39]
- **Site positioning line (safe):** "We are an advertising service. We connect you with independent C-NRPP certified professionals. We are not certified ourselves and do not test or install." There is no source to cite for this line; it is a statement about ourselves.
